\subsection{Goals of the Experiments}
\label{sec:exp_goals}

Each experiment has one goal and one deliverable.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{E\arabic*}]
\item Evaluate the discovered nets on two levels and keep those that are sound
and accept every sequence above the noise threshold. Deliverable: the
admissible nets.
\item Remove forbidden behaviour from the admissible nets, encode them as state
machines and add the stop guard, without losing observed behaviour.
Deliverable: the repaired nets and a shortlist of at most three.
\item Simulate the shortlist under identical conditions, select the
control-flow model with the decision matrix, and test the encoding decision.
Deliverable: the control-flow model used in E4 and E5.
\item Quantify how much of the remaining deviation between simulated
and observed turnaround time is caused by the demand, the block
allocation, the queue at the block and the service time, and whether the
simulated turnaround time responds to load at the blocks as observed.
Deliverable: the final simulation configuration, the attribution of the
error to components and the decision whether E5 is admissible.
\item Show what the simulation of E4 can and cannot say about a load-aware
block allocation for deliveries: the direction of the effect on turnaround
time, the mechanism, who gains and who loses, and whether this holds under the
weaknesses found in E4. Deliverable: a conditional what-if statement with its
conditions of validity, not a recommendation for the terminal.
\end{enumerate}

E1 to E3 answer RQ1. E3 and E4 answer RQ2. E5 answers RQ3. Every selection
uses the training log only; the test log reports the results. The one exception
is the generalisation check of the shortlist (Section~\ref{sec:exp_rules}).

The experiments do not aim to rank discovery algorithms in general, to
optimise the allocation rule, or to show that the simulator is valid for
purposes other than the turnaround and peak measures defined in
Section~\ref{sec:exp_kpis}.

\subsection{Model Selection}
\label{sec:exp_selection_logic}

The control-flow model is selected in four steps with criteria fixed in
advance (Figure~\ref{fig:exp_flow}).
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{E1, gates.} A1: the net is sound. A2: it accepts every sequence
above the noise threshold. Nets that pass are admissible.
\item \textbf{E2, treatment.} Every admissible net is repaired, encoded as a
state machine and given the stop guard. A4: no observed behaviour is lost.
\item \textbf{E2, shortlist.} A3: the repaired nets not dominated in precision
and generalisation, at most three.
\item \textbf{E3, decision.} The shortlist is simulated and judged with a
decision matrix. Rule A selects the most precise net unless the simulation
rejects it; Rule B, selection by simulation, is reported as a sensitivity.
\end{enumerate}
Discovery metrics alone are not enough, because a simulator executes the
model: behaviour that the language permits but the log never shows can become
frequent in the generated log (Section~\ref{sec:bg_coverage}). The last step
therefore takes place in the simulator.

\section{Outcome Measures and Peak Definitions}
\label{sec:exp_kpis}
\begin{hints}
\item Primary KPI: turnaround time (TTT) per visit, Gate Out minus Gate
In. Reported as median and P90, overall and by flow type. Why P90: delay
peaks are a tail phenomenon.
\item TTT decomposition (approach, stops, transfer between stops, exit)
as diagnostic measures, with the formula. Note that gate events are
instantaneous, so the decomposition is complete.
\item Arrival peak: Gate-In hour with at least the training P90 of
arrivals (165 trucks/h). Delay peak: Gate-In hour with at least 20
visits and median TTT at least the training P90 (37 min). Both
thresholds are frozen from training and reused unchanged in every
experiment, including the policy scenarios, so that improvement is not
normalised away.
\item Attribution rule: hourly TTT is attributed to the hour of Gate In
(arrival cohort), not to the hour in which the delay occurs.
\item Disruption episodes (e.g.\ 23 March): defined by a rule fixed in
advance (delay peak without arrival peak, exit-time dominated). Reported
separately \emph{and} included in the all-visits result. Never removed
to improve the fit.
\item Throughput and backlog: visits completed per day, visits
unfinished at the end of the horizon, used as guardrails.
\end{hints}

\section{Simulation Runs, Replications and Uncertainty}
\label{sec:exp_runs}
\begin{hints}
\item Horizon definition: fixed number of visits from the first
validation Gate In (current) vs.\ fixed calendar window. State which one
and why. Initial work in progress at the start (empty terminal at 15:50)
and how the first hours are treated (warm-up exclusion or reported).
\item Unfinished visits at the end of the horizon: counted and reported,
never silently dropped from TTT statistics.
\item Replications: number per configuration (5 for E3/E4, 10 for E5),
seeds, and how the number was chosen (precision of the median and P90
across replications).
\item Paired comparison: the same seeds for baseline and scenario.
Note that equal seeds do not guarantee common random numbers in ProSiT.
\item Reporting: mean over replications with min--max, or a $t$-based
interval over replication means. Replications quantify stochastic
variation only, not uncertainty in the fitted parameters.
\item Event order in generated logs: reconstructed from start
timestamps. Firing sequences are exported for new runs.
\item Run hygiene: one new run directory per configuration, hashed
inputs, recorded versions (details in the appendix).
\end{hints}

\section{Domain Review}
\label{sec:exp_review}
\begin{hints}
\item Purpose: an independent check of data meaning, rules and

\item Instrument: structured questionnaire with five parts. A: data

\section{Control-Flow Discovery (E1)}
\label{sec:exp_discovery}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

E1 asks which discovery configuration produces a sound control-flow
model that reproduces the recorded truck visits without permitting
much additional behaviour. It is designed as a controlled comparison
following the recommendations of Rehse et al.~\cite{RWRehse2024Experimentation}:
every candidate is fitted on the same cases and evaluated on the same
cohorts with the same implementation of each metric.

\subsection{Discovery Input}
\label{sec:exp_discovery_input}

The input is the truck-visit event log of
Chapter~\ref{chp:case_study_and_data}. Each case is one completed visit
from Gate In to Gate Out. The yard events use eleven generic labels:
\texttt{Gate In}, \texttt{Gate Out}, and \texttt{receive},
\texttt{delivery} or \texttt{mixed} at the three handling areas RMG, LL
and HO2. Block identities such as \texttt{T21} are kept in the resource
attribute and are not part of the activity label.

The sequence of events within a case is taken from the explicit order
attribute \texttt{case:event:order}. The log is not re-sorted by
timestamp. Start and readiness timestamps are not used to infer
concurrency during discovery. Before discovery, the training and
validation partitions are frozen. The preparation step rejects missing
labels, duplicate order positions within a case, cases that appear in
more than one partition and partitions that are not chronological by
Gate In. It stores SHA-256 hashes of every input. Seven unit tests cover
these checks. The XES files used in ProM are written from the frozen
partitions by a script that records their hashes, re-reads each file,
checks that it reproduces the partition sequence by sequence and checks
that OpenXES, the XES library of ProM, imports it. The reserved test
partition is not read in E1, E2 or E3.

\subsection{Candidate Algorithms and Parameter Grid}
\label{sec:exp_candidates}

Four discovery families were compared. They rest on different
construction principles, so that the comparison covers more than one
way of trading fitness against precision \cite{DiscoveryBenchmark}.
The Inductive Miner recursively splits the log into sequence, choice,
parallel and loop operators and guarantees a sound, block-structured
net. Its variant Inductive Miner -- infrequent (IMf) filters behaviour
below a noise threshold at each split. At threshold 0.0 nothing is
filtered and every training visit fits the net
\cite{Leemans2014InductiveMiner}. This configuration is called IM 0.0
in the following. The interactive Data-aware Heuristic Miner (iDHM)
builds a causal net from dependency measures on directly-follows
frequencies and converts it into a Petri net
\cite{Mannhardt2017DHM,Mannhardt2017iDHM}. It was used with the
dependency and binding heuristics of the Flexible Heuristics Miner
\cite{Weijters2011FHM}. The Alpha algorithm and its extensions Alpha+
and Alpha++ derive places directly from the ordering relations of the
log and have no noise handling
\cite{vanderAalst2004AlphaMiner,deMedeiros2004AlphaPlus,Wen2007AlphaPlusPlus}.
The ILP Miner constructs places as solutions of integer linear programmes
over the prefix-closed log, based on the theory of regions
\cite{RWVanZelst2018ILP}. Its sequence encoding filter removes infrequent
branches of the prefix tree: a branch is kept only if its frequency reaches
at least $(1-t)$ times that of its most frequent sibling, where $t$ is the
filter threshold. Unlike the noise threshold of IMf, a higher threshold
therefore filters less; $t = 1$ keeps all behaviour. Split Miner was not
part of the comparison.

All nets were discovered in ProM Lite 1.4 \cite{vanDongen2005ProM} on
the complete training partition of the expanded population, with the
activity label (\texttt{concept:name}) as classifier, and exported as
PNML files with initial and final marking. iDHM was run without data
attributes, so its nets contain control flow only. Table~\ref{tab:exp_grid}
lists the configurations. They are a bounded exploration, not an
exhaustive tuning. The grid sizes differ between families. Conclusions
therefore concern the tested configurations and not the algorithm
families in general.

\paragraph{Prefix reference.}
In addition to the miners, a prefix-tree reference model is built
\cite{DeLaBriandais1959Trie}. Every observed prefix of a training
sequence becomes one place, and every observed extension one transition.
The language of this net equals exactly the set of training sequences.
It is not a simulation candidate. It shows how well the validation
visits can be explained by doing nothing more than memorising the
training sequences, and thus calibrates the precision values of the
discovered models.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:exp_discovery_eval}

Every net is evaluated on the training log (59,139 visits) and on the test log
(32,014 visits) of the split at 13 April 2026 (Section~\ref{sec:exp_partitions}).
The selection uses training values only. Test values are reported. The
evaluation has two levels: the quality of the net as a model of the log
(Section~\ref{sec:bg_conformance}), and its fitness for the purpose of
simulation (Section~\ref{sec:bg_coverage}).

\paragraph{Noise threshold}
A sequence counts as noise if it accounts for less than 0.1\,\% of the training
visits. The 17 sequences above this threshold cover 99\,\% of the 59,139 visits;
the threshold removes all 21 sequences that occur only once. The simulation has
to reproduce the long turnaround times, not only the average. The rare sequences
are long visits: their mean turnaround time is 62 minutes, against 37 minutes for
all visits. A higher threshold would treat more of these long visits as noise
(Table~\ref{tab:exp_noise_threshold}). The threshold does not filter the logs:
Level 1 uses all visits. It only decides which sequences the selected net must
accept (Level 2 and selection).

\paragraph{Level 1: quality of the net}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Soundness.} WOFLAN \cite{Verbeek2001Woflan}. Required for the
execution in ProSiT.
\item \textbf{Fitness.} Alignment-based trace fitness
\cite{Adriansyah2011CostFitness}, averaged over the visits. ProM 6.15, ``Replay a
Log on Petri Net for Conformance Analysis'' (exact A$^*$ with ILP). Costs: 1 for a
move on the log or on a visible transition, 0 for a silent move.
\item \textbf{Precision.} Escaping-edges precision \cite{MunozGama2010FreshLook},
ETConformance in ProM 6.15, on the discovered net. Same settings for every net:
``Lazy Invisibles'' and ``First Indeterminism Solving''. A non-fitting visit
counts only up to its first deviation. Used for the shortlist (A3, E2).
\item \textbf{Generalisation.} New test sequences that the net accepts (of 16),
from the same replay on the test log. Used for the shortlist (A3, E2).
Token-based generalisation \cite{Buijs2014Quality} is not used: it depends on the
representation of the net.
\item \textbf{Structure.} Places, visible and silent transitions.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Level 2: fitness for simulation}
A sequence that a net rejects is never generated by the simulator.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Rejected sequences.} Above and below the noise threshold, with the
share of fitting visits overall and per visit group (no RMG stop, one RMG block,
two or more RMG blocks).
\item \textbf{Rejected visits in KPI terms.} Their share of the truck-minutes
(sum of the turnaround times), of the visits above the training P90 turnaround
time, and of the multi-block visits.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Check of the precision values}
In a net with silent transitions, ETConformance can keep a wrong marking after a
prefix (Section~\ref{sec:bg_conformance}). Each value is therefore compared with
ETConformance on the same-language state machine (E2b). The tables report the
value of the discovered net and any deviation.

\paragraph{Tools}
Fitness, fitting visits, accepted sequences and precision: ProM 6.15
\cite{vanDongen2005ProM} (PNetReplayer 6.11.191, ETConformance 6.9.52), on the
PNML files exported from ProM and the XES files of the partitions. Soundness:
WOFLAN as implemented in PM4Py 2.7.22.3 \cite{Berti2023PM4Py}. Cross-check: the
unmodified alignment functions of PM4Py give the same fitting visits and accepted
sequences for every net. For unsound nets, alignment values are a diagnostic only.

\paragraph{Gates}
A net of the discovery grid (Table~\ref{tab:exp_grid}) is \emph{admissible} if it
passes two gates: \textbf{A1}, it is sound; \textbf{A2}, it accepts every sequence
above the noise threshold. The prefix tree is a reference only. The admissible
nets go to E2.

\section{Rule-Based Improvement (E2)}
\label{sec:exp_rules}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

E2 treats every admissible net of E1 in the same way. It is a treatment, not a
selection criterion. Discovery algorithms do not know what a truck can physically
do: a net can reach high fitness and precision and still allow a visit without a
stop, or two stops of one truck at the same time. Each domain rule
(Table~\ref{tab:domain_rules}) is therefore enforced where it can be expressed
exactly (Table~\ref{tab:exp_e2_steps}).

\subsection{Audit}
Each rule is checked on three levels, because conformance on one level does not
imply conformance on the next: the recorded log (a violation would question the
rule or the data), the language of the net, and the generated log (E3; the only
level for rule 4 and the data rules). On the language, each sequence rule is a
small automaton (a monitor) that runs in a product with the reachability graph of
the net (Section~\ref{sec:bg_rules}). If the net can complete a run that a monitor
rejects, the shortest such sequence is reported. The check is exact for bounded
nets, including nets with loops \cite{RWMaggi2011Monitoring,RWCarmona2022Conformance}.

\subsection{Language Repair (E2a)}
\label{sec:mc_repair}
The repair restricts the language to the sequences that satisfy the rules, in
three steps. First, the reachability graph of the net, with silent steps
resolved, is combined with the rule monitors. Second, states with the same
continuations are merged, which gives the smallest automaton of the restricted
language. Third, each state becomes one place and each edge one visible
transition. The result is a sound state machine without silent transitions. It
keeps every sequence of the original net that satisfies the rules and adds none.
The construction is closest to Dixit et al.~\cite{RWDixit2015Domain}, but builds
the restricted model exactly instead of searching among random edits.

The bound of rule 6 (at most four stops) is not encoded in the net. The stop guard
enforces the stronger equality with the planned stops, and the bound would enlarge
the state machine and leave fewer observations per routing tree.
Figures~\ref{fig:mc_repair_xor} and~\ref{fig:mc_repair_stop} show the same
restriction as two local patterns on the original net.

\subsection{Encoding as a State Machine (E2b)}
Every admissible net is written as a state machine with the same language, also
when the repair does not change it. The reason lies in the routing of ProSiT
(Section~\ref{sec:prosit_execution}). It draws the next transition from weights
learned per transition and normalised over the enabled transitions. The weights
see the case attributes and how often each activity has occurred, but not the
marking of the net. In a net with silent transitions and parallel branches this
has three consequences, which the state machine removes by construction:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Unobserved decisions.} Silent transitions are not in the log, so
their weights are learned from a replay that has to guess when they fired. In the
state machine every transition is visible, and every routing decision is learned
from observed events.
\item \textbf{No state.} Choices that are each observed can be combined into
sequences that the log never shows; skipping every stop gives a visit without a
stop. In the state machine each transition has one input place, which is one state
of the minimal automaton of the language, so the weights become state-dependent.
\item \textbf{Concurrency within a visit.} A parallel split lets ProSiT start two
stops of one truck at the same time. The state machine holds one token per truck
and thereby enforces rule 4.
\end{enumerate}
The stop guard (E2c) can act only at a visible decision as well. Because the
language is unchanged, the measures of E1 do not change, and any difference in the
simulation comes from the encoding. E3 tests this with a control condition
(Section~\ref{sec:exp_baseline}). Encoding all nets in the same way makes E3
compare languages, not encodings. The cost is a larger net with repeated labels;
readability is judged on the discovered net.

\subsection{Stop Guard (E2c)}
ProSiT has no guards (Section~\ref{sec:prosit_limits}). The stop guard adds one
guard to the simulation loop, as in a data Petri net (Section~\ref{sec:bg_rules}).
Before each routing draw it compares the stops done, $c$, with the planned stops,
$n$, known at Gate In. While $c<n$, Gate Out gets the weight zero; once $c=n$,
every yard transition gets the weight zero. If all enabled transitions then have
weight zero, Gate Out fires when $c=n$ (forced exit); otherwise the learned
weights are kept and the draw counts as an \emph{override}. Before any
simulation, the reachability graph of each net is combined with the counter $c$
for every $n$ from 1 to 4, and every reachable state is checked for whether the
visit can still end with exactly $n$ stops \cite{FelliDeLeoniMontali2021}.

\subsection{Acceptance and Shortlist}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A4, acceptance.} A repaired net keeps every fitting training and
test visit, every accepted training sequence and every accepted new test
sequence. Otherwise a rule or the construction is wrong.
\item \textbf{A3, shortlist.} The repaired nets that are not dominated in
precision and generalisation go to E3, at most three. A net is dominated if
another net is at least as good on both and better on one. Precision is measured
on the state machines, where ETConformance needs no heuristic
(Section~\ref{sec:bg_conformance}). Generalisation needs sequences that discovery
has not seen; it is the only criterion read from the test log. It decides which
nets enter E3, not which net is selected (Rule A, Section~\ref{sec:exp_e3_measures}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Decision rule}
\textbf{Rule A (applied): precision first, simulation as veto.} The most precise
net of the shortlist is selected, unless it fails the gate or is worse in the
primary criterion beyond the seed range. Standard model quality decides
\cite{Buijs2014Quality}; the simulation only rejects a net that does not work in
ProSiT. \textbf{Rule B (shown, not applied): simulation first,} as in automated
discovery of simulation models \cite{Camargo2020}: the primary criterion, then the
secondary criteria in their order. If the two rules select different nets, the
net selected by Rule B is carried through E4 and E5 as a sensitivity run.

The order of events within a simulated visit is taken from the start
timestamps; for equal timestamps the completion time decides. Each measure is the
mean over the five replications with its minimum and maximum. The replications
show the random variation of the simulator, not the uncertainty of the fitted
parameters.

\section{Mechanism Audit and Simulation Validity (E4)}
\label{sec:exp_mechanisms}
% ======================================================================
% Code: trucksimulation/simulation_e3/rq2_components.py (retrained components),
%   run_rq2.py (configurations), evaluate_rq2.py (measures), ctb_engine.py (switches);
%   runs: simulation_e3/runs/rq2_20261107. Design notes: simulation_e3/E4_RQ2_DESIGN_20261107.md

E4 answers RQ2: to what extent does the ProSiT simulation reproduce the
turnaround time of trucks and its response to load at the capacity-limited
yard blocks, and which model components cause the remaining error? The base is
the main configuration of E3: IM 0.0 repaired, stop guard, manifest attributes,
ProSiT 1.0.4 with trees and workload features.

A simulation model is valid only for an intended use
\cite{Sargent2013Validation}. Here the intended use is E5, which moves trucks
from busy to less busy blocks. A model can only show the effect of this change
if trucks wait longer and are served more slowly when a block is busy, as at the
terminal (Section~\ref{sec:observed_behaviour}). E4 therefore checks two things:
whether the simulated turnaround time has the right level, and whether it is
produced by the right mechanism under load.

\subsection{Evaluation Design}
\label{sec:exp_e4_design}

The evaluation follows the three levels of Sargent
\cite{Sargent2013Validation} (Section~\ref{sec:simulation_validation_background}).
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Verification.} Does the simulator do what it should? The modified
simulation loop must reproduce ProSiT exactly when all changes are switched off.
A retrained component must be identical to the fitted one when its training data
are not changed. Replayed arrivals must be exact, and the generated logs must
respect the capacity and the domain rules. The number of activities in progress
at a resource, which ProSiT uses for the workload features and for the capacity
check, is recomputed from the generated log and compared with the value the
simulator used.
\item \textbf{Conceptual validity.} Are the assumptions of each component
plausible for the terminal? Every learned model is inspected (split features,
leaves), and every mechanism of the simulation loop is compared with the
physical process (Table~\ref{tab:exp_e4_mechanisms}).
\item \textbf{Operational validity.} Is the output accurate enough for the
intended use? Generated logs are compared with the observed test log on four
perspectives (Table~\ref{tab:exp_e4_measures}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Reference values}
A distance between a generated and an observed log has no meaning on its own.
Two observed periods also differ. The differences between real periods are
therefore used as the reference: between the three weeks of the test period,
the cycle-time distance is 1.7 to 3.1 minutes and the median TTT differs by up
to 3 minutes; between the training and the test period, the cycle-time distance
is 5.5 minutes, and median and P90 differ by 4 and 11 minutes. The load profiles
of P4 are much more stable: the profile gaps between real periods are at most
2.0 minutes for the approach and 1.0 minute for the stop duration. The load
response is thus a stable property of the terminal, while the level of the
turnaround time follows the demand of the period.

\paragraph{Two weaknesses of the usual comparison}
Özdemir et al.\ point out two weaknesses of comparing a generated log with a
later test log \cite{Oezdemir2025RethinkingBPS}. First, the comparison mixes the
error of the model with the change of the process between the periods. Second,
distances based on the Wasserstein distance can hide temporal patterns. E4
addresses both. Most configurations replay the observed Gate-In times and
manifests of the test period (\emph{conditional replay}), so that the simulated
trucks are the observed ones and the demand is not part of the error. The final
configuration is also run on the training period, which separates the error of
the model from the change between periods. And the medians, quantiles and load
profiles of P1 and P4 are reported next to the Wasserstein-based distances.

\subsection{Error Attribution by Component Substitution}
\label{sec:exp_e4_attribution}

A truck meets the components of the simulator in a fixed order: the demand
(arrival time and manifest), the block allocation, the queue at the block
(capacity and waiting time) and the service (execution time); the exit follows.
The error of a component is measured by replacing it and observing how much of
the error disappears. Two kinds of replacement are used.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Oracle.} The component is replaced by the observed values of the
test period: replayed arrivals and manifests, or the observed block of each RMG
stop. This is trace-driven simulation \cite{Law2015Simulation}. It shows the
error that remains if the component were perfect.
\item \textbf{Repair.} The component stays learned, but a documented defect of
its training data or of the simulation loop is corrected.
\end{itemize}
The components are examined in the order in which the truck meets them. Each
step keeps the components before it at their oracle values, so that only one
component varies at a time. The generative alternatives, for example learned
instead of observed blocks, are reported as deviations from this chain. After
the final configuration has been chosen, each of its changes is reversed alone
(\emph{leave one out}), which shows whether every change is needed. Finally, the
error of the mean TTT is split exactly into the errors of approach, stop time,
transfer and exit.

\paragraph{Retraining protocol}
Retrained components use ProSiT's own feature table, discovery functions,
hyperparameters and random state. Only the stated change of the training data
differs. As a check, every component is first retrained on unchanged data; it
must equal the fitted component exactly.

\paragraph{Design decisions}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{One fitted model.} All configurations start from the same fitted
model. A configuration replaces single components, so that differences come
from the replaced component and not from a new fit.
\item \textbf{Observed blocks for the queue and service steps.} The share of each
block changes from week to week, so no model learned on the training period can
reproduce the block loads of the test period. To judge the queue and the service
at a block, the observed block of each stop is used. The error of the block
allocation itself is measured separately (E4b).
\item \textbf{Where the queue comes from.} A queue can arise in the simulation,
when a stop has to wait for a free lane, or it can be part of the learned waiting
time. Using both counts the waiting twice. The two consistent combinations are
compared: waiting from the moment a lane became free together with capacity at
start, and the whole delay before the stop without capacity limit. The second
treats waiting like an extraneous delay that the model learns from data
\cite{RWChapela2024Delays}.
\item \textbf{Number of lanes.} A block has three lanes and serves at most three
trucks at once. This is a physical limit. The log nevertheless shows RMG stops
that start while three other stops at the same block are still in progress
(5.6\,\% of the RMG stops in training, 8.1\,\% in the test period). For these
stops, the first of the other three ends after a median of 3 minutes (in 68 to
73\,\% of the cases within 5 minutes). The fourth truck is therefore already
registered at the block while it waits for a lane: the recorded stop interval
is the time at the block, not the time in the lane. The simulation uses three
lanes. Four lanes, as the log suggests, are run only as a sensitivity check and
are not admissible. For the same reason, the load profiles of P4 are compared
only for the states that three lanes allow (up to two other trucks at the start
of a stop, up to three trucks at the block at Gate In).
\item \textbf{Load in the service time.} At the terminal, part of the time at
the block is spent waiting for the crane, which serves several trucks. The stop
interval therefore grows with the number of trucks at the block. ProSiT does not
offer load features for execution times. The extension adds one feature, the
number of other stops in progress at the block when the stop starts, both in
training and in the simulation. With capacity at start, the simulated count is at
most the number of lanes minus one.
\item \textbf{Exact counting.} ProSiT counts the activities in progress at a
resource by walking backwards through its schedule and stops at the first
activity that has already ended. This is exact only if the schedule is sorted
by end time. The simulation loop stores activities in the order in which they
are processed, so some counts are too low. In training, ProSiT counts exactly.
The verification of E4 found this after the first runs; all configurations from
E4b on are therefore also run with an exact count (marked x), and the rule for
the final configuration is applied to these runs.
\item \textbf{Unchanged parts.} Routing, stop guard, case attributes, calendars
and the exit are not changed. The exit does not depend on the load (12.4 minutes
on average in both periods).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Choice of the final configuration}
The rule is fixed before the results are read. As in E1 and E2, the selection
uses training data only: the configurations of E4c and E4d (with exact counting)
are also run on the training period, with the replayed training visits and
their observed blocks, and compared with the training log. The test log is only
reported. A configuration is admissible if it produces no rule violations and
never more than three trucks at a block at once. Among the admissible ones, the
configuration with the smallest sum of the three profile gaps of P4 is chosen;
ties are broken by the cycle-time distance. The load response comes first
because it is what E5 needs and because it is stable between periods. The first
version of this rule allowed four trucks at a block, as observed in the log. It
was corrected to three after the domain confirmed the limit and before the
training runs were read; the configurations with four lanes or without a
capacity limit are therefore not admissible and are not run on the training
period.

\paragraph{Acceptance criteria for the intended use}
Fixed before the results are read, with thresholds taken from the differences
between real periods:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Physical admissibility:} no rule violations; at most three trucks
at a block at once.
\item \textbf{Load response:} Spearman correlation of hourly arrivals and median
TTT at least 0.49 (the lowest weekly value of the test period); profile gaps at
most 2.0 minutes for the approach, 1.0 minute for the stop duration and 5.75
minutes for the TTT by trucks in the terminal (the largest gaps between real
weeks).
\item \textbf{Extreme condition:} TTT rises when the arrival intensity rises
(0.8, 1.0, 1.2).
\item \textbf{Level:} cycle-time distance at most 3.1 minutes (within the
variation between weeks) or at most 5.5 minutes (within the change between
periods); reported for the conditional and the fully generative configuration.
\end{enumerate}
E5 is admissible for a quantitative estimate if criteria 1 to 3 hold for the
final configuration under replay; otherwise it is run only as a what-if
demonstration (Section~\ref{sec:exp_policy}). Criterion 4 states how far the
absolute values can be trusted.

\section{What-If Demonstration (E5)}
\label{sec:exp_policy}
% ======================================================================
% Code: trucksimulation/simulation_e3/run_rq2.py (s1 and exec_load settings), ctb_engine.py (switch s1),
%   evaluate_e5.py; runs: simulation_e3/runs/rq3_20261108. Written before the E5 runs were made.

E5 answers RQ3: to what extent can the simulation be used to analyse a
load-aware block allocation for deliveries under the physical limits of the
yard blocks, and what does it indicate about the effect on truck turnaround
time? The purpose is not a recommendation for the terminal. E5 shows what the
simulation validated in E4 can and cannot say about one change of the process.

\paragraph{Status after E4}
The final configuration of E4 passed the physical and the extreme-condition
criterion, but missed the load-response criterion narrowly
(Section~\ref{sec:exp_e4_runs}). By the rule fixed in E4, the simulation is
therefore not admissible for a quantitative estimate of a policy effect. E5 is
run as a \emph{what-if demonstration}: it states the direction of an effect and
the mechanism behind it, and only if both are robust to the weaknesses found in
E4. Effect sizes are reported as values of the model under the conditions of
E4, not as estimates for the terminal.

\paragraph{Scenario S1}
The observed waiting before the first RMG stop grows from 6 to 16 minutes when
three trucks are already at the block, while other blocks often have free lanes
(Section~\ref{sec:observed_behaviour}). The storage block of an export delivery
is assigned by yard planning, whereas a receive must go to the block where its
container is. S1 therefore moves only RMG deliveries. When a delivery becomes
possible, the simulator counts the stops at its block that are in progress or
already scheduled. If this number is three or more, all lanes are taken, and the
delivery is moved to the eligible block with the fewest such stops below three;
ties go to the nearest block. If no block is eligible, the delivery stays.
Receives and mixed stops keep their observed block. The rule draws no random
numbers. Three variants differ in the eligible blocks: the observed block
$\pm 1$, $\pm 2$, or any block (upper bound).

\paragraph{Fixed conditions}
The baseline is the final configuration of E4 on the test period: replayed
arrivals and manifests, observed blocks, capacity at start with three lanes,
lane-free waiting, load-dependent service and exact counting. In the baseline,
S1 only records the deliveries it would move (dry run); as a verification, the
baseline log must equal the E4 log for the same seed. Baseline and variants use
the same ten seeds (42--51). Equal seeds give common random numbers only until
the first moved stop, so all differences are computed per seed and then
averaged.

\paragraph{Measures}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Z1 Effect.} Paired difference (S1 minus baseline) of the mean,
median and P90 of the TTT of all visits, of the visits with a moved delivery,
and of the visits that arrive with 100 or more trucks in the terminal; mean
over the ten seeds with a 95\,\% $t$-interval.
\item \textbf{Z2 Mechanism.} Approach to the first RMG stop, share of RMG stops
that start with two other trucks at the block (a full block), and waiting for a
free lane per RMG stop.
\item \textbf{Z3 Distribution.} Paired change of the TTT per visit, by group:
visits with a moved delivery, other visits at the blocks that gave deliveries
away, visits at the blocks that received them, and all other visits; and by
flow type.
\item \textbf{Z4 Costs and physical limits.} Share of RMG deliveries moved,
distance in block numbers, largest number of trucks at a block (at most three)
and rule violations. Not modelled: driving distance, later rehandling and the
constraints of the yard plan.
\end{itemize}
The share of delay-peak hours is reported but not interpreted, because the
simulation produces almost none (E4).

\paragraph{Robustness (Z5)}
The $\pm 2$ variant is repeated, each time with its own baseline, under four
changes that address the weaknesses of E4: (a) service without load, the lower
bound of the slowdown at a busy block; (b) a stronger slowdown, in which the
service model sees one more truck whenever at least one other truck is at the
block; (c) arrivals compressed to an intensity of 1.2, where the simulated level
matched the observed one; (d) fully generative arrivals and blocks (hour-of-week
arrivals and training block shares).

\paragraph{Rules for the claims}
Fixed before the runs:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A direction is stated only if the 95\,\% interval of the paired difference
for the affected visits excludes zero in the main variants and the sign is the
same in all four robustness runs. If the sign changes, the finding is that the
simulation is not adequate for this question.
\item A mechanism is stated only if the approach and the share of RMG stops that
start at a full block change in the expected direction.
\item Interpretation of the variants: an effect already at $\pm 1$ or $\pm 2$
means that a local rule is enough in the model; an effect only with any block
means that it needs unlimited flexibility; no effect means that the model shows
no lever; better affected visits but worse other visits mean a shift of
waiting, not a reduction.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Threats Addressed by the Design}
\label{sec:exp_threats}
% ======================================================================
\begin{hints}
\item Short table: threat, design response, remaining limitation. Discussed further in Chapter~\ref{chp:discussion}.
\item Representation vs.\ language change in the repair: control model
with the same language as a state machine (E2).
\item Information leakage through case attributes: only attributes from
the planned visit manifest known at Gate In.
\item Double counting of waiting: the queue either arises in the simulation
(lane-free waiting with capacity at start) or is learned (whole delay without
capacity), never both (E4).
\item Selection on the test log: every selection uses the training log; the
test log only reports the selected configuration; rules fixed before the
results were read (E4: one documented correction of the capacity rule).
\item Drift between periods: model variants compared on the same period.
\item Single seed or few replications: replication counts and intervals.
\item Author as domain source: external domain review.
\end{hints}
