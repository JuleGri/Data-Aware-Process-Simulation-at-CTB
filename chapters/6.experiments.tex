\chapter{Methodology and Experimental Setup}
\label{chp:experiments}

This chapter describes how the research questions are answered: which
experiments are run, on which data, with which measures and with which criteria.
The concepts behind the methods are introduced in Chapter~\ref{chp:background};
the simulation model on which E3 to E5 operate is described in
Chapter~\ref{chp:process_discovery_methodology}, and the results follow in
Chapter~\ref{chp:results} in the same order.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Overview of the Experiments}
\label{sec:exp_overview}
\label{sec:exp_goals}
\label{sec:exp_selection_logic}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Five experiments build on each other (Figure~\ref{fig:exp_flow}). Each has one goal
and one result that the next experiment uses.

\input{figures/schemas/experiment_flow}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{E\arabic*}]
\item \textbf{Control-flow discovery.} Discover candidate nets with four families
of discovery algorithms and several settings, and evaluate them with conformance
metrics: fitness, precision, generalisation and coverage. Keep the nets that are
sound and accept every frequent sequence. Result: the admissible nets.
\item \textbf{Rule audit.} Check the admissible nets against the domain rules of
the truck visit and select the net whose behaviour respects them. Result: the
control-flow model, used exactly as discovered.
\item \textbf{Simulation of the selected net.} Run the selected net in ProSiT and
check that the simulated visits follow the observed behaviour. Result: a confirmed
control-flow model that ProSiT can use and that produces visits close to the
observed ones.
\item \textbf{Mechanism audit.} Check how well the simulation reproduces the
turnaround time and its response to load, and which components of the simulator
cause the remaining error. Result: the configuration used in E5 and an assessment
of its validity.
\item \textbf{Improvement measure.} Evaluate a redesign of the allocation of
deliveries to the yard blocks that targets the visits above the corridor. Result:
the effect of the measure on the KPI, the mechanism behind it, its costs, and how
far the simulation can support it.
\end{enumerate}

E1 to E3 answer RQ1, E3 and E4 answer RQ2, and E5 answers RQ3. The experiments do
not aim to rank discovery algorithms in general, or to show that the simulation is
valid for measures other than the turnaround time.

\section{Setting}
\label{sec:exp_setting}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Training and Test Log}
\label{sec:exp_partitions}

The event log is split once in time: 65\,\% of the visits form the training log
(59,139 visits, 2 March to 11 April 2026), 35\,\% the test log (32,014 visits, 13
to 30 April). The boundary falls on a weekend. Heavy trucks may not drive on
Sundays in Germany \cite{StVO30}, so the terminal holds no trucks at the boundary, and neither
period needs a warm-up phase.

Everything that a model learns, and every decision of the experiments, comes from
the training log: the discovery of the nets, the fitting of ProSiT, the thresholds
of the measures and every selection. The test log is used only to evaluate. E1
counts how many of its sequences that do not occur in training a net accepts; E3
to E5 simulate its period, and E4 and E5 compare the simulated with the observed
visits of the same trucks.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/kpi/drift_daily.pdf}
\caption{Trucks per day and daily median turnaround time over the whole period
(days with at least 300 visits). Blue: training log; orange: test log. The

green band the corridor of 25 to 35 minutes.}
\label{fig:drift_daily}
\end{figure}

Between the two periods, a temporal drift is apparent (Figure~\ref{fig:drift_daily}).
In the test period more trucks are in the terminal at the same time (95 instead of
77 on average at Gate In), the median turnaround time is 33 instead of 29 minutes,
and a quarter instead of a tenth of the hours are delay peaks
(Section~\ref{sec:observed_behaviour}). The terminal could not name a specific
operational cause. One plausible reason is the Easter holidays at the end of the
training period: cargo that was delayed then may have been moved into the
following weeks and concentrated there. A model learned on the training period
cannot know such a shift. An error on the test period therefore mixes the error of
the model with the drift. For this reason, model variants are compared with each
other on the same period, and the configuration selected in E4 is also run on the
training period.

\subsection{KPI and Measures}
\label{sec:exp_kpis}

The KPI is the truck turnaround time (TTT), from Gate In to Gate Out, that is, the
total duration of a case. The terminal aims at a TTT between 25 and 35 minutes for
every truck (Section~\ref{sec:prob_statement}). A visit above 35 minutes is too long
for the customer; a visit below 25 minutes is faster than the terminal wants,
because the cranes also serve the waterside and the rail.
Figure~\ref{fig:kpi_corridor} shows where the visits lie: in the test period,
47\,\% of the visits take longer than 35 minutes, and on average a visit spends
12.5 minutes above the corridor (training log: 36\,\% and 9.5 minutes). These
visits are the target of the improvement measure in E5.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/kpi/kpi_corridor.pdf}
\caption{Turnaround time per visit in the training and the test log, with the
corridor of 25 to 35 minutes. Minutes above 35 per visit: the mean of
$\max(\mathrm{TTT}-35, 0)$ over all visits.}
\label{fig:kpi_corridor}
\end{figure}

The visits above the corridor lose time in every part of the visit, not at one
point: before the first stop, at the stops and before Gate Out
(Table~\ref{tab:kpi_long_visits} in Appendix~\ref{app:observed_behaviour}). The
waiting before the first stop grows with the load of the block
(Section~\ref{sec:observed_behaviour}), and the stops take longer at busy blocks; a
different allocation of deliveries to the blocks acts on both. The thesis uses
four groups of measures:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Corridor:} the share of visits below, in and above the corridor, and
the minutes above the corridor per visit, which count both how many visits are too
long and by how much.
\item \textbf{Level and tail:} the mean, the median and the 90th percentile (P90)
of the TTT. The mean is moved by single visits of several hours; the median is the
robust central value.
\item \textbf{Parts of the visit:} the approach to the first stop, the stops, the
transfer between stops and the exit after the last stop. They add up to the TTT
(Section~\ref{sec:timestamp_semantics}) and show where the time is spent.
\item \textbf{Delay peaks:} hours in which at least 20 trucks arrive and their
median TTT is at least 37 minutes, the 90th percentile of the hours of the training
log. The 37 minutes are computed once and then kept for every run, so that a
measure that shortens the visits also reduces the number of delay peaks instead of
moving the threshold.
\end{itemize}
The corridor is not a calibration target of the simulation, and effects on the
waterside are outside the model.

\subsection{Simulation Runs and Replications}
\label{sec:exp_runs}

Every run simulates the 32,014 visits of the test period from its first Gate In,
starting with an empty terminal, as the real test period does after the weekend;
runs on the training period simulate its 59,139 visits in the same way. Every visit
is completed, so no visit is cut at the end of the period. E3 and E4 use five
seeds (42--46) per configuration, E5 ten seeds (42--51). A scenario of E5 and its
baseline use the same seeds and the same trucks, and the difference is computed
seed by seed.

Results are reported as the mean over the seeds. In E3 and E4 a difference between
configurations counts only if it lies outside the range of the seeds; E5 reports
95\,\% $t$-intervals of the paired differences. These intervals describe the
random variation of the simulator for one fitted model, not the uncertainty of the
fitted parameters or of the model structure.

\subsection{Domain Knowledge}
\label{sec:exp_review}

The model draws on three sources: the operational records, the literature, and the
use case that was worked out with the project team of the terminal, from terminal
development and the data warehouse team. They support different claims. The
records support empirical checks of sequences, times and loads. The use case
gives the meaning of the activities and timestamps, the domain rules, the three
lanes of a block, the corridor and the moment at which a yard position is
assigned. An assumption that is put into the model cannot be confirmed by the same
model: a check against the records only shows that the records do not contradict
it. The review of the model and of the results by the terminal is reported in
Section~\ref{sec:disc_stakeholders}.

\section{Control-Flow Discovery (E1)}
\label{sec:exp_discovery}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

E1 discovers candidate control-flow models with several algorithms and settings
and evaluates them with conformance metrics, the usual quality measures of process
discovery: fitness, precision and generalisation, and coverage of the observed
sequences. It asks which net reproduces the recorded truck visits without
permitting much additional behaviour. It is designed as a controlled comparison
following the recommendations of Rehse et al.~\cite{RWRehse2024Experimentation}:
every candidate is fitted on the same cases and evaluated on the same cohorts with
the same implementation of each metric.

\subsection{Discovery Input}
\label{sec:exp_discovery_input}

The input is the truck-visit event log of
Chapter~\ref{chp:case_study_and_data}. Each case is one completed visit
from Gate In to Gate Out. The yard events use eleven generic labels:
\texttt{Gate In}, \texttt{Gate Out}, and \texttt{receive},
\texttt{delivery} or \texttt{mixed} at the three handling areas RMG, LL
and HO2. Block identities such as \texttt{T21} are kept in the resource
attribute and are not part of the activity label.

The sequence of events within a case is taken from the explicit order
attribute \texttt{case:event:order}; the log is not re-sorted by timestamp,
and start timestamps are not used to infer concurrency. This choice matters for
methods that read time intervals. The lifecycle variant of the Inductive Miner
\cite{RWLeemans2016Lifecycle} treats overlapping intervals as concurrency, and the
six training visits whose LL stop ends after Gate Out
(Section~\ref{sec:timestamp_semantics}), 0.01\,\% of the log, are enough for it to
place Gate Out in parallel with the stops: its net accepts a stop after Gate Out
and breaks rule~2. With the end of every stop capped at the Gate Out of its visit,
it returns a net with the same language as the Inductive Miner without lifecycle
information. The training and test logs are frozen before discovery and written to
XES with checks that the files reproduce the logs sequence by sequence
(Appendix~\ref{app:model_inspection}).

\subsection{Candidate Algorithms and Parameter Grid}
\label{sec:exp_candidates}

Four discovery families were compared. They rest on different
construction principles, so that the comparison covers more than one
way of trading fitness against precision \cite{DiscoveryBenchmark}.
The Inductive Miner recursively splits the log into sequence, choice,
parallel and loop operators and guarantees a sound, block-structured
net. Its variant Inductive Miner -- infrequent (IMf) filters behaviour
below a noise threshold at each split. At threshold 0.0 nothing is
filtered and every training visit fits the net
\cite{Leemans2014InductiveMiner}. This configuration is called IM 0.0
in the following. The interactive Data-aware Heuristic Miner (iDHM)
builds a causal net from dependency measures on directly-follows
frequencies and converts it into a Petri net
\cite{Mannhardt2017DHM,Mannhardt2017iDHM}. It was used with the
dependency and binding heuristics of the Flexible Heuristics Miner
\cite{Weijters2011FHM}. The Alpha algorithm and its extensions Alpha+
and Alpha++ derive places directly from the ordering relations of the
log and have no noise handling
\cite{vanderAalst2004AlphaMiner,deMedeiros2004AlphaPlus,Wen2007AlphaPlusPlus}.
The ILP Miner constructs places as solutions of integer linear programmes
over the prefix-closed log, based on the theory of regions
\cite{RWVanZelst2018ILP}. Its sequence encoding filter removes infrequent
branches of the prefix tree: a branch is kept only if its frequency reaches
at least $(1-t)$ times that of its most frequent sibling, where $t$ is the
filter threshold. Unlike the noise threshold of IMf, a higher threshold
therefore filters less; $t = 1$ keeps all behaviour. Split Miner was not
part of the comparison.

All nets were discovered in ProM Lite 1.4 \cite{vanDongen2005ProM} on
the complete training log, with the
activity label (\texttt{concept:name}) as classifier, and exported as
PNML files with initial and final marking. iDHM was run without data
attributes, so its nets contain control flow only. Table~\ref{tab:exp_grid}
lists the configurations. They are a bounded exploration, not an
exhaustive tuning. The grid sizes differ between families. Conclusions
therefore concern the tested configurations and not the algorithm
families in general.

\paragraph{Prefix reference.}
In addition to the miners, a prefix-tree reference model is built
\cite{DeLaBriandais1959Trie}. Every observed prefix of a training
sequence becomes one place, and every observed extension one transition.
The language of this net equals exactly the set of training sequences.
It is not a simulation candidate. It shows how well the test
visits can be explained by doing nothing more than memorising the
training sequences, and thus calibrates the precision values of the
discovered models.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:exp_discovery_eval}

Every net is evaluated on the training log (59,139 visits) and on the test log
(32,014 visits) of the split at 13 April 2026 (Section~\ref{sec:exp_partitions}).
The selection uses training values only. Test values are reported. The
evaluation has two levels: the quality of the net as a model of the log
(Section~\ref{sec:bg_conformance}), and its fitness for the purpose of
simulation (Section~\ref{sec:bg_coverage}).

\paragraph{Coverage threshold}
A sequence is required if it accounts for at least 0.1\,\% of the training
visits. This is an admissibility rule for a simulation model, not a claim that
rarer sequences are errors: they are not required, but every one of them that a
net rejects is reported. The 17 required sequences cover 99\,\% of the 59,139
visits; the threshold leaves out all 21 sequences that occur only once. Like the
cleaning (Section~\ref{sec:cleaning_population}), it shapes what the simulation can
reproduce: a sequence that the selected net rejects is never generated. The simulation has
to reproduce the long turnaround times, not only the average. The rare sequences
are long visits: their mean turnaround time is 62 minutes, against 37 minutes for
all visits. A higher threshold would leave out more of these long visits
(Table~\ref{tab:exp_noise_threshold}). The threshold does not filter the logs:
Level 1 uses all visits. It only decides which sequences the selected net must
accept (Level 2 and selection).

\paragraph{Level 1: quality of the net}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Soundness.} WOFLAN \cite{Verbeek2001Woflan}. Required for the
execution in ProSiT.
\item \textbf{Fitness.} Alignment-based trace fitness
\cite{Adriansyah2011CostFitness}, averaged over the visits. ProM 6.15, ``Replay a
Log on Petri Net for Conformance Analysis'' (exact A$^*$ with ILP). Costs: 1 for a
move on the log or on a visible transition, 0 for a silent move.
\item \textbf{Precision.} Escaping-edges precision \cite{MunozGama2010FreshLook},
ETConformance in ProM 6.15, on the discovered net.\footnote{In a net with silent
transitions, the replay of ETConformance has to decide which silent path it
follows. The same plugin settings are used for every net (``Lazy Invisibles'' and
``First Indeterminism Solving''); with the default settings the plugin cannot
replay IM 0.0.} A non-fitting visit counts only up to its first deviation. Used to
rank the admissible nets (E2).
\item \textbf{Generalisation.} New test sequences that the net accepts (of 16),
from the same replay on the test log. Reported.
Token-based generalisation \cite{Buijs2014Quality} is not used: it depends on the
representation of the net.
\item \textbf{Structure.} Places, visible and silent transitions.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Level 2: fitness for simulation}
A sequence that a net rejects is never generated by the simulator.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Rejected sequences.} Above and below the coverage threshold, with the
share of fitting visits overall and per visit group (no RMG stop, one RMG block,
two or more RMG blocks).
\item \textbf{Rejected visits in KPI terms.} Their share of the truck-minutes
(sum of the turnaround times), of the visits above the training P90 turnaround
time, and of the multi-block visits.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Tools}
Fitness, fitting visits, accepted sequences and precision are computed in ProM
6.15 \cite{vanDongen2005ProM} on the PNML files exported from ProM; soundness with
WOFLAN \cite{Verbeek2001Woflan}. The precision values depend on how a tool replays
silent transitions. Appendix~\ref{app:discovery_results} compares them with the
two precision functions of PM4Py \cite{Berti2023PM4Py}: the values agree where the
silent transitions of a net do not branch (0.998 for HM 0.7 with all three), and
the ranking of the nets that pass the coverage check is the same with every implementation.

\paragraph{Checks}
A net of the discovery grid (Table~\ref{tab:exp_grid}) is \emph{admissible} if it
passes two checks: the \emph{soundness check}, it is sound, and the \emph{coverage
check}, it accepts every sequence above the coverage threshold. The prefix tree is
a reference only. The admissible nets go to E2.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Rule Audit (E2)}
\label{sec:exp_rules}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Discovery algorithms do not know what a truck can physically do: a net can reach
high fitness and precision and still allow a visit without a stop, or two stops
of one truck at the same time. E2 therefore checks every admissible net of E1
against the domain rules (Table~\ref{tab:domain_rules}) before it is simulated.
The nets are not changed by a process repair: a net whose language breaks a rule
of the truck visit is not admissible.

\subsection{Audit}
Each rule is checked on three levels, because conformance on one level does not
imply conformance on the next: the recorded log (a violation would question the
rule or the data), the language of the net, and the generated log (E3; the only
level for the data rules). On the language, each sequence rule is a small
automaton (a monitor) that runs in a product with the reachability graph of the
net (Section~\ref{sec:bg_rules}). If the net can complete a run that a monitor
rejects, the shortest such sequence is reported. The check is exact for bounded
nets, including nets with loops \cite{RWMaggi2011Monitoring,RWCarmona2022Conformance}.
Rule 4, no two stops of one truck at the same time, is a property of the net: it
is violated if a reachable marking holds the tokens that two stops need at the
same time, so that both can be executed together. Two stops that are both enabled
as alternatives, of which only one can fire, do not violate it.

\paragraph{Rule check} A net passes the rule check only if its language respects
rules 3, 4 and 5: every visit has a stop, the stops of a truck follow one another,
and a mixed stop is not combined with a separate delivery or receive at the same
LL or HO2 area. Rules 1 and 2 hold in every net, and rule 6, at most eight stops,
is a bound that every net with a loop exceeds and that the simulator enforces. If
several nets pass the soundness, coverage and rule checks, the most precise one is
selected; generalisation is reported.

\subsection{Stop Guard}
\label{sec:exp_guard}
Beyond the domain rules, every visit has a planned number of stops $n$, a case
attribute that is known at Gate In (Section~\ref{sec:entry_known_attributes}). A
simulated truck should perform exactly these stops. No discovered net can express
this condition, and ProSiT has no guards (Section~\ref{sec:prosit_limits}). The
stop guard of the simulation model adds it as a condition on the data of the case,
as a guard does in a data Petri net (Section~\ref{sec:bg_rules}); its mechanism is
described in Section~\ref{sec:sim_adaptations}. It is computed from the
reachability graph of the net \cite{FelliDeLeoniMontali2021} and looks ahead,
because in a discovered net the decisions are taken by silent transitions. The
routing trees of ProSiT also split on the planned number of stops; the guard does
not replace them, it removes the visits that the learned probabilities would still
allow with too many or too few stops.

\section{Simulation of the Selected Net (E3)}
\label{sec:exp_baseline}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

E3 simulates the selected net exactly as it was discovered, with all other
conditions held constant. It shows whether the control flow works in the
simulator, and it is the baseline for the mechanisms analysed in E4.

\subsection{Conditions}
\label{sec:exp_e3_conditions}
\label{sec:mc_hyperparameters}
\label{sec:mc_ctb_adaptations}
The main condition is the selected net with the stop guard, fitted with the
settings of Table~\ref{tab:sim_hyperparameters} and run in ProSiT's own simulator,
with its learned block choice and its capacity check. Two variants show why the
guard is needed: without any guard, and with a simple guard that only acts on the
visible stops and on Gate Out. The nets that failed a check of E1 or E2 (IM 0.0,
ILP 1.00, IMf 0.3, HM 0.8) are run in the same way, with the stop guard; they test
whether the conformance metrics anticipate what a net does in the simulator
(RQ1). ProSiT learns its parameters separately for each net, so the variants compare
complete fitted simulators.

\subsection{Measures}
\label{sec:exp_e3_measures}
The generated visits are compared with the training log. Each measure is the mean over the five replications with its minimum
and maximum, and a difference counts only if it exceeds this range. The
replications show the random variation of the simulator, not the uncertainty of
the fitted parameters.

\section{Mechanism Audit and Simulation Validity (E4)}
\label{sec:exp_mechanisms}
% ======================================================================
% Code: trucksimulation/simulation_e3/rq2_components.py (retrained components),
%   run_rq2.py (configurations), evaluate_rq2.py (measures), ctb_engine.py (switches);
%   runs: simulation_e3/runs/hm07orig_20261115/e4 (HM 0.7 as discovered). Design notes: simulation_e3/E4_RQ2_DESIGN_20261107.md

E4 answers RQ2: to what extent does the ProSiT simulation reproduce the
turnaround time of trucks and its response to load at the capacity-limited
yard blocks, and which model components cause the remaining error? The base is
the main configuration of E3: HM 0.7 as discovered, stop guard, case attributes of the planned visit,
ProSiT 1.0.4 with trees and workload features.

A simulation model is valid only for an intended use
\cite{Sargent2013Validation}. Here the intended use is E5, which moves trucks
from busy to less busy blocks. A model can only show the effect of this change
if trucks wait longer and are served more slowly when a block is busy, as at the
terminal (Section~\ref{sec:observed_behaviour}). E4 therefore checks two things:
whether the simulated turnaround time has the right level, and whether it is
produced by the right mechanism under load.

\subsection{Evaluation Design}
\label{sec:exp_e4_design}

The evaluation follows the three levels of Sargent
\cite{Sargent2013Validation} (Section~\ref{sec:simulation_validation_background}).
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Verification.} Does the simulator do what it should? The modified
simulator must reproduce ProSiT exactly when all changes are switched off.
A retrained component must be identical to the fitted one when its training data
are not changed. Replayed arrivals must be exact, and the generated logs must
respect the capacity and the domain rules. The number of activities in progress
at a resource, which ProSiT uses for the workload features and for the capacity
check, is recomputed from the generated log and compared with the value the
simulator used.
\item \textbf{Conceptual validity.} Are the assumptions of each component
plausible for the terminal? Every learned model is inspected (split features,
leaves), and every mechanism of the simulator is compared with the
physical process (Table~\ref{tab:exp_e4_mechanisms}).
\item \textbf{Operational validity.} Is the output accurate enough for the
intended use? Generated logs are compared with the observed test log on four
perspectives (Table~\ref{tab:exp_e4_measures}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Reference values}
A distance between a generated and an observed log has no meaning on its own.
Two observed periods also differ. The differences between real periods are
therefore used as the reference: between the three weeks of the test period,
the cycle-time distance is 1.7 to 3.1 minutes and the median TTT differs by up
to 3 minutes; between the training and the test period, the cycle-time distance
is 5.5 minutes, and median and P90 differ by 4 and 11 minutes. The load profiles
of P4 are much more stable: the profile gaps between real periods are at most
2.0 minutes for the approach and 1.0 minute for the stop duration. The load
response is thus a stable property of the terminal, while the level of the
turnaround time follows the demand of the period.

\paragraph{Two weaknesses of the usual comparison}
Özdemir et al.\ point out two weaknesses of comparing a generated log with a
later test log \cite{Oezdemir2025RethinkingBPS}. First, the comparison mixes the
error of the model with the change of the process between the periods. Second,
distances based on the Wasserstein distance can hide temporal patterns. E4
addresses both. Most configurations replay the observed Gate-In times and
case attributes of the test period (\emph{conditional replay}), so that the simulated
trucks are the observed ones and the demand is not part of the error. The final
configuration is also run on the training period, which separates the error of
the model from the change between periods. And the medians, quantiles and load
profiles of P1 and P4 are reported next to the Wasserstein-based distances.

\subsection{Error Attribution by Component Substitution}
\label{sec:exp_e4_attribution}

A truck meets the components of the simulator in a fixed order: the demand
(arrival time and case attributes), the block allocation, the queue at the block
(capacity and waiting time) and the service (execution time); the exit follows.
The error of a component is measured by replacing it and observing how much of
the error disappears. Two kinds of replacement are used.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Oracle.} The component is replaced by the observed values of the
test period: replayed arrivals and case attributes, or the observed block of each RMG
stop. This is trace-driven simulation \cite{Law2015Simulation}. It shows the
error that remains if the component were exact; for the blocks this holds only
for the simulated stops that have an observed counterpart (about three quarters
of the RMG stops).
\item \textbf{Repair.} The component stays learned, but a documented defect of
its training data or of the simulator is corrected.
\end{itemize}
The components are examined in the order in which the truck meets them. Each
step keeps the components before it at their oracle values, so that only one
component varies at a time. The generative alternatives, for example learned
instead of observed blocks, are reported as deviations from this chain. After
the final configuration has been chosen, each of its changes is reversed alone
(\emph{leave one out}), which shows whether every change is needed. Finally, the
error of the mean TTT is split exactly into the errors of approach, stop time,
transfer and exit.

\paragraph{Retraining protocol}
Retrained components use ProSiT's own feature table, discovery functions,
hyperparameters and random state. Only the stated change of the training data
differs. As a check, every component is first retrained on unchanged data; it
must equal the fitted component exactly.

\paragraph{Design decisions}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{One fitted model.} All configurations start from the same fitted
model. A configuration replaces single components, so that differences come
from the replaced component and not from a new fit.
\item \textbf{Observed blocks for the queue and service steps.} The share of each
block changes from week to week, so no model learned on the training period can
reproduce the block loads of the test period. To judge the queue and the service
at a block, the observed block of each stop is used where the simulated stop
has an observed counterpart, and the training shares otherwise. The error of the block
allocation itself is measured separately (E4b).
\item \textbf{Where the queue comes from.} A queue can arise in the simulation,
when a stop has to wait for a free lane, or it can be part of the learned waiting
time. Using both counts the waiting twice. The two consistent combinations are
compared: waiting from the moment a lane became free together with capacity at
start, and the whole delay before the stop without capacity limit. The second
treats waiting like an extraneous delay that the model learns from data
\cite{RWChapela2024Delays}.
\item \textbf{Number of lanes.} A block has three lanes and serves at most three
trucks at once. This is a physical limit. The log nevertheless shows RMG stops
that start while three other stops at the same block are still in progress
(5.6\,\% of the RMG stops in training, 8.1\,\% in the test period). For these
stops, the first of the other three ends after a median of 3 minutes (in 68 to
73\,\% of the cases within 5 minutes). A plausible reading is that the fourth
truck is already registered at the block while it waits for a lane, so that the
recorded interval includes waiting; delayed completion records would look the
same, and the data cannot tell the two apart. The simulation uses three lanes as
an approximation of the physical limit. Four lanes, as the log suggests, are run only as a sensitivity check and
are not admissible. For the same reason, the load profiles of P4 are compared
only for the states that three lanes allow (up to two other trucks at the start
of a stop, up to three trucks at the block at Gate In).
\item \textbf{Load in the service time.} At the terminal, part of the time at
the block is spent waiting for the crane, which serves several trucks. The stop
interval therefore grows with the number of trucks at the block. ProSiT does not
offer load features for execution times. The extension adds one feature, the
number of other stops in progress at the block when the stop starts. In training
it is counted at the recorded start; in the simulation it is counted when the
stop is scheduled, before the capacity check can move the stop to a later start,
and it is at most the number of lanes minus one.
\item \textbf{Exact counting.} ProSiT counts the activities in progress at a
resource by walking backwards through its schedule and stops at the first
activity that has already ended. This is exact only if the schedule is sorted
by end time. The simulator stores activities in the order in which they
are processed, so some counts are too low; in training, ProSiT counts exactly.
The verification of E4 measures how often this happens
(Section~\ref{sec:res_e4_verification}). The baseline and all configurations
from E4b on are run with an exact count (marked x), and the rule for the final
configuration is applied to these runs.
\item \textbf{Unchanged parts.} Routing, stop guard, case attributes, calendars
and the exit are not changed. The exit does not depend on the load (12.4 minutes
on average in both periods).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Choice of the final configuration}
The rule is fixed before the training runs of the choice are read. As in E1
and E2, the selection
uses training data only: the configurations of E4c and E4d (with exact counting)
are also run on the training period, with the replayed training visits and
their observed blocks, and compared with the training log. The test log is only
reported. A configuration is admissible if it produces no rule violations and
never more than three trucks at a block at once. Among the admissible ones, the
configuration with the smallest sum of the three profile gaps of P4 is chosen;
ties are broken by the cycle-time distance. The load response comes first
because it is what E5 needs and because it is stable between periods. The
configurations with four lanes or without a capacity limit break the physical
limit; they are sensitivity runs on the test period and are not run on the
training period.

\paragraph{Acceptance criteria for the intended use}
Fixed in the design of E4, before the final configuration is run, with
thresholds taken from the differences between real periods:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Physical admissibility:} no rule violations; at most three trucks
at a block at once.
\item \textbf{Load response:} Spearman correlation of hourly arrivals and median
TTT at least 0.49 (the lowest weekly value of the test period); profile gaps at
most 2.0 minutes for the approach, 1.0 minute for the stop duration and 5.75
minutes for the TTT by trucks in the terminal (the largest gaps between real
weeks).
\item \textbf{Extreme condition:} TTT rises when the arrival intensity rises
(0.8, 1.0, 1.2).
\item \textbf{Level:} cycle-time distance at most 3.1 minutes (within the
variation between weeks) or at most 5.5 minutes (within the change between
periods); reported for the conditional and the fully generative configuration.
\end{enumerate}
E5 is admissible for a quantitative estimate if criteria 1 to 3 hold for the
final configuration under replay; otherwise it is run only as a what-if
demonstration (Section~\ref{sec:exp_policy}). Criterion 4 states how far the
absolute values can be trusted.

\section{Improvement Measure: Load-Aware Block Allocation (E5)}
\label{sec:exp_policy}
% ======================================================================
% Code: trucksimulation/simulation_e3/run_rq2.py (s1 and exec_load settings), ctb_engine.py (switch s1),
%   evaluate_e5.py, evaluate_e5_kpi.py (corridor measures); runs: simulation_e3/runs/hm07orig_20261115/e5,
%   eval_e5, eval_e5_kpi35.

E5 evaluates an improvement measure: a redesign of the allocation of deliveries
to the yard blocks that aims at the visits above the corridor. It answers RQ3: to
what extent can the simulation be used to evaluate such a measure under the
physical limits of the blocks, and what does it indicate about its effect on the
turnaround time? Three variants of the measure differ in how far a delivery may be
moved, so that E5 also shows how much flexibility the measure needs. Whether the
result can support an operational recommendation, or only a statement about the
simulated system, depends on the validation of E4.

\paragraph{Status after E4}
The final configuration of E4 passes the physical and the extreme-condition
criterion, but misses two of the four thresholds of the load-response criterion
(Section~\ref{sec:res_e4_acceptance}). By the rule fixed in E4, the simulation is
therefore not admissible for a quantitative estimate of a policy effect. E5 is
run as a \emph{what-if demonstration}: it states the direction of an effect and
the mechanism behind it, and only if both are robust to the weaknesses found in
E4. Effect sizes are reported as values of the model under the conditions of
E4, not as estimates for the terminal. Robustness within the tested models
does not independently establish the direction of an effect at CTB.

\paragraph{Scenario S1}
The observed waiting before the first RMG stop grows from 6 to 16 minutes when
three trucks are already at the block, while other blocks often have free lanes
(Section~\ref{sec:observed_behaviour}). The storage block of an export delivery
is assigned by yard planning, whereas a receive must go to the block where its
container is. S1 therefore moves only RMG deliveries. When a delivery becomes
possible, the simulator counts the committed load of its block: the stops in
progress there or already scheduled. If this number is three or more, the block
counts as full, and the delivery is moved to the eligible block with the lowest
committed load below three; ties go to the nearest block. The rule thus decides
when the delivery stop becomes possible: at Gate In for a delivery that is the
first stop of a visit, the moment the terminal assigns the yard position, and
later for a delivery after another stop. If no block is eligible, the delivery stays.
Receives and mixed stops keep their observed block. The rule draws no random
numbers. Three variants differ in the eligible blocks: the observed block
$\pm 1$, $\pm 2$, or any block (upper bound).

\paragraph{Fixed conditions}
The baseline is the final configuration of E4 on the test period: replayed
arrivals and case attributes, observed blocks, capacity at start with three lanes,
lane-free waiting, load-dependent service and exact counting. In the baseline,
S1 only records the deliveries it would move (dry run); as a verification, the
baseline log must equal the E4 log for the same seed. Baseline and variants use
the same ten seeds (42--51). Equal seeds give common random numbers only until
the first moved stop, so all differences are computed per seed and then
averaged.

\paragraph{Measures}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Z1 Effect.} Paired difference (S1 minus baseline) of the corridor
measures (share of visits above 35 minutes and in the corridor, minutes above 35
per visit; Section~\ref{sec:exp_kpis}), of the mean, median and P90 of the TTT of
all visits, of the TTT of the visits with a moved delivery, and of the visits that
arrive with 100 or more trucks in the terminal; mean over the ten seeds with a
95\,\% $t$-interval. Every difference is measured against the simulated baseline
of the same seed. No simulated difference is added to an observed value: the
model does not reproduce the observed level (E4), so a simulated difference is
not a prediction for the terminal.
\item \textbf{Z2 Mechanism.} Approach to the first RMG stop, share of RMG stops
that start with two other trucks at the block (a full block), and waiting for a
free lane per RMG stop.
\item \textbf{Z3 Distribution.} Paired change of the TTT per visit, by group:
visits with a moved delivery, other visits at the blocks that gave deliveries
away, visits at the blocks that received them, and all other visits; and by
flow type.
\item \textbf{Z4 Costs and physical limits.} Share of RMG deliveries moved,
distance in block numbers, largest number of trucks at a block (at most three)
and rule violations. Not modelled: driving distance, later rehandling and the
constraints of the yard plan.
\end{itemize}
The share of delay-peak hours is reported but not interpreted, because the
simulation produces almost none (E4).

\paragraph{Robustness (Z5)}
The $\pm 2$ variant is repeated, each time with its own baseline, under four
changes that address the weaknesses of E4: (a) service without load, the lower
bound of the slowdown at a busy block; (b) a stronger slowdown, in which the
service model sees one more truck whenever at least one other truck is at the
block; (c) arrivals compressed to an intensity of 1.2, where the simulated level
matched the observed one; (d) fully generative arrivals and blocks (hour-of-week
arrivals and training block shares).

\paragraph{Rules for the claims}
Fixed before the runs of E5:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A direction is stated only if the 95\,\% interval of the paired difference
for the affected visits excludes zero in the main variants and the sign is the
same in all four robustness runs. If the sign changes, the finding is that the
simulation is not adequate for this question.
\item A mechanism is stated only if the approach and the share of RMG stops that
start at a full block change in the expected direction.
\item Interpretation of the variants: an effect already at $\pm 1$ or $\pm 2$
means that a local rule is enough in the model; an effect only with any block
means that it needs unlimited flexibility; no effect means that the model shows
no lever; better affected visits but worse other visits mean a shift of
waiting, not a reduction.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Chapter Summary}

E1 and E2 select the control-flow model with checks fixed on the training log:
soundness, acceptance of every frequent sequence and the domain rules; the most
precise admissible net is used as discovered, with a stop guard for the planned
number of stops. E3 shows whether this net works in the simulator. E4 measures
how far the simulation reproduces the turnaround time and its response to load,
attributes the remaining error to the components the truck meets, chooses the
final configuration on the training period and checks it against acceptance
criteria fixed in advance. E5 applies one block allocation rule to this model and
states only what the rules for the claims allow.
