\chapter{Methodology and Experimental Setup}
\label{chp:experiments}

This chapter describes the pipeline illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:exp_flow}. The
pipeline takes the training and test logs of Chapter~\ref{chp:case_study_and_data}
and the domain rules of the terminal as input and produces three outputs. These
are a control-flow model that ProSiT can execute, a simulation model whose validity
for its intended use is assessed, and the effect of a load-aware block allocation
on the turnaround time. First, candidate nets are discovered from the training log
and evaluated with conformance measures (E1). Next, the admissible nets are audited
against the domain rules (E2), and the discovered nets are simulated in ProSiT to
test whether they can be used for simulation (E3). The simulation is then validated
against the observed turnaround time and its response to load (E4). Finally, the
validated model is used to evaluate a redesign of the block allocation in a what-if
analysis (E5). The configuration and the adaptations of the simulation model are
described in Chapter~\ref{chp:process_discovery_methodology}.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Overview of the Experiments}
\label{sec:exp_overview}
\label{sec:exp_goals}
\label{sec:exp_selection_logic}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Five experiments were designed that build on each other
(Figure~\ref{fig:exp_flow}). Each has one goal and produces one result that the
next experiment uses. Every selection is made on the training log, and the test log
is used for evaluation.

\input{figures/schemas/experiment_flow}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{E\arabic*}]
\item \textbf{Control-flow discovery.} Discover candidate nets with four families
of discovery algorithms and several settings, and evaluate them with conformance
measures, namely fitness, precision, generalisation and coverage. The result is the
set of admissible nets, which are sound and accept every frequent sequence.
\item \textbf{Rule audit.} Audit the admissible nets against the domain rules of
the truck visit and select the most precise net whose language respects them. The
result is the control-flow model, used exactly as discovered, together with a stop
guard for the planned number of stops.
\item \textbf{Simulation of the discovered nets.} Give the discovered nets to
ProSiT and test whether ProSiT can learn and run them and whether the simulated
visits reproduce the observed stop structure. The result is a control-flow model
that is confirmed for simulation.
\item \textbf{Mechanism audit and simulation validity.} Assess how well the
simulation reproduces the turnaround time and its response to load, and which parts
of the simulator cause the remaining error. The result is the final configuration
used in E5 and an assessment of its validity.
\item \textbf{Load-aware block allocation.} Evaluate a redesign of the allocation
of deliveries to the yard blocks that aims to reduce the hours above the corridor.
The result is the effect of the redesign on the KPI, the mechanism behind it, its
costs, and the extent to which the simulation can support it.
\end{enumerate}

E1 to E3 answer RQ1, E3 and E4 answer RQ2, and E5 answers RQ3. The experiments do
not aim to rank discovery algorithms in general, or to show that the simulation is
valid for measures other than the turnaround time.

\section{Setting}

\subsection{Training and Test Log}
\label{sec:exp_partitions}

The event log is split once in time. Of the visits, 65\,\% form the training log
(59,139 visits, 2 March to 11 April 2026) and 35\,\% the test log (32,014 visits, 13
to 30 April). The boundary falls on a weekend. Heavy trucks may not drive on
Sundays in Germany \cite{StVO30}, so the terminal holds no trucks at the boundary,
and neither period needs a warm-up phase.

Everything that a model learns comes from the training log, namely the discovery
of the nets, the fitting of ProSiT, the delay-peak threshold and every selection
between nets or configurations. The test log is used for evaluation. E1 counts how
many of its sequences that do not occur in training a net accepts. E3 to E5
simulate its period, and E4 and E5 compare the simulated with the observed visits
of the same trucks. One exception concerns the acceptance criteria of E4
(Section~\ref{sec:exp_e4_runs}). Their thresholds come from the natural variation
between real periods, and this variation is measured partly between the weeks of
the test period. The thresholds use only observed data, never simulated results,
and they were fixed before the final configuration was run. The evaluation of E4
is nevertheless not fully independent of the test period.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/kpi/drift_daily.pdf}
\caption{Trucks per day and daily median turnaround time, days with at least 300 visits (training blue, test orange, Easter grey, corridor green).}
\label{fig:drift_daily}
\end{figure}

Between the two periods, a temporal drift is apparent (Figure~\ref{fig:drift_daily}).
In the test period more trucks are in the terminal at the same time (95 instead of
77 on average at Gate In), and the median turnaround time is 33 instead of 29
minutes. The yard utilisation data, which record every four hours how full each
block is, show one concrete difference. Blocks T26, T18 and T15 were emptied in
April and stayed empty for up to three weeks, so that on average 2.4 of the 22
blocks were empty in the test period, against 1.1 in the training period
(Figure~\ref{fig:obs_block_occupancy}). According to the terminal, these closures
were planned for maintenance. With fewer blocks in use, the same trucks meet fully
loaded blocks more often. A second plausible reason is the Easter holidays at the
end of the training period. Cargo that was delayed then may have been moved into
the following weeks and concentrated there. A model learned on the training period
cannot anticipate such a shift. An error on the test period therefore mixes the
error of the model with the drift. For this reason, model variants are compared
with each other on the same period, and the configuration selected in E4 is also
run on the training period.

\subsection{KPI and Measures}
\label{sec:exp_kpis}

The KPI is the truck turnaround time (TTT), from Gate In to Gate Out, which is the
total duration of a case. The terminal judges it per hour. The mean TTT of the
trucks that leave through Gate Out in an hour, that is, of all visits completed in
that hour, should lie between 25 and 35 minutes
(Section~\ref{sec:prob_statement}). Only hours with at least 20 trucks are counted,
the same minimum as for the delay peaks below. Figure~\ref{fig:kpi_corridor} shows
the hourly mean TTT. In the test period it exceeds 35 minutes in 56\,\% of the
hours, and averaged over all hours it lies 5.5 minutes above the corridor. In the
training log the values are 38\,\% and 3.8 minutes. These hours are the target of
the improvement measure in E5.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/kpi/kpi_corridor_hourly.pdf}
\caption{Mean turnaround time per Gate-Out hour in the training and the test log.}
\label{fig:kpi_corridor}
\end{figure}

Visits longer than 35 minutes lose time in every part of the visit, not at a single
point. They spend longer before the first stop, at the stops and before Gate Out
(Table~\ref{tab:kpi_long_visits} in Appendix~\ref{app:observed_behaviour}). The
waiting before the first stop grows with the load of the block
(Section~\ref{sec:observed_behaviour}), and the stops take longer at busy blocks. A
different allocation of deliveries to the blocks acts on both. The thesis uses
four groups of measures.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Corridor.} The share of hours whose mean TTT lies below, in or above
the corridor, and the minutes by which the hourly mean exceeds 35, averaged over
the hours. They capture both how many hours are too long and by how much. The same
measures per visit show which visits gain.
\item \textbf{Level and tail.} The mean, the median and the 90th percentile (P90)
of the TTT. The mean is sensitive to single visits of several hours. The median is
the robust central value.
\item \textbf{Parts of the visit.} The approach to the first stop, the stops, the
transfer between stops and the exit after the last stop. They add up to the TTT
(Section~\ref{sec:timestamp_semantics}) and show where the time is spent.
\item \textbf{Delay peaks.} Hours in which at least 20 trucks arrive and their
median TTT is at least 37 minutes, the 90th percentile of the hours of the training
log. The threshold of 37 minutes is computed once and then kept for every run, so
that a measure that shortens the visits also reduces the number of delay peaks
instead of moving the threshold.
\end{itemize}
The corridor is not a calibration target of the simulation, and effects on the
waterside are outside the model.

\subsection{Simulation Runs and Replications}
\label{sec:exp_runs}

Every run simulates the 32,014 visits of the test period from its first Gate In,
starting with an empty terminal, as the real test period does after the weekend.
Runs on the training period simulate its 59,139 visits in the same way. Every visit
is completed, so no visit is cut at the end of the period. E3 and E4 use five
seeds (42--46) per configuration, E5 ten seeds (42--51). A scenario of E5 and its
baseline use the same seeds and the same trucks, and the difference is computed
seed by seed.

Results are reported as the mean over the seeds. In E3 and E4, a difference between
two configurations is interpreted only if it is larger than the spread of their
results over the five seeds, from the smallest to the largest value. E5 reports
95\,\% $t$-intervals of the paired differences. These intervals describe how much
the results of one fitted model vary between seeds. They do not describe the
uncertainty of the fitted parameters or of the model structure.

\subsection{Sources of Knowledge}
\label{sec:exp_review}

The model combines three sources of knowledge, each of which supports a different
kind of claim. The operational records of the terminal support empirical
statements about sequences, times and loads. The literature provides the methods
and the concepts against which the model is designed and evaluated. The terminal
contributes knowledge that the records do not contain, such as the physical limits
of the blocks, the domain rules, the definition of the KPI corridor and the way in
which the yard position of a delivery is assigned.

\section{Control-Flow Discovery (E1)}
\label{sec:exp_discovery}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

E1 discovers candidate control-flow models with several algorithms and settings
and evaluates their conformance with the usual quality measures of process
discovery. These are fitness, precision and generalisation, complemented by the
coverage of the observed sequences. The goal is a net that reproduces the recorded
truck visits without permitting much additional behaviour. E1 is designed as a
controlled comparison following the recommendations of Rehse et
al.~\cite{RWRehse2024Experimentation}. Every candidate is discovered from the
training log and evaluated with the same implementation of each measure.

\subsection{Discovery Input}
\label{sec:exp_discovery_input}

The input is the truck-visit event log of
Chapter~\ref{chp:case_study_and_data}. Each case is one completed visit
from Gate In to Gate Out. The yard events use eleven generic labels, namely
\texttt{Gate In}, \texttt{Gate Out}, and \texttt{receive},
\texttt{delivery} or \texttt{mixed} at the three handling areas RMG, LL
and HO2. Block identities such as \texttt{T21} are kept in the resource
attribute and are not part of the activity label.

The events of a visit are ordered by their start timestamps, with a tie in the
same minute resolved by the complete timestamp
(Section~\ref{sec:timestamp_semantics}). The miners receive the resulting sequence
of activity labels. The time intervals are not used to infer concurrency, because
after cleaning no two stops of a visit overlap. A sensitivity analysis with the
lifecycle variant of the Inductive Miner \cite{RWLeemans2016Lifecycle}, which reads
the intervals, shows what they would add. It treats overlapping intervals as
concurrency, and the six training visits whose LL stop is completed after Gate Out
(Section~\ref{sec:timestamp_semantics}), 0.01\,\% of the log, are enough for it to
place Gate Out in parallel with the stops. Its net accepts a stop after Gate Out
and breaks rule~2. With the completion of these stops set to the Gate Out of their
visit, it returns a net with the same language as the Inductive Miner without
lifecycle information. The intervals therefore add no behaviour beyond these six
booking artefacts. The training and test logs are frozen before discovery and
written to XES in this time order, and a script verifies that the files reproduce
the logs sequence by sequence (Appendix~\ref{app:model_inspection}).

\subsection{Candidate Algorithms and Parameter Grid}
\label{sec:exp_candidates}

Four families of discovery algorithms were compared, each with several parameter
settings. A discovery algorithm has to balance two goals. The net should replay as
much of the log as possible (fitness) and allow as little behaviour beyond the log
as possible (precision). The four families build a net in different ways and treat
rare behaviour differently, so they reach different balances between these two
goals \cite{DiscoveryBenchmark}.

The Inductive Miner recursively splits the log into sequence, choice, parallel and
loop operators and guarantees a sound, block-structured net. Its variant Inductive
Miner, infrequent (IMf) filters behaviour below a noise threshold at each split. At
threshold 0.0 nothing is filtered and every training visit fits the net
\cite{Leemans2014InductiveMiner}. This configuration is called IM 0.0 in the
following. The interactive Data-aware Heuristic Miner (iDHM) builds a causal net
from dependency measures on directly-follows frequencies and converts it into a
Petri net \cite{Mannhardt2017DHM,Mannhardt2017iDHM}. It was used with the
dependency and binding heuristics of the Flexible Heuristics Miner
\cite{Weijters2011FHM}. The Alpha algorithm and its extensions Alpha+ and Alpha++
derive places directly from the ordering relations of the log
\cite{vanderAalst2004AlphaMiner,deMedeiros2004AlphaPlus,Wen2007AlphaPlusPlus}. If
two activities directly follow each other in both orders somewhere in the log, the
Alpha algorithms treat them as parallel. Because they have no noise handling, a
single visit with an unusual order is enough for this, and the resulting net may
allow many more orders than observed or may not be sound. The ILP Miner constructs
places as solutions of integer linear programmes over the prefix-closed log, based
on the theory of regions \cite{RWVanZelst2018ILP}. In simple terms, it searches for
places that never block an observed sequence and, among these, prefers the places
that restrict the behaviour most. Its sequence encoding filter removes infrequent
branches of the prefix tree. A branch is kept only if its frequency reaches at
least $(1-t)$ times that of its most frequent sibling, where $t$ is the filter
threshold. Unlike the noise threshold of IMf, a higher threshold therefore filters
less, and $t = 1$ keeps all behaviour. Split Miner was not part of the comparison.

All nets were discovered in ProM Lite 1.4 \cite{vanDongen2005ProM} on the complete
training log, with the activity label (\texttt{concept:name}) as classifier, and
exported as PNML files with initial and final marking. iDHM was run without data
attributes, so its nets contain only the control flow.
Table~\ref{tab:exp_grid} in Appendix~\ref{app:discovery_results} lists the
configurations. The executed configurations are a bounded exploration, not an
exhaustive tuning, and the results apply to these configurations only.

\paragraph{Prefix reference}
In addition to the miners, a prefix-tree reference model is built
\cite{DeLaBriandais1959Trie}. Every observed prefix of a training sequence becomes
one place, and every observed extension one transition. The language of this net
equals exactly the set of training sequences. It is not a simulation candidate. It
shows how well the test visits can be explained by memorising the training
sequences and nothing more, and thus calibrates the precision values of the
discovered models.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:exp_discovery_eval}

Every net is evaluated on the training log (59,139 visits) and on the test log
(32,014 visits). The selection uses the values on the training log only. The values
on the test log are reported for comparison and do not influence the selection.
The evaluation considers two aspects, the quality of the net as a model of the log
and its fitness for the purpose of simulation (Section~\ref{sec:bg_conformance}).

\paragraph{Quality of the net}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Soundness.} A net is sound if every run from the initial marking can
still reach the final marking, if no token is left behind when it does, and if
every transition can fire in some run (Section~\ref{sec:bg_petri}). Soundness was
tested with WOFLAN in ProM. WOFLAN adds a transition from the final place back to
the initial place and tests whether the resulting net is live and bounded, which
is equivalent to soundness \cite{Verbeek2001Woflan}. ProSiT requires a sound net.
\item \textbf{Fitness.} Alignment-based trace fitness
\cite{Adriansyah2011CostFitness}, averaged over the visits. ProM 6.15, ``Replay a
Log on Petri Net for Conformance Analysis'' (exact A$^*$ with ILP). The costs are 1
for a move on the log or on a visible transition and 0 for a silent move.
\item \textbf{Precision.} Escaping-edges precision \cite{MunozGama2010FreshLook},
ETConformance in ProM 6.15, on the discovered net.\footnote{In a net with silent
transitions, the replay of ETConformance has to decide which silent path it
follows. The same plugin settings are used for every net (``Lazy Invisibles'' and
``First Indeterminism Solving''). With the default settings the plugin cannot
replay IM 0.0.} A non-fitting visit counts only up to its first deviation. Precision
ranks the admissible nets in E2.
\item \textbf{Generalisation.} The number of new test sequences, of 16, that the
net accepts, from the same replay on the test log. Reported only. Token-based
generalisation \cite{Buijs2014Quality} is not used, because it depends on the
representation of the net.
\item \textbf{Structure.} Places, visible and silent transitions.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Fitness for simulation}
A sequence that a net rejects is never generated by the simulator. This aspect
therefore measures which observed sequences a net rejects and how much of the
turnaround time they represent.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Coverage threshold.} A sequence is required if it accounts for at
least 0.1\,\% of the training visits. This is an admissibility rule for a
simulation model, not a claim that rarer sequences are errors. The 17 required
sequences cover 99\,\% of the 59,139 visits, and the threshold leaves out all 21
sequences that occur only once. Like the cleaning
(Section~\ref{sec:cleaning_population}), the threshold shapes what the simulation
can reproduce. The rare sequences are long visits, with a mean turnaround time of
62 minutes against 37 minutes for all visits, and the simulation has to reproduce
the long turnaround times, not only the average. A higher threshold would leave
out more of these visits. The discovery and all measures use all visits. The
threshold only decides which sequences an admissible net must accept.
\item \textbf{Rejected sequences.} The number of training sequences that the net
rejects, above and below the coverage threshold, and the share of fitting visits,
overall and per visit group (no RMG stop, one RMG block, two or more RMG blocks).
\item \textbf{Rejected visits in KPI terms.} The share of the rejected visits in
the truck-minutes (the sum of the turnaround times), in the visits above the
training P90 turnaround time and in the multi-block visits. These shares matter,
because a net that rejects long or multi-block visits removes the delay peaks and
the visit types at the bottleneck from the simulation.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Tools}
Fitness, fitting visits, accepted sequences and precision are computed in ProM
6.15 \cite{vanDongen2005ProM} on the PNML files exported from ProM Lite, and
soundness with WOFLAN in ProM \cite{Verbeek2001Woflan}. As a cross-check, precision
is also computed with the two precision functions of PM4Py \cite{Berti2023PM4Py}.

\paragraph{Admissibility}
A net of the discovery grid (Table~\ref{tab:exp_grid}) is \emph{admissible} if it
meets two criteria. It is sound (\emph{soundness criterion}), and it accepts every
sequence above the coverage threshold (\emph{coverage criterion}). The prefix tree
serves as a reference only. Admissibility does not imply good conformance values.
The admissible nets go to E2.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Rule Audit (E2)}
\label{sec:exp_rules}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Discovery algorithms do not know what a truck can physically do. A net can reach
high fitness and precision and still allow a visit without a stop, or two stops
of one truck at the same time. E2 therefore audits every admissible net of E1
against the domain rules (Table~\ref{tab:domain_rules}) before it is simulated.

\subsection{Audit}
Each rule is tested on three levels, because conformance on one level does not
imply conformance on the next. These levels are the recorded log, where a violation
would question the rule or the data, the language of the net, and the generated log
(E3), which is the only level for the data rules.

On the language, each sequence rule is written as a finite automaton that accepts
exactly the sequences that respect the rule (Section~\ref{sec:bg_rules}). This
automaton is combined with the reachability graph of the net, the graph of all
markings that the net can reach. In the resulting synchronous product, the net and
the automaton advance together on every visible activity, while silent transitions
advance only the net. Every complete run of the net is thereby aligned with the
rule. If the net can complete a run that the automaton does not accept, the
shortest such sequence is reported. The test is exact for bounded nets, including
nets with loops \cite{RWMaggi2011Monitoring,RWCarmona2022Conformance}. Rule~4, no
two stops of one truck at the same time, is a property of the net. It is violated if
a reachable marking holds the tokens that two stops need at the same time, so that
both can be executed together. Two stops that are both enabled as alternatives, of
which only one can fire, do not violate it.

\paragraph{Rule criterion} A net meets the rule criterion only if its language
respects rules 3, 4 and 5. Every visit has a stop, the stops of a truck follow one
another, and a mixed stop is not combined with a separate delivery or receive at
the same LL or HO2 area. Rules 1 and 2 hold in every net. Rule~6, at most eight
stops, is a bound that every net with a loop exceeds and that the simulator
enforces. If several nets meet the soundness, coverage and rule criteria, the most
precise one is selected. Generalisation is reported.

\subsection{Stop Guard}
\label{sec:exp_guard}
Beyond the domain rules, every visit has a planned number of stops $n$, a case
attribute that is known at Gate In (Section~\ref{sec:entry_known_attributes}). A
simulated truck should perform exactly that number of stops. A Petri net without
data cannot express this condition, and ProSiT does not support guards
(Section~\ref{sec:prosit_limits}). To include the condition nevertheless, the
simulation model borrows the guard of a data Petri net
(Section~\ref{sec:bg_rules}). A guard is a condition on the data of a case that
must hold before a transition may fire. The stop guard allows a transition only if
the visit can still end with exactly $n$ stops after it.

In a discovered net, the decision whether a visit continues with another stop or
goes to Gate Out is often taken by a silent transition that fires before the stop
or Gate Out becomes enabled. A guard that only acts on the visible stops and on
Gate Out would therefore act too late. The stop guard instead uses the reachability
graph of the net (Section~\ref{sec:bg_rules}). Before the simulation, it computes
for every marking and every number of stops already performed the numbers of stops
with which a visit can still end \cite{FelliDeLeoniMontali2021}. During the
simulation, it excludes every enabled transition, visible or silent, after which the
planned number can no longer be reached. Its implementation is described in
Section~\ref{sec:sim_adaptations}.

The routing trees of ProSiT can use the planned number of stops as a feature. A
tree, however, only changes the probabilities of the next step and cannot exclude a
visit with more or fewer stops than planned. The guard excludes such visits, and the
learned probabilities decide among the steps that remain.

\section{Simulation of the Discovered Nets (E3)}
\label{sec:exp_baseline}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

E3 tests whether good conformance values also lead to a usable simulation. Each
net is given to ProSiT as a marked Petri net, that is, with its initial and final
marking, exactly as it was discovered. ProSiT learns its simulation parameters on
the net and generates visits with them. E3 compares the nets by whether ProSiT can
learn and run them and by how well the generated visits reproduce the stop
structure of the observed visits, the first of the four properties that a
simulation has to reproduce (Section~\ref{sec:obs_implications}). The other three
properties depend on the arrivals and on the mechanisms at the blocks rather than
on the net and are evaluated in E4.

\subsection{Conditions}
\label{sec:exp_e3_conditions}
\label{sec:mc_hyperparameters}
\label{sec:mc_ctb_adaptations}

To ensure comparability, all nets are simulated under the same conditions. For each
net, ProSiT is fitted on the training log with the settings of
Section~\ref{sec:sim_settings} and runs with its own simulator, including its
learned block choice and its capacity check. Each fitted model simulates the 32,014
visits of the test period with five seeds. Because ProSiT learns its parameters
separately for each net, the comparison is one between complete fitted simulators.

\paragraph{Nets} The selected net of E2 is the main case. In addition, four nets
that fail a criterion are simulated in the same way. These are the two admissible
nets that break the domain rules, and one net each of the Heuristics Miner and of
the filtered Inductive Miner that fails the coverage criterion. They show whether
the conformance values anticipate what a net does in the simulator (RQ1).

\paragraph{Stop guard} Every net is simulated with the stop guard
(Section~\ref{sec:exp_guard}). For the selected net, two further variants show what
the guard changes. Without a guard, the loops of a net allow any number of stops,
so ProSiT may generate visits with more or fewer stops than planned and visits that
repeat their stops many times. A simple guard that acts only on the visible stops
and on Gate Out shows whether it suffices to apply the condition to the visible
activities.

\subsection{Measures}
\label{sec:exp_e3_measures}
The generated visits are compared with the training log, on which the nets were
discovered and ProSiT was fitted. Five measures are used.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Executability.} ProSiT can fit its parameters on the net, and every
simulated visit reaches Gate Out.
\item \textbf{Physical admissibility.} No visit without a stop and no overlapping
stops of one truck, in every seed.
\item \textbf{Planned stops.} The share of visits with exactly the planned number
of stops, and the share of visits with more.
\item \textbf{Stop structure.} The total-variation distance (TVD) between the
distributions of the sequences, that is, half the sum of the absolute differences
of their shares, and the same for the number of stops per visit. In addition, the
share of visits with an RMG stop that use two blocks, and the share of generated
visits whose sequence does not occur in training.
\item \textbf{Work of the guard.} The number of routing draws whose weights the
guard changed.
\end{enumerate}
The turnaround time is reported but judged only in E4, because it depends mainly on
the mechanisms at the blocks, which are the same for every net. The order of events
within a simulated visit is taken from the start timestamps.

\section{Mechanism Audit and Simulation Validity (E4)}
\label{sec:exp_mechanisms}
\label{sec:exp_e4_design}
% ======================================================================
% Code: trucksimulation/simulation_e3/rq2_components.py (retrained components),
%   run_rq2.py (configurations), evaluate_rq2.py (measures), ctb_engine.py (switches);
%   runs: simulation_e3/runs/hm07orig_20261115/e4 (HM 0.7 as discovered). Design notes: simulation_e3/E4_RQ2_DESIGN_20261107.md

E4 answers RQ2. Its goal is to determine how well the simulation reproduces the
turnaround time and its response to load at the yard blocks, and which parts of the
simulation model cause the remaining error. It starts from the simulation model
that E3 confirms, the selected net with the stop guard, fitted by ProSiT with the
settings of Section~\ref{sec:sim_settings}.

Following Sargent, the validity of a simulation model is judged with respect to the
purpose for which it is built \cite{Sargent2013Validation}. The model of this thesis
is built to evaluate a what-if scenario (E5) that moves trucks with delivery
containers from busy blocks to less busy ones, in order to reduce the turnaround
time in the hours above the corridor. Such a scenario can only show an effect if,
as at the terminal, trucks wait longer and are served more slowly when a block is
busy (Section~\ref{sec:observed_behaviour}). The primary goal of E4 is therefore to
establish whether the simulation reproduces this response to load, that is, whether
the queue at a block and the duration of the stops grow with the number of trucks
there. The second goal is to establish whether the simulated turnaround time has
the right level.

The evaluation follows the three levels of validation of Sargent
(Section~\ref{sec:simulation_validation_background}), namely verification
(Section~\ref{sec:exp_e4_verification}), conceptual validity
(Section~\ref{sec:exp_e4_mechanisms}) and operational validity
(Section~\ref{sec:exp_e4_operational}). Section~\ref{sec:exp_e4_configs} first
defines the configurations that are compared.

\subsection{Configurations}
\label{sec:exp_e4_configs}

A configuration specifies three parts of a simulation run.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Input.} The arrivals and case attributes, either replayed from the
observed log or generated by a learned model, and the block of each RMG stop,
either observed or chosen by a learned model.
\item \textbf{Predictive parameters.} The learned models of ProSiT, either as fitted
in E3 or retrained after a documented defect of their training data has been
corrected.
\item \textbf{Mechanisms of the simulator.} The number of lanes per block, the
moment at which the capacity is checked, the point from which the waiting time is
measured, and whether the execution time depends on the load
(Table~\ref{tab:sim_adaptations}).
\end{itemize}
Table~\ref{tab:exp_e4_configs} in Appendix~\ref{app:sim_results} lists all
configurations. Their design rests on the following decisions.

\paragraph{One fitted model} All configurations use the same net and start from the
same predictive parameters, fitted once on the training log. A configuration
changes only the parts it states and keeps everything else, so that a difference
between two configurations is caused by the changed part and not by a new fit. A
retrained parameter uses ProSiT's own feature table, discovery functions,
hyperparameters and random state, and only the stated change of the training data
differs.

\paragraph{Conditional replay} A comparison of a simulated log with the log of a
later period mixes the error of the model with the change of the process between
the periods \cite{Oezdemir2025RethinkingBPS}. Most configurations therefore replay
the observed Gate-In times and case attributes of the test period. The simulated
trucks are then the observed ones, and the change of demand between the periods is
not part of the error. The final configuration is also run on the training period,
where no change between periods is involved.

\paragraph{Observed blocks} The share of each block in the RMG stops changes from
week to week, so no model learned on the training period can reproduce the block
loads of the test period. To evaluate the queue and the service at a block, the
observed block of each RMG stop is used where the simulated stop has an observed
counterpart in the same visit, and the training shares otherwise. The error of the
block choice itself is measured separately (E4b).

\paragraph{Three lanes} A block has three handover lanes and holds at most three
trucks at once. The crane handles the trucks in the lanes one after another, so a
recorded stop contains the waiting for the crane
(Section~\ref{sec:timestamp_semantics}). ProSiT would instead give each block a capacity equal to the largest number of
simultaneous stops observed in the log plus one, which is four or five at the blocks
of CTB (Table~\ref{tab:sim_adaptations}). The log contains RMG stops that start
while three other stops at the same block are still in progress (5.6\,\% of the RMG
stops in training, 8.1\,\% in the test period). For these stops, the first of the
other three ends after a median of 3 minutes, and in 68 to 73\,\% of the cases
within 5 minutes. A plausible reading is that the fourth truck is already
registered at the block while it waits for a lane, so that its recorded stop
includes waiting. Delayed completion records would look the same, and the data
cannot distinguish the two. The simulation model therefore replaces the learned
capacity by the physical limit of three lanes. Four lanes are run only as a
sensitivity analysis and are not admissible. For the same reason, the load
profiles are compared only for the load states that three lanes allow, namely up to
two other trucks at the start of a stop and up to three trucks at the block at Gate
In.

\paragraph{Exact counting} ProSiT counts the activities in progress at a resource
by walking backwards through its schedule and stops at the first activity that has
already ended. This count is exact only if the schedule is sorted by end time. The
simulator stores activities in the order in which it processes them, so some counts
are too low, while the counts in training are exact. The verification measures how
often this happens. The baseline and all configurations from E4b on are also run
with an exact count, marked by the suffix x, and the final configuration is chosen
among these runs.

\paragraph{Unchanged parts} Routing, stop guard, case attributes, calendars and the
exit after the last stop are the same in every configuration. The exit does not
depend on the load (12.4 minutes on average in both periods).

\paragraph{Runs} Every configuration is run with five seeds (42--46) on the 32,014
visits of the test period. The runs on the training period simulate its 59,139
visits. A configuration whose queue grows without bound is simulated only for the
first day of the test period (2,393 visits) and compared with the observed visits
of that day.

\subsection{Verification}
\label{sec:exp_e4_verification}

Verification tests whether the extended simulator does what it is designed to do.
Five tests were fixed in advance.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The extensions of the simulation model are switches in ProSiT's simulator
(Section~\ref{sec:sim_adaptations}). With all switches off, the extended simulator
must generate exactly the same log as ProSiT 1.0.4 for the same seed. This shows
that the extensions change nothing beyond what they are meant to change.
\item A predictive parameter retrained on unchanged training data must be identical
to the fitted one. This shows that the retraining itself causes no difference.
\item The replayed arrivals must equal the observed ones.
\item The generated logs must respect the domain rules and the capacity of three
lanes.
\item The capacity check is the queueing mechanism of the simulator. A stop that
finds all lanes of its block busy has to wait until a lane becomes free. The
capacity check and the workload features both rely on the number of activities in
progress at a block. This number is recomputed from the generated log and compared
with the value that the simulator used.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Conceptual Validity}
\label{sec:exp_e4_mechanisms}

Conceptual validity concerns whether the assumptions of each part of the model are
plausible for the terminal. It is assessed in two ways. First, every learned model
is inspected, namely the features on which its tree splits, the values in its
leaves and the quantity it is trained to predict. Second, every mechanism of the
simulator along the path of a truck, from the demand and the block allocation to
the capacity, the waiting and the service, is compared with the physical process at
the terminal (Table~\ref{tab:sim_adaptations}). Two modelling decisions follow from
this comparison.

\paragraph{Where the queue comes from} A queue can arise in the simulation, when a
stop has to wait for a free lane, or it can be part of the learned waiting time.
Using both would count the same waiting twice. Two consistent combinations are
therefore compared. The first measures the learned waiting from the moment a lane
became free and lets the capacity check, applied at the start of a stop, create the
queue. The second learns the whole delay before the stop and has no capacity limit,
which treats waiting as a delay that the model learns from data
\cite{RWChapela2024Delays}.

\paragraph{Load in the service time} At the terminal, part of the time at a block
is spent waiting for the crane, which serves several trucks. The duration of a stop
therefore grows with the number of trucks at the block. ProSiT offers no load
features for execution times. The extension adds one feature, the number of other
stops in progress at the block when the stop starts. In training it is counted at
the recorded start. In the simulation it is counted when the stop is scheduled,
before the capacity check can move the stop to a later start, and it is at most the
number of lanes minus one.

\subsection{Operational Validity}
\label{sec:exp_e4_operational}
\label{sec:exp_e4_attribution}
\label{sec:exp_e4_runs}

Operational validity concerns whether the output of the simulation is accurate
enough for E5. Generated logs are compared with the observed log from four
perspectives, each described by several measures (Table~\ref{tab:exp_e4_measures}
in Appendix~\ref{app:sim_results}).
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{P\arabic*}]
\item \textbf{Turnaround time.} The distribution of the TTT, summarised by its
median, P90 and mean, and the cycle-time distance (CTD) between the simulated and
the observed distribution.
\item \textbf{Where the time is spent.} The approach to the first stop, the stops,
the transfer between stops and the exit. Their means add up to the mean TTT, so the
error of the mean TTT splits exactly into the errors of the four parts.
\item \textbf{Physical constraints.} The number of trucks in the terminal and at
each block, the concentration of the RMG stops on single blocks, and the violations
of the domain rules.
\item \textbf{Response to load.} The Spearman correlation of the hourly arrivals and
the hourly median TTT, and three load profiles. These are the median TTT by the
number of trucks in the terminal, the median approach to a first RMG stop by the
number of trucks at that block at Gate In, and the median duration of an RMG stop
by the number of other trucks at the block at its start. For each profile, the
profile gap is the mean absolute difference between the simulated and the observed
medians over the load levels.
\end{enumerate}
Because a single distance between two distributions can hide temporal patterns
\cite{Oezdemir2025RethinkingBPS}, the medians, quantiles and load profiles are
reported next to the CTD. Two standard measures of BPS, the 3-gram distance of the
activity sequences and the case-arrival distance, make the results comparable with
the evaluation of ProSiT \cite{Vinci2026prosit}.

\paragraph{Reference values} A distance between a simulated and an observed log is
interpreted against the distance between two real periods. Between the three weeks
of the test period, the CTD is 1.7 to 3.1 minutes, and between the training and the
test period 5.5 minutes. The load profiles differ much less between real periods,
by at most 2.0 minutes for the approach and 1.0 minute for the stop duration. These
differences define the thresholds of the acceptance criteria below.

\paragraph{Error along the path of the truck} A truck meets the parts of the
simulator in a fixed order, namely the demand, the block allocation, the queue at
the block and the service, followed by the exit. The runs follow this order in four
steps, E4a to E4d. Each step replaces one part and measures how much of the error
disappears. A part is replaced in one of two ways. Either it is set to the observed
values of the test period, for example the replayed arrivals or the observed block
of each RMG stop, which is known as trace-driven simulation
\cite{Law2015Simulation}. Or it stays learned, but a documented defect of its
training data or of the simulator is corrected. Each step keeps the parts before it
at their observed values, so that only one part changes at a time. Alternatives that
keep a part generative, for example a learned instead of the observed block, are
reported next to this sequence.

\paragraph{Choice of the final configuration} As in E1 and E2, the choice uses
training data only. The admissible configurations of E4c and E4d with exact
counting are run on the replayed training visits with their observed blocks and
compared with the training log. A configuration is admissible if it produces no
rule violation and never more than three trucks at a block at once. Among the
admissible configurations, the one with the smallest sum of the three profile gaps
of P4 is chosen, and ties are broken by the CTD. The response to load is ranked
first, because E5 depends on it and because it is stable between periods. This rule
was fixed before the training runs were evaluated. Configurations with four lanes
or without a capacity limit exceed the physical limit and are run only as
sensitivity analyses on the test period. After the choice, each change of the final
configuration is reversed alone, which shows whether every change is needed.

\paragraph{Acceptance criteria} The criteria for the intended use were fixed before
the final configuration was run, with thresholds taken from the differences between
real periods.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Physical admissibility.} No rule violations, and at most three trucks
at a block at once.
\item \textbf{Response to load.} A Spearman correlation of hourly arrivals and
median TTT of at least 0.49, the lowest weekly value of the test period. Profile
gaps of at most 2.0 minutes for the approach, 1.0 minute for the stop duration and
5.75 minutes for the TTT by trucks in the terminal, the largest gaps between real
weeks.
\item \textbf{Extreme condition.} The TTT rises when the arrival intensity rises
from 0.8 to 1.0 and 1.2.
\item \textbf{Level.} A CTD of at most 3.1 minutes, within the variation between
weeks, or at most 5.5 minutes, within the change between periods. Reported for the
conditional and the fully generative configuration.
\end{enumerate}
E5 can give a quantitative estimate only if criteria 1 to 3 hold for the final
configuration under replay. Otherwise E5 is run as a what-if demonstration
(Section~\ref{sec:exp_policy}). Criterion 4 states how far the absolute values can
be trusted. The thresholds make the decision reproducible. They do not prove that a
model which meets them is accurate enough for the allocation decision, and a
rising TTT under more arrivals (criterion 3) is a plausibility test rather than
evidence of correct behaviour under an intervention.

\section{Load-Aware Block Allocation (E5)}
\label{sec:exp_policy}
% ======================================================================
% Code: trucksimulation/simulation_e3/run_rq2.py (s1 settings), ctb_engine.py (switch s1, availability),
%   evaluate_e5.py, evaluate_e5_kpi.py, runs/hm07orig_20261115/e5_hourly_kpi.py, e5_grid_hourly.py,
%   make_rq3_grid_tables.py; run scripts run_availability.sh, run_demand_grid.sh, run_exec_final.sh,
%   run_full_availability.sh.

E5 answers RQ3. It evaluates a redesign of the allocation of deliveries to the yard
blocks that aims to reduce the hours above the corridor. Its primary goal is to
determine whether assigning alternative blocks to RMG deliveries lowers the share of
hours whose mean turnaround time lies above 35 minutes. How far the result can
support an operational recommendation depends on E4. If the final configuration of
E4 meets acceptance criteria 1 to 3, E5 estimates the size of the effect. Otherwise
E5 is a what-if demonstration. It then states the direction of an effect and the
mechanism behind it, and only if both are robust to the weaknesses found in E4.
Effect sizes are then values of the model, not estimates for the terminal.

\paragraph{Starting point}
The observed waiting before the first RMG stop grows from 6 to 16 minutes when
three trucks are already at the block, while other blocks often have free lanes
(Section~\ref{sec:observed_behaviour}). The destination block of a truck delivery
is assigned by yard planning, whereas a receive must go to the block where its
container is. When a truck arrives at Gate In with a delivery container for the
storage blocks, the yard planning system decides on a specific block. If the target
vessel and its berth are known, it chooses a block near the berth. Otherwise the
container goes to the centre of the yard, usually to block T13 by default
(M.~Petersen, project manager for the cross-terminal KPI dashboard, data warehouse
and business intelligence, HHLA, personal communication, 12 November 2026). The
assignment does not consider the demand that the block will be facing.

\paragraph{The allocation rule}
The scenario changes the default block allocation at the moment a truck passes Gate
In. It moves only RMG deliveries, because for a delivery the blocks are
interchangeable, whereas a receive or a mixed stop needs the block where its
container is and keeps its observed block. Because the terminal assigns the yard
position at Gate In, the rule can move only a delivery that is the first stop of its
visit. A delivery later in the visit keeps its block. At Gate In, the simulator
counts the committed load of the planned block, which is the number of stops in
progress there plus the stops already scheduled to start there. If this number is
three or more, the block counts as full, and the delivery is moved to the available
block with the lowest committed load below three. Ties go to the nearest block. A
block counts as available unless the yard utilisation data show it as empty, with a
utilisation below 5\,\%, which marks the blocks that were closed for maintenance
(Section~\ref{sec:obs_drift}). If no block qualifies, the delivery stays at its
block. The rule is deterministic. It chooses the new block only from the committed
loads and the distances and draws no random numbers.

\paragraph{Scenarios}
The rule is evaluated in six scenarios that combine two factors.
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt,topsep=4pt]
\item \textbf{Eligible blocks.} A delivery may move to a block within one block
number of its observed block ($\pm 1$), within two ($\pm 2$), or to any block.
Within one or two block numbers, the later retrieval towards the berth stays
short. Any block is the upper bound and shows how much a completely free choice
would add to a rule restricted to neighbouring blocks.
\item \textbf{Demand.} The arrivals of the test period are replayed as observed,
or compressed to an intensity of 1.2, which is equivalent to 20\,\% more trucks
within the same time period. The higher demand stands for growth and for busy
periods, and it tests the rule under stronger congestion.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Baseline}
Each scenario is compared with a baseline run that has the same demand and keeps
the observed blocks. The scenarios with 20\,\% more trucks are thus compared with a
baseline that also has 20\,\% more trucks. The baseline is the final configuration
of E4 on the test period. It uses the same net and predictive parameters as E4, the
replayed arrivals, case attributes and observed blocks as input, and the mechanisms
chosen in E4. In the baseline, the rule only records the deliveries it would move,
without moving them. As a verification, the baseline with replayed demand must
generate the same log as the final configuration of E4 for the same seed. Baselines
and scenarios use the same ten seeds (42--51). Equal seeds give the same random
numbers only until the first moved stop, so all differences are computed per seed
and then averaged.

\paragraph{Primary measure}
The rule is judged by its effect on the upper bound of the corridor
(Section~\ref{sec:exp_kpis}). The primary measure is the change of the share of
hours whose mean TTT lies above 35 minutes, together with the change of the minutes
above 35 per hour. Each change is the difference between the scenario and its
baseline for the same seed, averaged over the ten seeds and reported with a
95\,\% $t$-interval. A simulated difference is a property of the model and is
never added to an observed value.

\paragraph{Further measures}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Corridor and turnaround time.} The changes of the shares of hours in
and below the corridor, the same shares per visit, and the changes of the mean,
median and P90 of the TTT of all visits and of the visits with a moved delivery.
\item \textbf{Mechanism.} The time from Gate In to the first RMG stop (the
approach), the share of RMG stops that start while two other trucks are already at
the block, so that the block is full, and the waiting for a free lane per RMG stop.
\item \textbf{Distribution.} The change of the TTT per visit in four groups, namely
the visits with a moved delivery, the other visits at the blocks that gave
deliveries away, the visits at the blocks that received them, and all other visits.
\item \textbf{Costs and physical limits.} The share of RMG deliveries moved, the
distance in block numbers, the largest number of trucks at a block and the rule
violations. Driving distance, later rehandling and the constraints of the yard plan
are not modelled.
\end{itemize}
The share of delay-peak hours is reported as well.

\paragraph{Robustness}
To test whether the result depends on the weaknesses found in E4, the scenario with
$\pm 2$ and replayed demand is repeated under four changes of the model. Each change
is applied to the scenario and to its baseline, so that the scenario is always
compared with a baseline that contains the same change.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item \textbf{Service without load.} The execution time does not depend on the
load. This is the lower bound of the slowdown at a busy block.
\item \textbf{Stronger slowdown.} The service model sees one more truck whenever at
least one other truck is at the block.
\item \textbf{More demand.} The arrivals are compressed to an intensity of 1.2.
This run is identical to the scenario $\pm 2$ with 20\,\% more trucks.
\item \textbf{Fully generative.} Arrivals by hour of the week and blocks drawn from
the training shares replace the replayed arrivals and the observed blocks.
\end{enumerate}
A further run lifts the two restrictions of the rule. The rule may then move a
delivery whenever it becomes possible and may use every block, including the closed
ones. This run shows how much the restrictions change the effect.

\paragraph{Rules for the claims}
The following rules were fixed before the runs of E5.
\begin{enumerate}
\item A direction is stated only if the 95\,\% interval of the paired difference
for the visits with a moved delivery excludes zero in the three scenarios with
replayed demand, and if the sign is the same in all four robustness runs. If the
sign changes, the finding is that the simulation is not adequate for this question.
\item A mechanism is stated only if the rule relieves the full blocks as intended,
that is, if the trucks reach their first RMG stop sooner and fewer RMG stops start
at a full block.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Interpretation of the variants} An effect already at $\pm 1$ or $\pm 2$
means that a local rule is enough in the model. An effect only with any block means
that it needs unlimited flexibility. No effect means that the model shows no lever.
Better affected visits but worse other visits mean a shift of waiting, not a
reduction.

\section{Threats Addressed by the Design}
\label{sec:exp_threats}
% ======================================================================

The design responds to the main threats to the conclusions. Physically impossible
visits are prevented at two points and detected at a third. The rule audit admits
only nets whose language respects the sequence rules, the simulator enforces the
planned number of stops and the three lanes of a block, and every generated log is
tested for violations of the domain rules. Leakage is limited, because only the
attributes of the planned visit are used, which are known at Gate In. Whether a
schedule was changed after Gate In is not recorded
(Section~\ref{sec:disc_threats}). Every selection uses the training log, and its
criteria were fixed before the runs they decide. The change between the periods is
addressed by comparing variants on the same period and by running the final
configuration on the training period as well. The waiting at a block is counted
only once. Either the simulator creates it, when a stop waits for a free lane, or
the learned waiting time contains it, but no configuration uses both. Five or ten
seeds show the random variation. Section~\ref{sec:disc_threats} discusses what
remains of these threats.

\section{Chapter Summary}

E1 and E2 select the control-flow model with criteria fixed on the training log,
namely soundness, acceptance of every frequent sequence and the domain rules. The
most precise admissible net is used as discovered, with a stop guard for the
planned number of stops. E3 shows whether ProSiT can learn and run the discovered
nets and how well they reproduce the observed stop structure. E4 assesses the
simulation on the three levels of Sargent, attributes the remaining error to the
parts of the simulator that a truck meets, chooses the final configuration on the
training period and tests it against acceptance criteria fixed in advance. E5
applies one block allocation rule to this model in six scenarios of flexibility and
demand and states only what the rules for the claims allow.
