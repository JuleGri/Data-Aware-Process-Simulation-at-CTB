\begin{itemize}

    \item receiving an import container,

An efficient pattern is the \textit{multi move}. A truck delivers one or more

\subsubsection{Terminal Operating Systems}
Container terminals use several information systems to coordinate operations.
The Order Management and Terminal Control System (TCS) manages container
movements, storage locations, and operational orders. It is connected to other
systems, including a Gate Operating System (GOS), yard management systems,
equipment control systems, and appointment platforms. Each system records a
different part of the operation. Gate systems record arrivals and departures,
while yard systems record container movements and storage assignments.
Equipment control systems record the execution of handling orders. A complete
truck visit must be reconstructed from several data sources. This
thesis mainly uses the Order and Terminal Control System and adds data from
other systems to build and check the event log. Chapter~\ref{chp:experiments_and_framework}
describes this data integration.

\subsubsection{Sources of Operational Delays} 
Truck turnaround time depends on several factors. Common reasons for delay
include:

\begin{itemize} 
    \item congestion at entry and exit gates, including effects from road
    traffic outside the terminal;
    \item pick-up containers that are blocked by other containers after a long
    dwell time;
    \item truck traffic congestion within container yard sections and main routes,
    \item limited availability of container handling equipment (CHE), 
    \item high occupancy levels within storage blocks, 
    \item resource and priority conflicts between truck and vessel operations, 
    \item unexpected equipment failures or maintenance activities 
\end{itemize} 

These factors are connected, so a local disruption can affect other terminal
operations. For example, low yard-crane availability or vessel priority can
increase truck service and waiting times. Longer queues can then cause yard
traffic, reduce gate throughput, and slow hinterland operations
\cite{CTSteenken2004,Bett2024}.

\subsubsection{Operational Performance Indicators} 
Truck operations are evaluated with operational key performance indicators
(KPIs).
\begin{itemize} 
    \item \textbf{Truck Turnaround Time (TTT):} Total time a truck spends inside the terminal between entry and exit. 
    \item \textbf{Waiting Time:} Time spent waiting for gate processing, yard equipment, or storage area access.  
    \item \textbf{Yard Utilization:} Degree to which storage areas are occupied and operational resources are utilized.
\end{itemize} 

Among these indicators, Truck Turnaround Time is often the main measure of
truck service quality because it reflects the time experienced by trucking
companies and cargo owners. It is one of four main KPIs used by the HHLA
container terminals \cite{Bett2024,CTprocessMiningTAS2020}.

\subsection{Simulation-Based Decision Support in Container Terminals} 
\textit{This is now trying to explain Why is there a need for better decision support in container terminals in general, why is simulation a common approach for that purpose, and why are traditional simulation approaches insufficient?}

Terminal managers make decisions about truck appointments, resources,
equipment schedules, maintenance, and yard operations while the terminal
remains active. Because terminal processes are connected, local decisions can
change waiting times, resource use, throughput, and service quality.
Even experienced terminal managers face uncertainty. Changes to procedures or
capacity can create congestion, increase handling times, or reduce service
quality. Simulation allows managers and researchers to evaluate such changes
without affecting live operations. Common applications include Truck
Appointment Systems (TAS), equipment allocation, resource planning, yard
management, and storage strategies. Simulation is useful for congestion,
bottlenecks, and trade-offs that are difficult to analyse directly. Studies
have shown its value for truck turnaround time, resource use, and terminal
performance \cite{Kastner2021,Bett2024,Carboni2025}. Practical use is still
limited by model complexity, costs, and missing technical expertise in
terminal teams.

\begin{sloppypar}
Different simulation approaches are used in terminal research. Discrete Event
Simulation (DES) is the most common because terminal operations can be modelled
as events such as truck arrivals, gate processing, container handling, and
equipment use. System Dynamics, Agent-Based Modelling, and traffic simulation
are used for other operational and strategic questions
\cite{Muravev2019,Muravev2021,Carboni2025}. Traditional simulation projects
have limits. Building a model requires domain knowledge, process
understanding, and much manual work. Data collection, implementation,
calibration, and validation take time. Manual models depend on expert
assumptions, which may describe the intended process rather than its actual
execution \cite{PMRozinat2009}.
\end{sloppypar}

These differences can reduce the quality of simulation results. Manual models
must be maintained and calibrated when processes, workloads, and operating
rules change. This work is costly and requires technical support. At the same
time, terminal information systems record large amounts of operational data.
Process Mining can use these data to discover, analyse, and monitor processes
from their recorded execution. This may reduce manual modelling work and make
simulation models closer to the observed process.

Most business processes are supported by information systems. These systems

Process Mining describes a process as a sequence of recorded events. The
following terms define this event-log structure.

\paragraph{Event}
An event records an activity at a point in time. Examples from a container
terminal include:
\begin{itemize} 
    \item Truck drives through OCR gate, 
    \item Gate Check completed, 
    \item Truck arrives at yard position, 
    \item Container operation at yard position completed, 
    \item Truck exits the terminal. 
\end{itemize} 

\paragraph{Activity} 
An activity describes the type of action that is being executed. Multiple events can belong to the same activity while occurring at different times or for different process instances. Examples of activities are: 

\begin{itemize} 
    \item Gate In, 
    \item Container-Delivery,
    \item Container-Receive, 
    \item Gate Out. 
\end{itemize} 

\paragraph{Case} 
A case represents a single process instance. It is defined by the object whose process execution is being analyzed. Depending on the application domain, a case can represent different entities, such as: 

\begin{itemize} 
    \item a customer order, 
    \item a container,  
    \item a truck visit. 
\end{itemize} 

In this thesis, one truck visit is one case.

In traditional event logs, events are often represented by a single timestamp, typically indicating the completion of an activity. For performance-oriented Process Mining and simulation applications, richer temporal information is required. Two directly observable lifecycle states are commonly distinguished:

\begin{itemize} 
    \item \textbf{Start Timestamp}: The moment execution of the activity actually begins. 
    \item \textbf{Complete Timestamp}: The moment execution of the activity finishes. 
\end{itemize} 

The duration between these two timestamps is the \textit{service time} (or execution time) of the activity. A third temporal concept, \textit{enablement}, describes the moment at which an activity becomes executable according to the process-model state. In process-mining-based simulation approaches, enablement is typically not an observed input-log attribute but is derived by replaying the event log against the discovered process model \cite{PMRozinat2009,Camargo2020,Vinci2026prosit}. The interval between model-derived enablement and the observed start timestamp is referred to as \textit{waiting time} in the ProSiT framework \cite{Vinci2026prosit} and corresponds to a pre-service delay that may include resource contention, queueing effects, and — in domains with spatial separation between activities — physical movement time. 

\paragraph{Attributes}
Events may contain attributes that describe the process context. Examples from
container terminal operations include:

\begin{itemize} 
    \item Truck ID,
    \item Container Number, 
    \item Shipping Line, 
    \item Yard Block, 
    \item Container Type, 
    \item Equipment Identifier, 
    \item Trucking Company. 
\end{itemize} 

Data-aware Process Mining and simulation methods can use these attributes as
model inputs.
\paragraph{Trace}
A trace is the chronologically ordered sequence of all events belonging to a specific case. For a truck visit, a trace may appear as follows: 

\begin{quote} OCR Gate Entry $\rightarrow$ Gate In $\rightarrow$ Yard Arrival $\rightarrow$ Container Delivery $\rightarrow$ Container Pick-Up $\rightarrow$ Gate Out 
\end{quote} 

A trace describes the execution path of one process instance.

\subsection{Event Logs} 
An event log groups events into cases and orders them by timestamp. Each event
requires at least three attributes:
\begin{itemize} 
\item Case Identifier, 
\item Activity,

For simulation-oriented process mining, richer event information is required:
\begin{itemize} 
\item Start Timestamp,
\item Complete Timestamp,

\section{Process Mining Foundations}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Definitions} 
Companies record growing amounts of operational data through systems such as
Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP), Customer Relationship Management (CRM),
and Workflow Management Systems (WfMS) [SOURCE]. Business Intelligence, Data
Mining, and Process Mining analyse these data in different ways. Business
Intelligence (BI) uses dashboards, reports, and key performance indicators
(KPIs) to describe past and current performance
\cite{PMvanDerAalst2016}.
\begin{sloppypar}
Data Mining identifies patterns, correlations, classifications, and predictive
relationships in datasets. Traditional methods often treat observations as
independent records and ignore the order of events
\cite{PMvanDerAalst2016}. Process Mining connects process models with recorded
event data. It uses event sequences to discover and monitor actual process
behaviour and to identify bottlenecks or deviations
\cite{PMRozinat2009,PMvanDerAalst2016}.
\end{sloppypar}


\subsubsection{Process Perspectives} Process Mining studies a process from four
perspectives \cite{PMRozinat2009}.

\paragraph{Control-Flow Perspective} 
The control-flow perspective describes the ordering relations between activities and captures how process instances progress through a process. It focuses on questions such as which activities occur, in which order, and which alternative execution paths exist. 
\paragraph{Time Perspective} 
The time perspective focuses on temporal process characteristics such as activity durations, waiting times, throughput times, and bottlenecks. The analysis of timestamps contained in event logs enables the identification of performance issues and delays. 
\paragraph{Resource Perspective} 
The resource perspective provides the organizational dimension of a process. It considers which resources execute certain activities, how work is distributed among employees or other resource types, and how organizational structures influence process behavior. 
\paragraph{Data Perspective}
The data perspective analyzes process-related attributes and case characteristics. Depending on the type of case, attributes can include customer types, object categories or risk classes. Such attributes often influence process decisions and execution paths \cite{PMRozinat2009}. 

\begin{sloppypar}
Together, these perspectives describe the process structure, timing, resources,
and data.
\end{sloppypar}

\subsubsection{Process Discovery}
The objective of process discovery is to construct a process model from an
event log without a predefined process model \cite{PMvanDerAalst2016}. The
output should reproduce the observed behaviour. Depending on the discovery
algorithm, it can be a Petri net, process tree, BPMN model, or directly-follows
graph \cite{PMRozinat2009}. Three common algorithms are [SOURCES]:

\begin{itemize} 
\item \textbf{Alpha Miner}: One of the earliest process discovery algorithms, capable of identifying causal dependencies and parallel behaviour from event logs. 
\item \textbf{Heuristic Miner}: An extension of Alpha Miner that incorporates frequency information and is more robust towards noise and infrequent behaviour. 
\item \textbf{Inductive Miner}: Recursively decomposes event logs using
sequence, exclusive-choice, parallel, and loop cuts. It produces a sound
block-structured model and can filter infrequent behaviour
\cite{Leemans2014InductiveMiner}.
\end{itemize} 

Process discovery shows how a recorded process was executed, rather than how it
was expected to operate \cite{PMvanDerAalst2016}.

\paragraph{Prefix trees and the observed trace language.}
A prefix tree, or \emph{trie}, is a rooted tree representation of a set
of sequences \cite{DeLaBriandais1959Trie}. Let an event log
$L$ be a multiset of traces over activities $\mathcal{A}$. The trie has
one node for every observed prefix
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{Pref}(L)=\{\epsilon\}\cup
\{\langle a_1,\ldots,a_k\rangle \mid
\exists\langle a_1,\ldots,a_n\rangle\in L,\;1\leq k\leq n\},
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon$ denotes the empty prefix. An edge labelled $a$ connects
prefix $p$ to $p\cdot\langle a\rangle$ whenever that continuation occurs
in the log. Traces with the same history share one path up
to their first different activity; nodes and edges may be
annotated with occurrence frequencies or data distributions. PRETSA
uses precisely this property in process mining: each node groups events
of the same activity whose traces share the same activity prefix
\cite{FahrenkrogPetersen2019PRETSA}.

Without pruning or smoothing, a prefix trie accepts exactly the observed
sequence variants. It maximises replay fitness and precision
on its construction log, but it does not infer unseen combinations,
loops or concurrency. An Inductive-Miner model makes the opposite
trade-off: its block-structured cuts abstract from individual
variants and can admit behaviour not present verbatim in the log
\cite{Leemans2014InductiveMiner}. Neither representation is always
superior. The appropriate choice depends on whether the application
requires controlled generalisation or a strict, auditable trace language.

\subsubsection{Conformance Checking}
While process discovery focuses on constructing process models, conformance checking focuses on comparing observed process executions with an existing process model \cite{aalst2012ConformanceChecking}. The objective is to identify deviations between the discovered model and the observed reality. Resulting deviations can indicate compliance violations, process inefficiencies, exceptional cases, or outdated process documentation. The quality of discovered process models is commonly evaluated using four criterias \cite{aalst2012ConformanceChecking,PMvanDerAalst2016}: 

\begin{itemize} \item \textbf{Fitness}: 
Measures how well the process model can reproduce the observed behavior in the event log. A model with high fitness allows most observed traces to be replayed without deviations.
\item \textbf{Precision}:
Measures how much additional behavior the model allows beyond the behavior observed in the event log. A precise model avoids allowing many unrealistic or unobserved execution paths. 
\item \textbf{Generalization}: 
Measures whether the model captures the underlying process behaviour instead
of only reproducing the traces in the event log. A model should allow
plausible behaviour that was not observed in the available sample.
\item \textbf{Simplicity}: 
Measures the complexity of the process model. If several models describe the
observed behaviour well enough, the simpler model is usually preferred because
it is easier to interpret and analyse.
\end{itemize}

These criteria provide a balanced framework for evaluating process model quality and form the basis of many process-mining evaluation approaches. 

\paragraph{A*-based optimal alignment.}
\label{sec:astar_alignment_definition}
An alignment relates an observed trace to an executable run of a process
model. Formally, it is a sequence of three move types: a
\emph{synchronous move} consumes an event and fires a transition with the
same visible label; a \emph{log move} consumes an event without a
corresponding model transition; and a \emph{model move} fires a model
transition without consuming an observed event. A cost function normally
assigns zero cost to synchronous moves and positive, configurable costs
to deviations. An optimal alignment $\lambda^{\star}$ is
\begin{equation}
\lambda^{\star}=\arg\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda(\sigma,N)} c(\lambda),
\end{equation}
where $\Lambda(\sigma,N)$ is the set of legal alignments between trace
$\sigma$ and process model $N$ \cite{aalst2012ConformanceChecking,
Adriansyah2011CostFitness}.

The qualifier ``A*-based'' refers to the search procedure, not to a
different kind of alignment. A* explores the product state space of the
current trace position and the current model state (for a Petri net, its
marking). It orders candidates by $f=g+h$, where $g$ is accumulated move
cost and $h$ is an admissible lower bound on the remaining cost. With an
admissible heuristic, the first complete solution is cost-optimal
\cite{Adriansyah2011CostFitness}. The resulting event--transition
correspondence supports conformance diagnostics and timestamp replay for
performance analysis \cite{aalst2012ConformanceChecking}; it does not
turn a model-derived enablement instant into an independently observed
timestamp.

\subsubsection{Process Enhancement} Process Mining includes process discovery,
conformance checking, and process enhancement \cite{PMvanDerAalst2016}.
Discovery creates a process model from an event log. Conformance checking
compares observed behaviour with a model. Process enhancement adds information
from event data to an existing model. Examples include waiting times, activity
durations, resource assignments, bottlenecks, and workload. This helps users
understand the process and find possible improvements.

\section{Business Process Simulation} %%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%% 

\subsection{Fundamentals of Business Process Simulation} 
Business Process Simulation (BPS) uses executable models to study process
behaviour in a virtual environment. Organisations can run these models under
controlled conditions without disturbing live operations
\cite{BPSAalst2015}. Simulation can estimate throughput times, waiting times,
resource use, service levels, and costs before a change is implemented. It is
often used for \textit{what-if analysis}. Examples include changing the number
of resources, routing rules, workload, or operational policies. The results of
different scenarios can then be compared \cite{BPSAalst2015}. BPS is used in
manufacturing, healthcare, logistics, supply chains, and business-process
management. Process Mining allows such models to be discovered and
calibrated from recorded process executions
\cite{PMRozinat2009,Camargo2020}.

\subsection{Business Process Simulation Models}
\label{sec:bps_models}
A simulation model is a simplified representation of a process. Most Business
Process Simulation models describe the following parts
\cite{BPSAalst2015,PMRozinat2009}.

\subsubsection*{Control Flow} 
Control flow defines which activities exist, their possible order, and the
alternative execution paths. It can be represented by Petri nets, BPMN models,
process trees, or workflow graphs \cite{PMvanDerAalst2016,BPSAalst2015}.

\subsubsection*{Arrival Process} 
The arrival process defines how new cases enter the system. Examples are
customer requests, orders, applications, and service tickets. Statistical
distributions often model the time between arrivals. Arrival volumes affect
waiting times and resource use \cite{BPSAalst2015,PMRozinat2009}.

\subsubsection*{Resources} 
Resources execute process activities. They can be employees, machines,
vehicles, cranes, or other equipment. Resource models describe availability,
capacity, capabilities, schedules, and allocation rules. Limited resources can
create competition between cases \cite{BPSAalst2015}.

\subsubsection*{Activity Durations} 
The timing perspective describes processing times, waiting times, and case
durations. Probability distributions fitted to historical observations can
represent these durations \cite{BPSAalst2015,PMRozinat2009}.

\subsubsection*{Decision Logic} 
Business processes contain decision points with alternative paths. A model's
decision logic determines which path is selected during simulation. Traditional
models use static branching probabilities. Data-aware approaches can instead
condition the decision on context, process attributes, or the current
operational state \cite{PMRozinat2009,mannhardt2023stochasticProcessesModelling}.
\begin{sloppypar}
Together, control flow, arrivals, resources, timing, and decision logic define
the simulation model. Both manual modelling and simulation discovery use these
parts.
\end{sloppypar}


\subsection{Simulation-Based Decision Support} 
Container terminal demand changes over time, resource capacity is limited, and
operational processes depend on each other. Managers make decisions about
equipment, workforce, storage, truck appointments, and capacity while the
terminal remains active. Tests in the live terminal can affect service quality
and throughput. Simulation allows alternatives to be assessed before they are
implemented \cite{Bruzzone2012,Kastner2021,Carboni2025}. Applications include:

\begin{itemize} 
    \item evaluation of truck appointment policies, 
    \item resource and workforce planning, 
    \item capacity planning and investment decisions, 
    \item yard layout and storage strategies, 
    \item yard crane allocation policies, 
    \item congestion analysis and bottleneck identification, 
    \item equipment scheduling and maintenance planning. 
\end{itemize} 

Simulation supports what-if analysis by comparing scenarios under the same
conditions. Measures can include truck turnaround time, resource use,
throughput, waiting time, and service levels. This is useful in container
terminals because a change in gate, yard, or vessel operations can affect other
areas \cite{CTSteenken2004,Bruzzone2012,Kastner2021}. Truck-operation studies
have assessed Truck Appointment Systems (TAS), gate capacity, yard use, and
equipment allocation \cite{Bett2024,CTprocessMiningTAS2020}.

\subsection{Simulation Model Calibration}
Calibration estimates or adjusts simulation parameters to describe the
observed operating conditions. Typical tasks
include fitting arrival and duration distributions, estimating resource
capacities, and learning routing probabilities or context-dependent
decision rules from historical observations
\cite{PMRozinat2009,Camargo2020}. In simulation discovery, many of these
steps form part of the automated discovery procedure itself. Calibration
must still be distinguished from validation: agreement with the
data used to fit a model shows in-sample fit, whereas an
independent assessment requires data that did not determine those
parameters. Section~\ref{sec:simulation_validation_background} develops
the corresponding verification and validation concepts.


%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Simulation Discovery and Data-Aware Simulation} 
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

The previous sections introduced Business Process Simulation and event logs.
Simulation discovery connects both by estimating simulation behaviour from
recorded process executions. The estimated parameters are integrated into an
executable process model \cite{PMRozinat2009,Camargo2020,simod2024}. Research
has moved from basic stochastic parameters towards context-dependent and
data-aware behaviour.

\subsection{From Simulation Discovery to Data-Driven Simulation} 

Rozinat et al. proposed an early approach for deriving simulation models from
event logs \cite{PMRozinat2009}. It extracts control flow, timing, resource
assignments, arrivals, and routing probabilities from historical executions.

Later research extended this idea. Solti et al.
\cite{stochastikPetriNets2013Solti} study stochastic Petri nets with flexible
delay distributions. Camargo et al. \cite{Camargo2020} proposed a framework
that discovers process structure, activity durations, resource pools,
calendars, and arrival behaviour from event logs. Simod further automates the
discovery and calibration of simulation models \cite{simod2024}.

Another research branch studies generative process models. Camargo et al.
\cite{Camargo2021} compare conventional data-driven simulation with
deep-learning models. These models can learn dependencies beyond separately
fitted distributions, but they are harder to inspect and configure. This issue
is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:explainable_simulation}.

Research has moved from extracting standard simulation parameters to learning
relations between process state and simulated behaviour
\cite{PMRozinat2009,Camargo2020,Camargo2021}.


\subsection{Discovery of Simulation behavior} 

Simulation discovery still models the parts introduced in
Section~\ref{sec:bps_models}, but later methods represent them in different
ways.

\subsubsection{Control-Flow and Stochastic Routing}

Early simulation-discovery approaches combined a discovered process model with
stochastic information from observed executions \cite{PMRozinat2009}. The
process model defines which activities may occur, while stochastic weights
estimate the probability of alternative paths
\cite{stochastikPetriNets2013Solti,burke2020stochasticPD}.

The discovered control-flow structure defines the behaviour that the simulator
can generate. Too much generalisation may allow
implausible paths, while a restrictive model may miss valid process variation
\cite{PMvanDerAalst2016,VinciDeLeoni2025Repair}. The balance between fitness
and precision defines the behavioural range of the simulator
\cite{VinciDeLeoni2025Repair}.

\subsubsection{Temporal and Resource Behavior}

Early simulation-discovery approaches estimate execution times, waiting
behaviour, and resource characteristics from event-log distributions
\cite{PMRozinat2009,stochastikPetriNets2013Solti}. Later approaches model
calendars, resource pools, allocation rules, and differences between resources
\cite{Camargo2020,BPSdifferentiatedResources2022}.

Timing often depends on operational context. Execution and waiting times may
change with workload, resource availability, case characteristics, or earlier
process behaviour. Resource selection may depend on more than a fixed
activity-resource mapping \cite{BPSdifferentiatedResources2022,Vinci2026prosit}.
Recent approaches model these aspects conditionally instead of using one
global distribution per activity or resource.

\subsubsection{Arrival behavior}

Classical simulation-discovery approaches estimate inter-arrival times from
historical case-start timestamps \cite{PMRozinat2009,Camargo2020}. Later
approaches condition arrivals on time or context. This allows the simulator to
model demand that changes across hours, weekdays, or operating periods instead
of using one pooled arrival process \cite{Vinci2026prosit}.


\subsection{Data-Aware and Process-State-Aware Simulation}

Classical stochastic simulation models often estimate one set of routing
probabilities for all cases. For example, if two paths occur with frequencies
of 70\,\% and 30\,\%, the model may use these probabilities for every case.
This describes how often each path occurred, but not why cases followed
different paths \cite{PMRozinat2009,burke2020stochasticPD}.

Data-aware simulation conditions stochastic behaviour on process data.
Mannhardt et al. \cite{mannhardt2023stochasticProcessesModelling} extend
stochastic Petri nets with transition weights that depend on process variables.
The model can then capture differences between types of cases.

De Leoni et al. \cite{Vinci2023Inf.Activ.Prob} extend this idea through the concept of the \textit{process state}. Rather than conditioning decisions only on static case attributes, the process state may also describe information accumulated during the execution of the case, including process-variable values and the history of previously executed activities. Their results show that incorporating this richer representation of the current process state can improve the estimation of activity-firing probabilities compared with basic frequency-based branching probabilities.

The development can be summarised as:

\begin{center}
Static Branching Probabilities \\
$\downarrow$ \\
Stochastic Process Models \\
$\downarrow$ \\
Data-Dependent behavior \\
$\downarrow$ \\
Process-State-Aware Simulation
\end{center}

Recent work extends data awareness beyond routing behavior. López-Pintado et al. \cite{lopez2024} investigate the discovery and simulation of data-aware business processes more broadly, reflecting a shift toward simulation models in which multiple behavioral dimensions depend on process context rather than only on marginal historical frequencies.


\subsection{ProSiT as a Probabilistic Data-Aware Simulation Framework}

ProSiT applies data awareness to several simulation dimensions
\cite{Vinci2026prosit}. Its probabilistic white-box predictors model
control-flow decisions, execution times, waiting times, resource selection, and
arrival behaviour.

The framework uses probabilistic decision trees to split the feature space.
Each leaf contains a local probability distribution. Simulation behaviour can
vary by context while remaining stochastic. This differs from both a model
with one global distribution per parameter and a deterministic model that
returns one value.

ProSiT can use trace attributes, execution history, resource information,
temporal information, and workload features \cite{Vinci2026prosit}. It does
not support every event-level attribute that changes during execution. The
process-state approach of Mannhardt et al.
\cite{mannhardt2023stochasticProcessesModelling} and de Leoni et al.
\cite{Vinci2023Inf.Activ.Prob} covers a broader range of context than the
current ProSiT implementation.

ProSiT is useful for this thesis because its level of contextual conditioning
can be changed. This allows a comparison of model expressiveness and held-out
accuracy in the CTB truck process.


\paragraph{Configurable expressiveness in ProSiT.}

Two ProSiT configuration parameters are especially relevant for the experimental design in Section~\ref{sec:simulation_discovery_methodology} \cite{Vinci2026prosit}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{max\_depth\_tree} controls the maximum depth of the probabilistic decision trees used for conditional simulation behavior. Setting \texttt{max\_depth\_tree}$=0$ disables conditional tree-based modelling and yields an unconditional distribution-based configuration. Increasing the depth allows the model to distinguish more detailed combinations of contextual features, while also increasing model complexity and potential overfitting.

    \item \texttt{use\_workload\_features} determines whether workload-related features are made available to the waiting-time and resource-selection models. When enabled, these models can condition their behavior on the current operational workload and queue state in addition to the remaining contextual attributes.
\end{itemize}

These parameters define the later comparison between a distribution-based
configuration, a data-aware configuration, and a data-aware configuration with
workload information. The evaluation tests whether the added model detail
improves simulation accuracy.

\section{Explainable and White-Box Simulation}
\label{sec:explainable_simulation}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Explainability in Simulation-Based Decision Support}
Business Process Simulation supports decisions about resources, process
design, capacity, and investments. Decision makers need to understand why a
model produces an outcome and how that outcome changes when parameters are
modified. Traditional BPS models are transparent because their control flow, routing probabilities, resources, and timing assumptions are defined by hand \cite{BPSAalst2015}. Machine-learning models can learn behaviour from historical event data and may improve prediction \cite{Camargo2020,Camargo2021}. However, their internal logic can be difficult to inspect. A model may reproduce historical behaviour without showing why it selected a route, waiting time, or resource. This makes validation and what-if analysis harder \cite{runtimeIntegration2024}. For what-if analysis, users must be able to change parameters or rules and judge whether the model response is reasonable. Explainable simulation tries to combine data-driven prediction with visible decision logic.

\subsection{Probabilistic White-Box Simulation Models}
The Goal of White-box simulation is making learned decisions visible and especially editable. The RIMS framework combines a discrete-event simulation with predictive models at
runtime while keeping an inspectable simulation model
\cite{runtimeIntegration2024}.

Vinci et al. introduced ProSiT (\textit{PROcess SImulation Tool}) as a
white-box simulation-discovery framework \cite{Vinci2026prosit}. ProSiT uses
probabilistic decision trees for several parts of the simulation:
\begin{itemize}
    \item control-flow decisions,
    \item activity execution times,
    \item waiting times,
    \item resource assignments, and
    \item arrival processes.
\end{itemize}
The tree conditions are visible, and each leaf contains a probability
distribution. Users can inspect and change these rules without retraining a
deep-learning model. The distributions keep the stochastic variation
needed for simulation \cite{Vinci2026prosit}.

Published evaluations show that probabilistic white-box models can reach
similar accuracy to black-box alternatives while being easier to inspect and
configure \cite{Vinci2026prosit}. However, the evidence mainly comes from
benchmarks and business-process settings. There is little evidence from
physical logistics systems such as container terminals. Explainability is
difficult to measure. A decision tree is easier to read than a neural network,
but this does not prove that its rules are correct or useful. White-box
simulation must balance accuracy, explainability, and configurability.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
For terminological precision, the relationships exposed by such learned
rules are statistical associations conditional on the training data and
model specification. Their visibility supports inspection and deliberate
configuration, but does not by itself establish causal effects.

\section{Simulation Verification, Validation, and Trustworthiness}
\label{sec:simulation_validation_background}
Validation measures, wether a simulaton model is accurate enough for its intended purpose. A generated model may reproduce historical truck turnaround distributions but still fail to predict an intervention not seen in the observed data \cite{Sargent2013Validation,BPSAalst2015}.
Automatically discovered simulation models do not remove this
requirement. They replace some manual assumptions with assumptions about
event-log semantics, discovery algorithms, data attributes and fitted
distributions, all of which require examination.

\subsection{Foundational Distinction Between Verification and Validation}

Sargent separates several questions that are often conflated under the
single term \emph{validation} \cite{Sargent2013Validation}.
\emph{Conceptual-model validity} asks whether the theories, assumptions
and logical relationships in the conceptual model are reasonable for
the intended purpose. \emph{Computerized-model verification} asks
whether that conceptual model has been implemented correctly.
\emph{Data validity} concerns whether the data used to construct,
calibrate and evaluate the model are adequate. Finally,
\emph{operational validity} asks whether the computerised model's
output behaviour is accurate enough over the domain in which the
model is intended to be used. Historical-data validation---running the
model under historical input conditions and comparing its output with
observed system data---is one technique for assessing operational
validity \cite{Sargent2013Validation}.

Table~\ref{tab:validation_evidence_types} maps these distinctions to the
type of evidence needed in an automatically discovered BPS study. The
categories are complementary rather than interchangeable. For example,
a low event-log distance is evidence of operational accuracy, but it
cannot show that a timestamp proxy has the correct physical meaning or
that the simulator implemented a resource constraint as intended.

\centering
\caption{Validation questions and corresponding evidence in an
event-log-discovered simulation study, based on Sargent's framework
\cite{Sargent2013Validation}.}
\label{tab:validation_evidence_types}
\begin{tabular}{p{0.23\textwidth}p{0.29\textwidth}p{0.39\textwidth}}
\toprule
\textbf{Question} & \textbf{Validity concept} & \textbf{Typical evidence} \\
\midrule
Is the abstraction reasonable? & Conceptual-model validity &

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

This distinction clarifies the scope of this thesis. The
real-versus-simulated event-log comparison is a form of held-out
historical-data or operational validation. The event-log construction
and the physical RMG capacity constraint contribute data- and
conceptual-validity evidence, while executable case contracts and
provenance checks contribute verification evidence. None of these alone
proves that every what-if response is the true causal response of the
terminal.

\subsection{From Historical Logs to Operational Validation}

Process Mining provides a natural representation for historical-data
validation because both the observed system and the simulator can be
represented by event logs. Rozinat et al. established an early version
of this principle: they discover control-flow, data, performance and
resource perspectives, integrate them into a coloured Petri-net
simulation model, simulate a second event log, and analyse how well that
generated log approximates the characteristics of the original log
\cite{PMRozinat2009}. This is stronger than checking whether the
discovered model can execute; it evaluates whether its generated
behaviour resembles recorded behaviour.

Camargo et al. formalise the same idea as an optimisation objective.
Candidate BPS configurations generate event logs and hyperparameter
optimisation searches for the configuration that maximises similarity
to observed log behaviour \cite{Camargo2020}. Their later comparison of
data-driven simulation and deep generative models further shows
why quality must not be inferred from trace frequencies alone:
conventional data-driven simulators may reproduce activity sequences
and their frequencies while performing worse on temporal dynamics
\cite{Camargo2021}.

The implication is that operational accuracy is multi-dimensional.
Relevant perspectives include control flow, activity and case timing,
case arrivals, congestion and resource behaviour. A model can be close
to the real log in one perspective and far away in another; collapsing
all discrepancies into one number can hide the mechanism
that is incorrectly represented
\cite{ChapelaCampa2023Trust,Chapela2025SimQuality}.

\subsection{Multi-Perspective Event-Log Comparison}

Chapela-Campa et al. provide a validation framework that matches the
evaluation problem of this thesis
\cite{ChapelaCampa2023Trust,Chapela2025SimQuality}. Their starting point
is that the true BPS model is normally unavailable and that different
simulation engines use incomparable internal formats. They evaluate a model
indirectly as a generator: multiple logs generated by
the BPS model are compared with an actual event log that was not used
to construct the model. The framework separates control-flow, temporal
and congestion perspectives and reports a measure for each instead of
using one aggregate score for overall model quality.
The measures are evaluated using controlled perturbations and by
comparing alternative automatically discovered BPS models, showing that
perspective-specific measures can identify the source of a discrepancy
\cite{Chapela2025SimQuality}.

\subsubsection{Control-Flow Perspective}

Control-flow output quality asks whether the simulator generates
activity sequences and sequence frequencies resembling the observed
process. It is distinct from replay fitness of a control-flow model:
fitness asks whether real traces are allowed by the model, whereas
output validation asks which traces the stochastic simulator actually
generates and with what frequency.

Stochastic conformance work applies transport distances to probability
distributions over traces or other behavioural abstractions
\cite{leeman2013EarthMovers}. Chapela-Campa et al. instead define two
simulation-oriented log distances \cite{ChapelaCampa2023Trust,
Chapela2025SimQuality}. Control-Flow Log Distance (CFLD) computes the
normalised Damerau--Levenshtein distance between trace pairs and uses
the Hungarian algorithm to find the minimum-cost one-to-one matching
between the two logs. CFLD compares complete traces; it is not
merely a comparison of variant-frequency vectors. Because this matching
is computationally expensive, N-Gram Distance (NGD) compares normalised
frequencies of local activity subsequences. NGD is scalable and detects
local ordering differences, although it loses some
complete-trace information.

This thesis does not claim to implement the full Chapela-Campa metric
suite. Its structural evidence combines held-out Petri-net conformance,
explicit case-language contracts and yard-activity frequency error.
These measures answer the CTB-specific structural questions, while the
Chapela-Campa framework supplies the rationale for keeping structural
evidence separate from temporal accuracy.

\subsubsection{Temporal Perspective}

Identical or similar traces do not imply similar process performance.
Temporal validation considers whether the simulator reproduces when
events occur, how cases progress after arrival and how long activities
and complete cases take. Camargo et al. show empirically that a
generative model may reproduce control-flow frequencies but still miss
temporal dynamics \cite{Camargo2021}.

The Chapela-Campa framework distinguishes three event-distribution
measures \cite{ChapelaCampa2023Trust,Chapela2025SimQuality}.
\emph{Absolute Event Distribution} (AED) compares event counts across
absolute calendar-time bins; \emph{Circadian Event Distribution} (CED)
compares recurring weekday and hour-of-day patterns; and
\emph{Relative Event Distribution} (RED) offsets timestamps by each
case's arrival and compares its temporal progression. AED and RED
mean \emph{event distribution}, not absolute or relative error
distance. For end-to-end performance, Cycle Time Distribution (CTD)
compares the empirical distributions of complete case durations.

The CTB evaluation uses more directly interpretable distributions at
the level of its research questions: inter-arrival time, per-activity
yard service time, model-derived pre-service delay and case turnaround
time. Reporting complete distributions rather than only their means is
useful because equal averages can conceal differences in dispersion,
skewness or tails. Per-activity results prevent common RMG
events from concealing weaker accuracy for less frequent yard
activities.

\subsubsection{Arrival and Congestion Perspectives}

Congestion is a system-level consequence of the interaction between
demand, service requirements and resource availability. It cannot be
validated from activity-duration marginals alone. Chapela-Campa et al.
use Case Arrival Rate (CAR) to compare binned arrival counts and shapes,
and CTD to compare another congestion component, the length of
time cases remain in the system
\cite{ChapelaCampa2023Trust,Chapela2025SimQuality}. An arrival model can
be temporally unrealistic even if it generates the correct
total number of cases.

This issue matters at CTB: truck demand determines competition for
yard resources, and simultaneous arrivals result from the minute-resolved
source data. The evaluation reports
inter-arrival distance, simultaneous-arrival share and arrivals per
elapsed hour alongside turnaround and yard-service distributions.
These measures do not directly observe a physical queue; they assess
whether the generated workload and its resulting temporal behaviour
match what the available event log can identify.

\subsubsection{Wasserstein Distance}

The Wasserstein Distance is a metric for comparing probability
distributions that originates from optimal transport theory
\cite{Panaretos2019Wasserstein}. Intuitively, it quantifies the minimum
amount of work required to transform one probability distribution into
another by transporting probability mass between their respective
locations. For one-dimensional probability distributions $F$ and $G$
with finite first moments, the first Wasserstein distance can be
expressed as

\begin{equation}
    W_1(F,G)
    =
    \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
    |F(x)-G(x)|\,dx.
\end{equation}

A value of zero indicates identical distributions, while larger values
indicate increasing distributional dissimilarity
\cite{Panaretos2019Wasserstein}. An important property for the present
study is that the first Wasserstein distance retains the unit of the
underlying variable. Thus, when comparing observed and simulated
duration distributions measured in minutes, the resulting distance is
also expressed in minutes.

The Earth Mover's Distance (EMD) is closely related to the Wasserstein
distance, but the terms are not universally interchangeable. EMD is
commonly used for discrete mass-transport measurements, whereas the
Wasserstein distance is defined between probability measures more
generally. In the formulation used by Chapela-Campa et al., EMD may
also compare time series with unequal total mass and penalise the
creation of missing mass. The first Wasserstein distance
assumes only probability distributions with equal normalised mass
\cite{leeman2013EarthMovers,ChapelaCampa2023Trust,Chapela2025SimQuality}. When both empirical
samples are normalised to probability distributions, the corresponding
one-dimensional transport distance is equivalent to the first
Wasserstein distance.

Related optimal-transport distances have also been applied in Process
Mining for stochastic conformance checking \cite{leeman2013EarthMovers}. Although the thesis uses the term EMD, the numerical quantity actually calculated for service times, inter-arrival times and turnaround times is the first Wasserstein distance between two normalized empirical one-dimensional distributions.

\subsubsection{Kolmogorov--Smirnov Statistic}

The two-sample Kolmogorov--Smirnov (KS) statistic is the maximum
vertical difference between the empirical cumulative distribution
functions of the simulated and observed samples
\cite{Massey1951KS}:
 [EQUATION]
%\begin{equation}
  % D_{n,m}=\sup_x
   % \left|F_{n,\mathrm{sim}}(x)-F_{m,\mathrm{real}}(x)\right|.
%\end{equation}
Unlike $W_1$, it is dimensionless and lays between zero and one. The two
statistics are complementary: $W_1$ measures the area between the two CDFs on the operational scale, while KS reports the single maxmimum vertical distance between two distributions.
The KS statistic can be accompanied by a significance-test $p$-value, but
that $p$-value answers a different question from practical accuracy.
With large event logs, small and operationally unimportant differences
can be statistically significant. The magnitude of the statistic and
the distributional plots must be interpreted separately from
binary significance decisions.

\subsubsection{Additional Requirements for Data-Aware Discovery}

A data-aware simulator conditions behaviour on case attributes, process
state or workload information rather than using only global
distributions and branching frequencies
\cite{Vinci2023Inf.Activ.Prob,lopez2024,Vinci2026prosit}. This adds a
validation layer. First, every predictor used at a simulated decision
point must be available at that point; attributes computed from a later
outcome create target leakage even if aggregate log similarity is high.
Second, discovery and model selection must be separated from the final
test log. Third, the generated log must satisfy process and domain
invariants that aggregate distances may overlook.

White-box rules help because their split conditions and leaf
distributions can be inspected and modified
\cite{Vinci2026prosit}. Interpretability, however, is not equivalent to
validity or causality. A readable rule may encode a spurious association,
and two differently specified models may reproduce the same marginal
distribution. Validation should combine held-out output
accuracy with rule inspection, leakage checks, structural contracts,
domain constraints and sensitivity analysis. This matters in the CTB
application because timestamp fields are operational proxies and a resource
label does not always denote a
physically interchangeable crane or storage position.

\subsubsection{Stochastic Replication and Confidence Intervals}

A BPS model is a stochastic generator. One fixed model can generate
many possible event logs, whereas the real test log is only one observed
realisation. A single simulated log mixes model accuracy
with the random draws of that run. Chapela-Campa et al. address this
problem directly: they generate $K$ logs from $K$ simulation runs,
match their case count and start point to an actual test log that was
not used for model construction, compare every generated log with that
test log, and construct confidence intervals for each perspective-
specific distance \cite{ChapelaCampa2023Trust}. Their controlled
evaluation uses $K=10$ replications and reports means and 95\,\%
intervals.

This design is consistent with classical simulation-output analysis,
where independent replications with different random-number streams
provide observations for estimating a mean and its Monte-Carlo
uncertainty \cite{Law2015Simulation}. For replication-level values
$x_1,\ldots,x_N$, a two-sided Student-$t$ interval is
\begin{equation}
    \bar{x}\ \pm\
    t_{0.975,N-1}\frac{s}{\sqrt{N}},
\end{equation}
where $s$ is the sample standard deviation across replications.

The interpretation of this interval must remain narrow. Conditional on
one frozen discovered parameter bundle and one fixed real test log, it
quantifies the variability caused by simulation sampling. It does not
include event-log sampling uncertainty, input-distribution estimation
uncertainty, alternative discovery outcomes, measurement error or
uncertainty about the correctness of a what-if response. Those require
additional resampling, repeated discovery, sensitivity experiments or
external domain evidence. Chapter~\ref{chp:experiments_and_framework}
uses this conditional Monte-Carlo interpretation for its ten-seed
protocol.

\subsection{Trustworthiness and the Boundary of Historical Validation}

Historical accuracy is necessary for a simulator that claims to
represent an existing process, but it is not sufficient for unrestricted
decision support. Chapela-Campa et al. identify this limit:
comparison with an original event log assesses replication of the
\emph{as-is} process, not the accuracy of predictions after a process
change \cite{ChapelaCampa2023Trust,Chapela2025SimQuality}. Similarly,
Sargent's purpose-relative view implies that evidence collected in one
operating region cannot automatically validate use in another
\cite{Sargent2013Validation}.

Trustworthiness cannot be measured with one metric. Evidence is needed for
data and conceptual validity, implementation, held-out accuracy,
reproducibility, assumptions, stochastic robustness, domain review, and the
limits of extrapolation. ProSiT provides probabilistic rules that can be
inspected and configured \cite{Vinci2026prosit}. Applied work shows how a
discovered simulation model can be connected to a resource-allocation problem
\cite{Hospital2026Vinci}. These properties support validation and scenario
analysis, but they do not replace evidence for each intervention.

This thesis does not claim to solve the general
trustworthiness problem of simulation discovery. It contributes a
container-terminal case study: the model is tested against an unseen
historical period, across structural, temporal and arrival perspectives,
with repeated stochastic runs and explicit domain constraints. Scenario
results are interpreted as conditional consequences of the documented
model assumptions, not as experimentally established causal effects.

\subsection{Research Gap}

Container terminals are dynamic systems with limited resources and connected
truck, yard, storage, and equipment processes. Simulation is used to
support decisions in this setting \cite{Bruzzone2012,Kastner2021,Carboni2025}.
However, most terminal simulation models are built by hand. Their construction,
calibration, and maintenance require time and domain knowledge
\cite{PMRozinat2009}.

Simulation discovery creates models from event logs. Recent work covers
simulation parameters, data-aware decision logic, and automated model building
\cite{Camargo2020,simod2024,Vinci2026prosit}. Data-aware approaches can use
context and process state instead of only fixed branching probabilities
\cite{mannhardt2023stochasticProcessesModelling,Vinci2023Inf.Activ.Prob}.
ProSiT adds visible probabilistic decision models
\cite{Vinci2026prosit}.

Most evaluations come from healthcare, administration, and standard business
processes. Evidence from large container terminals is limited. Open questions
include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the robustness of automatically discovered models,
    \item their use for operational decision support,
    \item their ability to reproduce congestion and workload, and
    \item the value of white-box simulation in logistics.
\end{itemize}
This thesis studies these questions for truck handling at HHLA Container
Terminal Burchardkai (CTB). It provides evidence about the strengths and
limits of simulation discovery in this setting.
