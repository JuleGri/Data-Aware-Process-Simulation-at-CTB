\section{Container Terminal Truck Operations}
\label{sec:bg_terminal}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

A container terminal connects ships, trains and trucks. Containers are stored
in a yard between the waterside and the landside. In many terminals, including
the one studied here, the yard consists of storage blocks. Automated
rail-mounted gantry cranes (RMG) move containers inside a block and hand them
over to trucks at the landside end of the block. Terminals often have further
areas, for example a depot for empty containers and a manually operated area.
Trucks enter and leave through a gate \cite{CTSteenken2004}.

\paragraph{The truck visit}
A truck visit starts when the truck passes the entry gate (\emph{Gate In}). The
truck then drives to one or more handling areas. At each \emph{stop}, a
container is delivered to the terminal, received from it, or both. A visit that
delivers and receives in one trip is called a double cycle or \emph{mixed}
visit. After the last stop, the truck leaves through the exit gate (\emph{Gate
Out}). Most visits have one stop. Visits with two or more stops are less
frequent but take longer.

\paragraph{Physical constraints}
A truck visit is a physical process. Several constraints follow from this. They
hold for every visit, whether or not they are visible in the recorded data.
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt,topsep=4pt]
\item A truck is one vehicle. Its stops happen one after another and never at
the same time.
\item A visit exists to move containers. A visit without any stop between the
gates does not occur in practice.
\item A storage block has a limited number of handover lanes and cranes. Trucks
share them with each other and with the waterside and rail operations.
\item A truck can carry a limited number of containers.
\end{itemize}
These constraints run through the whole thesis. A model that is learned only
from event frequencies does not know them. Section~\ref{sec:bg_rules} shows how
such constraints can be written down as rules, and Section~\ref{sec:prosit_limits}
explains why the simulator needs them explicitly.

\paragraph{Key performance indicators}
Truck operations are measured with key performance indicators (KPIs). The
\emph{truck turnaround time} (TTT) is the time from Gate In to Gate Out. Waiting
times isolate the delay before a gate or a yard stop, and utilisation measures
describe how much of the yard and the equipment is in use. TTT is the main
measure of service quality for truck handling, because it is the time that
trucking companies and cargo owners experience. It is one of the four main
KPIs of the HHLA container terminals \cite{Bett2024,CTprocessMiningTAS2020}.
The mean alone hides the long waits that cause complaints. This thesis
therefore also reports the 90th percentile (P90) of TTT and the number of hours
in which TTT is unusually high (Section~\ref{sec:exp_kpis}).

\paragraph{Simulation in container terminals}
Terminal managers decide about truck appointments, equipment, staff and yard
operations while the terminal is running. Because the processes are
connected, a local change can affect waiting times and service elsewhere.
Simulation allows such changes to be tested without disturbing live
operations. In the literature, discrete-event simulation is the most common
approach, because a terminal can be described by events such as arrivals, gate
passages and container moves \cite{YUN1999DESSimulation,Carboni2025}. Studies
use simulation to compare equipment numbers and operating policies
\cite{Kastner2021}, to generate scenarios \cite{Hartmann2005cenariosforCTSimulation},
and to evaluate truck appointment systems and double cycles
\cite{CTprocessMiningTAS2020,Bett2024}. These models are built by hand. This
needs domain knowledge, much manual work, and recalibration when operations
change. A hand-built model may also describe how the process is meant to work
rather than how it actually runs \cite{PMRozinat2009}.

\paragraph{Process mining in logistics}
Process mining has been applied to logistics, mostly to discover and analyse
processes rather than to build executable simulation models
\cite{RWAlnahas2023Logistics}. Reconstructing a logistics process from several
information systems requires care, because timestamps and identifiers from
different systems may describe different operational stages
\cite{RWBecker2017Context}. Grobis and Ihlenfeldt use event data to prepare a
simulation model for production logistics and split the time between two
activities into transport and waiting \cite{Grobis2025ProductionLogistics}. In
ports, process mining has been used to describe operations, for example the
performance and the deviations of a bulk port, but not to build simulation
models \cite{Wang2014BulkPort}. Across 105 studies that combine simulation and
process mining, Sulis et al.\ found one in logistics and one in transportation.
Most of the studies concern healthcare and manufacturing
\cite{Sulis2026SimulationReview}. In a hospital, Vinci et al.\ discover a
simulation model from an event log, check it against observed behaviour and only
then use it to compare resource configurations \cite{Hospital2026Vinci}. This
thesis follows the same order. The authors argue that queueing models suit
capacity problems of a single activity, whereas processes with several
activities and several resource pools need simulation. Their method assumes that
delays arise only from limited resources, and the simulation was judged in
discussions with the staff rather than by a structured procedure. The authors
name both as limitations.

An adequate simulation of the truck process must therefore reproduce TTT, its
tail and its growth under load. It must also respect the physical constraints
listed above, because a policy that changes the load on the blocks is only
credible if the simulated trucks behave like real trucks.

\section{Event Logs}
\label{sec:operational_data_event_logs}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Process mining works on \emph{event logs}. An event is one recorded
occurrence of an activity. Each event belongs to a \emph{case}, the object
whose process is followed. In this thesis, one truck visit is one case. Each
event records at least the case, the \emph{activity} (for example
\texttt{Gate In} or \texttt{RMG\_receive}) and a timestamp. An event log groups
the events by case and keeps their order. The ordered list of activities of a
case is its \emph{trace}. Cases with the same trace share a \emph{variant}. The
set of variants is the observed language of the log
\cite{Aalst2016}. Table~\ref{tab:eventlogexample} shows two simplified
truck visits.

\paragraph{Timestamps}
Simulation needs more than the order of events. When an event has a
\emph{start} and a \emph{complete} timestamp, their difference is its
execution time. A third point in time, \emph{enablement}, is the moment at
which the process model allows the activity to begin. It is not recorded. It is
reconstructed by replaying the log on the process model
\cite{PMRozinat2009,Vinci2026prosit}. The time between enablement and start is
called waiting time. In a physical process, this interval can contain queueing,
driving and other delays. The meaning of each timestamp must therefore be
checked. In the terminal data, a stop starts when the truck reports ready in the
handover lane. The stop interval can therefore already contain waiting for the
crane (Section~\ref{sec:timestamp_semantics}).

\paragraph{Attributes}
Further attributes describe the case and its context, for example the number of
containers, the planned stops or the load of the terminal at Gate In. A
data-aware simulation may only use an attribute at a decision point if its
value is known at that point. Otherwise the model uses information from the
future, which is called leakage (Section~\ref{sec:entry_known_attributes}).

Event logs are exchanged between tools in the XES format \cite{Aalst2016}.
In this thesis, the same log is read by ProM \cite{vanDongen2005ProM} and by
PM4Py \cite{Berti2023PM4Py}.

\section{Process Discovery and Petri Nets}
\label{sec:bg_discovery}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Process discovery builds a process model from an event log
\cite{Aalst2016,Aalst2022Foundations}. The model describes the
\emph{control flow} of the process, that is, which activities occur and in
which order they can follow each other. In this thesis, the terms process
model and control-flow model mean the same thing, a Petri net discovered from
the event log. The model shows how the process was executed, not how it was
planned. ProSiT, the simulator used in this thesis, needs the control flow as a
Petri net. This section introduces Petri nets, the discovery algorithms that
the thesis compares, and how domain rules can be brought into a discovered
control flow.

\subsection{Petri Nets, Workflow Nets and Silent Transitions}
\label{sec:bg_petri}

A Petri net is a graph of \emph{places} (circles) and \emph{transitions}
(boxes), connected by arcs \cite{Aalst2016}. For a reader who comes from the
event log, the two kinds of nodes have a simple meaning. Transitions correspond
to the recorded activities, for example \texttt{Gate In} or
\texttt{RMG\_receive}. Places lie between the transitions, like junctions
between the activities. They determine in which order the activities can occur
and where the process can take one of several ways.

Formally, a labelled Petri net is a tuple $N=(P,T,F,\lambda)$. $P$ is a finite
set of places, $T$ a finite set of transitions, and
$F\subseteq(P\times T)\cup(T\times P)$ the set of arcs. The labelling function
$\lambda: T\rightarrow A\cup\{\tau\}$ gives each transition either an activity
of the event log or the label $\tau$. The places with an arc to a transition
$t$ are its input places ${\bullet}t$. The places with an arc from $t$ are its
output places $t{\bullet}$.

The state of a net is called a \emph{marking}. It says how many tokens lie in
each place. A transition is \emph{enabled} when each of its input places holds
a token. Firing it removes one token from each input place and adds one token
to each output place. A place with several outgoing arcs is a choice, because
only one of the enabled transitions can take the token. A transition with
several output places starts parallel branches. It creates one token per
branch, and the branches can then fire independently of each other.

A \emph{workflow net} has one source place $i$ and one sink place $o$, and
every node lies on a path from $i$ to $o$. A run starts with one token in $i$
and should end with one token in $o$. For a truck visit, the token in $i$ is the
truck before the gate and the token in $o$ the completed visit. A workflow net
is \emph{sound} if three conditions hold \cite{Aalst2016}. First, from
every reachable marking the final marking can still be reached (option to
complete). Second, when $o$ is marked, no other place holds a token (proper
completion). Third, every transition can fire in some run (no dead
transitions). Soundness matters for simulation, because the simulator executes
the net and must bring every case to the end.

Transitions labelled $\tau$ are \emph{silent}. They fire like other
transitions but leave no event in the log. They express routing that has no
recorded counterpart, for example skipping an activity, repeating a loop, or
splitting and joining parallel branches. The net in
Figure~\ref{fig:bg_petri_silent} accepts both \texttt{Gate In, RMG\_receive,
Gate Out} and \texttt{Gate In, Gate Out}, because the silent transition skips
the stop.

Two notions have to be kept apart, the language of a net and its
representation. The \emph{language} of a workflow net is the set of visible
activity sequences of all its complete runs, that is, the behaviour it allows.
The \emph{representation} is the net itself, its places, transitions and arcs.
Two nets can look very different and still have the same language, for example
because their silent transitions are placed differently. Discovery algorithms
add silent transitions systematically. The Inductive Miner, for example, turns
an optional activity into a silent skip and a parallel part into a silent split
and join \cite{Leemans2014InductiveMiner}.

Silent transitions have three consequences in this thesis. First,
combinations of silent skips can allow behaviour that never occurs, such as the
visit without a stop in Figure~\ref{fig:bg_petri_silent}. Because silent paths
leave no trace, this is easy to overlook. Second, alignments
(Section~\ref{sec:astar_alignment_definition}) must decide for every silent
transition whether it fires, which enlarges the search. Third, a simulator also
has to choose at every silent transition whether it fires. The event log never
shows when a silent transition fired, so the simulator can learn these choices
only from a replay of the log on the net (Section~\ref{sec:prosit_limits}).


\subsection{Discovery Algorithms}
\label{sec:bg_algorithms}

Discovery algorithms differ in how they turn observed sequences into a model.
Ideally, the discovered net contains every recorded activity once, and a run
through the net can reproduce every combination of activities that was
observed, without allowing much more. All algorithms receive the same
sequences, but they make different assumptions about what the sequences mean.
Some derive ordering relations between pairs of activities, some count how
often one activity follows another, some split the log step by step into
smaller parts, and some search for constraints that every observed sequence
satisfies. They also differ in what they guarantee, for example whether the
resulting net is always sound. The same log therefore leads to different nets,
and the nets receive different quality values
(Section~\ref{sec:bg_conformance}).

Most algorithms also have a frequency or noise threshold. Behaviour that occurs
less often than the threshold is treated as noise and left out of the model.
The higher the threshold, the more rare sequences the model does not represent.
Whether rare behaviour is noise or regular behaviour cannot be decided from its
frequency alone. It is a question of the data quality and of the domain. In a
terminal, a rare sequence can be a recording error, but it can also be a
regular, infrequent visit, for example a truck that serves two blocks.

The process discovery in this thesis compares four families of algorithms.
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=3pt,topsep=4pt]
\item \textbf{Inductive Miner.} The Inductive Miner splits the log step by step
into smaller parts. At each step it looks for one of four patterns, called
cuts. The activities can follow each other in a sequence, they can be
alternatives in a choice, they can repeat in a loop, or they can be independent
of each other in a parallel part. In a parallel part, the miner assumes that
the activities may occur in any order and even at the same time. The result is
a process tree, which is converted into a sound workflow net. The variant with
filtering (IMf) first removes behaviour below a noise threshold
\cite{Leemans2014InductiveMiner}.
\item \textbf{Heuristics Miner.} The Heuristics Miner counts how often one
activity directly follows another. It keeps a dependency between two activities
when this order is frequent and mostly one-directional, measured against a
dependency threshold \cite{heuristicsMiner2006,Weijters2011FHM}. The interactive
data-aware variant (iDHM) can also keep infrequent dependencies that depend on
data, and converts the result into a Petri net
\cite{Mannhardt2017DHM,Mannhardt2017iDHM}. In this Petri net every activity
appears once, and every dependency between two activities becomes a silent
transition between them. The result is not guaranteed to be sound.
\item \textbf{ILP Miner.} Region-based miners look for places that the observed
behaviour never violates. Such a place can be read as a rule on the order of
the activities, for example that a stop can only happen after Gate In. The ILP
Miner searches for these places with integer linear programming, a standard
optimisation method. Because it keeps only the rules that all observed
sequences respect, it tends to allow many orders that were never observed, so
its nets are often permissive. A filter controls how much infrequent behaviour
is taken into account \cite{RWVanZelst2018ILP}.
\item \textbf{Alpha family.} The Alpha Miner, the oldest of the four, derives
ordering relations between pairs of activities from the log. It records which
activity directly follows which, and whether two activities are causally
related, parallel or alternatives. From these relations it builds the places
\cite{vanderAalst2004AlphaMiner}. Alpha+ and Alpha++ add short loops and
long-distance dependencies \cite{deMedeiros2004AlphaPlus,Wen2007AlphaPlusPlus}.
The algorithms do not handle noise and do not guarantee a sound net.
\end{itemize}

In the literature, benchmarks show that no algorithm is best on every log and
every quality dimension \cite{DiscoveryBenchmark}. Rehse et al.\ argue that
discovery experiments need explicit assumptions and a controlled design. Only
then are their results comparable and a justified basis for choosing the net
that the later stages work with. This means that every algorithm receives the
same input log and that every quality measure is computed with the same
implementation \cite{RWRehse2024Experimentation}. The process discovery in this
thesis therefore follows an experiment protocol that is defined beforehand and
used to select the most fitting control flow for the later simulation. Time is
a further point. Two activities that occur in either order are not necessarily
parallel. They may simply be ordered freely. Lifecycle-aware discovery uses
start and complete timestamps to make this distinction
\cite{RWLeemans2016Lifecycle}. For trucks this matters, because two stops may
occur in either order but never at the same time.

\subsection{Domain Rules and Model Repair}
\label{sec:bg_rules}

A discovered model generalises beyond the observed traces, so it also accepts
sequences that the event log does not contain. This is important, because only
then can the model represent behaviour that may occur in the future or in an
alternative scenario. Otherwise it would reproduce only what happened in the
training period, which may be short, incomplete or partly wrong. Part of this
generalisation can, however, contradict knowledge that is known independently of
the data, for example the physical constraints of Section~\ref{sec:bg_terminal}.
Such knowledge can be written down as rules.

\paragraph{Rules as automata}
Many rules on activity sequences can be written as Declare constraints, for
example ``Gate In occurs exactly once'' or ``a mixed stop and a delivery do not
both occur at the same area'' \cite{RWDiCiccio2022Declarative}. Each such
constraint can be translated into a finite automaton
\cite{RWMaggi2011Monitoring}. A finite automaton is a simple machine with a
finite number of states. It reads a sequence of activities one by one, moves
from state to state, and at the end accepts or rejects the sequence. The
automaton of a constraint accepts exactly the sequences that satisfy the
constraint. A sound workflow net can be read as an automaton as well. Its
reachable markings form a finite graph, called the reachability graph, and the
paths through this graph from the initial to the final marking spell out
exactly the language of the net. Two automata can be combined into a product
that accepts exactly the sequences that both of them accept. Running the net and
the rule automata together therefore answers two questions exactly. The first
is whether the net accepts a sequence that breaks a rule. The second is which
sequences of the net satisfy all rules.

\paragraph{Rules on data and time}
Not every rule is a rule on the order of activities. ``Stops never overlap'' is a
rule on timestamps. ``The number of stops equals the number of planned stops''
depends on a case attribute. A classic Petri net describes only orders, so it
cannot express such rules. For these cases, data Petri nets exist
\cite{Mannhardt2016Precision}. They extend a classic Petri net by variables, for
example the number of planned stops of a truck, and by \emph{guards}. A guard is
a rule attached to a transition. It is a condition on the variables that must
hold before the transition may fire. With guards, soundness also depends on the
data, because every case must still be able to complete under the guards
\cite{FelliDeLeoniMontali2021}.

\paragraph{Domain knowledge in discovery and repair}
In the literature, Schuster et al.\ classify how domain knowledge enters process
discovery \cite{schuster2022}. Knowledge can be used before discovery, during
discovery, or \emph{a posteriori} on a discovered model. It can be explicit
or come from user feedback, and it can be applied automatically or
interactively. Explicit knowledge is written in one of two kinds of formalism.
An \emph{imperative} formalism, such as a Petri net, follows a closed-world
assumption, so only the behaviour it describes is allowed. A \emph{declarative}
formalism, such as Declare, follows an open-world assumption, so everything is
allowed that does not break one of its constraints \cite{schuster2022}. This
thesis uses both. The discovered net is imperative, the domain rules are
declarative, and a net is admitted only if its language respects the rules.
Above all, the rules enforce physical feasibility, for example that a truck
never performs two stops at once. Schuster et al.\ also classify approaches by
the formalism of the output model. Here the output must be a sound workflow net,
because ProSiT executes Petri nets and learns its routing per transition
(Section~\ref{sec:prosit_background}). Several methods have been proposed to
bring rules into discovery or to use them for the repair of a discovered model
\cite{RWGreco2015Precedence,RWNorouzifar2026Rules,RWDixit2015Domain,Revoredo2023DomainRepair}.

Process enhancement changes a discovered model in two ways. \emph{Repair}
changes the model so that it reflects reality better, and \emph{extension} adds
a perspective such as data, time or resources \cite{Aalst2016,RWdeLeoni2022Enhancement}.
Classical model repair changes a model so that it can replay every trace of a
log while staying close to the original \cite{RWFahland2015Repair}.
This adds behaviour. The opposite direction removes behaviour that the log
does not support. One way is to add places that connect distant parts of the
net, so that combinations of activities that never occur together are forbidden
\cite{RWKalenkova2020NonLocal}. Another way is to refine process trees
\cite{RWRennert2024Precision}. Vinci and de Leoni repair a model for
simulation. They simulate it, find behaviour that is missing in the generated
log and add it \cite{SimulationGuidedRepair2025}. Restricting behaviour that
the simulator generates too often is named as future work.

In this thesis, explicit and declarative domain knowledge is used after
discovery. The rules on activity sequences are checked on the language of each
discovered net, which is not changed. The rule on the planned stops becomes a
guard in the simulator (Section~\ref{sec:exp_rules}).

\section{Conformance Checking}
\label{sec:bg_conformance}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Conformance checking measures how well a model represents an event log
\cite{aalst2012ConformanceChecking,RWCarmona2022Conformance}. Four quality
dimensions are commonly used in the literature \cite{Buijs2014Quality}.
\emph{Fitness} measures how much of the observed behaviour the model can replay.
\emph{Precision} penalises behaviour that the model allows but the log does not
show. \emph{Generalisation} rewards a model that is likely to accept new, unseen
traces of the same process. \emph{Simplicity} prefers small models. The
dimensions pull in different directions. This creates a trade-off that has to be
balanced when the best model is searched. A model that accepts every sequence
has perfect fitness but poor precision. A model that lists every observed trace
has perfect precision but poor generalisation.

\paragraph{Alignments}
\label{sec:astar_alignment_definition}
An alignment fits an observed trace to the model. It pairs each event of the
trace with a step of a complete run of the model and marks where the two do not
match. Alignments are the basis for computing fitness. An alignment is a
sequence of moves of three types. A \emph{synchronous move} consumes an event
from the trace and fires the transition with the same label. A \emph{log move}
consumes an event that the model cannot follow at this point. A \emph{model
move} fires a transition without a matching event. This happens when the model
requires an activity that the trace does not contain, and also whenever a
silent transition fires, because silent transitions never have an event. Model
moves on silent transitions are therefore free. The other deviations have a
positive cost. An optimal alignment $\gamma^{\star}$ of a trace $\sigma$ with a
net $N$ is
\begin{equation}
\gamma^{\star}=\arg\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\sigma,N)} c(\gamma),
\end{equation}
where $\Gamma(\sigma,N)$ is the set of all alignments and $c$ the total cost
\cite{aalst2012ConformanceChecking,Adriansyah2011CostFitness}. Optimal
alignments are usually computed with the A* search algorithm. A* works like a
route planner. It always extends the most promising partial alignment and
estimates the cost that is still to come. If this estimate never overestimates
the remaining cost, the first complete alignment that A* finds is the cheapest
one \cite{Adriansyah2011CostFitness}.

\paragraph{Fitness}
Trace fitness is one minus the cost of the optimal alignment divided by the
cost of the worst case. In the worst case, every event of the trace is a log
move and the model runs its shortest path alone. An example makes this concrete.
The trace \texttt{Gate In, LL\_delivery, RMG\_receive, Gate Out} has four events.
Suppose that the model accepts only \texttt{Gate In, RMG\_receive, Gate Out}. The
optimal alignment has one log move for \texttt{LL\_delivery}, so its cost is 1.
The worst case has four log moves and three model moves, a cost of 7. The trace
fitness is $1-1/7\approx 0.86$. A trace \emph{fits} if its optimal alignment has
no deviation.

\paragraph{Precision}
When calculating precision, the goal is to penalise behaviour that the model
allows but the event log never shows. This includes behaviour that the model
allows through silent transitions, for example a silent skip that permits a
visit without any stop. Several ways to compute precision exist. The most common
is escaping-edges precision \cite{MunozGama2010FreshLook}. It works with the
prefixes of the traces. A \emph{prefix} is the beginning of a trace. For
example, \texttt{Gate In, LL\_delivery} is a prefix of the trace \texttt{Gate In,
LL\_delivery, RMG\_receive, Gate Out}. For every prefix, the measure compares the
activities that the model allows next with the activities that the log shows
next after the same prefix. An activity that the model allows but the log never
shows after that prefix is an \emph{escaping edge}. Precision is one minus the
share of escaping edges, weighted by how often each prefix occurs. A variant
first aligns every trace with the model, so that traces which the model cannot
replay can be included as well \cite{Adriansyah2015Precision}.

Precision and fitness pull against each other. A model that allows more
behaviour replays more traces, so its fitness rises. At the same time it allows
more behaviour that the log never shows, so its precision falls. A model that
allows less is more precise but rejects more traces. Discovery algorithms with a
noise threshold move along this trade-off.

Silent transitions make precision harder to compute. To find the activities
that the model allows after a prefix, a tool must know in which state the model
is after that prefix. Without silent transitions this state is unique. With
silent transitions, the model can be in several states after the same prefix,
and all of them have to be considered. Some implementations follow only one of
these states, chosen by a heuristic \cite{MunozGama2010FreshLook}. Two nets with
the same language can then receive different precision values
\cite{Tax2018Imprecisions}. This contradicts a basic requirement on quality
measures, which van der Aalst formulates as the proposition BehPro+. A measure
should depend only on the behaviour of the model, its language, and not on how
the model is drawn \cite{vanderAalst2018Propositions}. This thesis reports the
values of ProM and checks them against implementations that follow every state
after a prefix (Section~\ref{sec:exp_discovery_eval}).

\paragraph{Generalisation}
Generalisation rewards a model that is likely to accept new behaviour of the
same process. This is important when the training log does not cover every
possible sequence, for example because the observed period is short or because
some cases were recorded wrongly. Generalisation pulls against precision. A model
that generalises well also allows sequences that the log does not show, and
precision counts exactly these as escaping. Token-based generalisation counts how
often each transition is used in the replay. A transition that fires many times
contributes little uncertainty, and a transition that fires rarely contributes
much \cite{Buijs2014Quality}. The measure looks at transitions, not at
sequences. A model that allows many unobserved sequences through frequently used
transitions can therefore still score high.

\paragraph{Coverage}
\label{sec:bg_coverage}
Fitness gives partial credit. A trace with one deviation among six events still
reaches a trace fitness of about 0.8. Coverage measures ask a stricter question
about whole cases. They count whether the model accepts the complete sequence of
a case or not. When simulation is the goal, coverage is essential. A sequence
that the model does not accept can never be generated by a simulator built on
this model, and the behaviour missing from a simulated log is exactly what
simulation-guided repair has to add afterwards \cite{SimulationGuidedRepair2025}.
This thesis uses three coverage measures.
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt]
\item \textbf{Share of fitting cases.} The share of the traces of the event log
that the model replays without any deviation. It is the simplest fitness measure
\cite{Aalst2016}. It counts every case, so frequent sequences weigh more.
\item \textbf{Share of accepted sequences.} The share of the distinct sequences
(variants) of the event log that the model accepts, each counted once. It
measures how much of the observed behaviour the model covers, regardless of how
often each sequence occurs.
\item \textbf{Coverage per group.} The share of fitting cases within a group of
cases. Groups are formed from the attributes or resources of the cases, for
example the visits that use two RMG blocks. A dominant variant can hide that a
smaller but regular group is not accepted \cite{RWNorouzifar2026Rules}.
\end{itemize}
A fourth, outside view uses a hold-out log, that is, data that the discovery has
not seen. It counts how many of the sequences that occur only in the hold-out log
the model accepts. This measures generalisation directly.

When a few sequences make up most of the cases, the measures can differ a lot. A
model that accepts only the few frequent sequences fits almost all cases but
accepts only a small share of the sequences. For a simulator the unweighted view
matters as well, because a sequence that the model does not accept can never be
generated.

High coverage is not the same as overfitting. A model \emph{overfits} if it
allows only the observed sequences and fails on new ones. It \emph{underfits} if
it allows much more than the log shows \cite{Aalst2016}. Precision measures the
second error. The first can only be seen on a hold-out log, which is why the
outside view matters. How a noise threshold decides between noise and rare
regular behaviour is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:bg_algorithms}.

Fitness, precision and coverage compare the language of a model with the
observed traces. Coverage shows which observed behaviour a simulator built on the
model could never generate. None of the measures says how often a simulator will
generate each permitted sequence. A path that the model permits but that never
occurs in the log is rare in the language, but a simulator can still generate it
often. The selected nets are therefore also given to the simulator for a first
run, before the actual simulation experiments analyse the model in detail
(Section~\ref{sec:exp_baseline}).

\section{Business Process Simulation}
\label{sec:bps_models}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Business process simulation (BPS) runs an executable model of a process to
study its behaviour without disturbing the real system \cite{Aalst2015}. A
BPS model consists of five components, which it uses to generate a simulated
event log \cite{Aalst2015,PMRozinat2009}.
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt,topsep=4pt]
\item An \emph{arrival process} decides when new cases start.
\item A \emph{control-flow model} decides which activities can follow each other.
This is the Petri net of Section~\ref{sec:bg_discovery}, together with
\emph{routing} probabilities or rules at each choice.
\item \emph{Resources} have a capacity and working calendars.
\item \emph{Time models} give the execution and waiting times of activities.
\item \emph{Case attributes} describe each case.
\end{itemize}
Most BPS tools use discrete-event simulation. The simulation clock does not run
continuously. It jumps from one event to the next, for example from the arrival
of a truck to the start of its first stop. Arrivals, routing decisions and
durations are drawn at random from the learned distributions. One simulation run
is therefore one possible course of events, and a second run gives a slightly
different one. The random draws are controlled by a \emph{seed}, a start number
for the random number generator. The same seed reproduces exactly the same run,
and different seeds give different runs. Several runs with different seeds,
called \emph{replications}, show how much the results vary
\cite{Law2015Simulation}. Simulation is mainly used for \emph{what-if analysis}.
The same model is run with and without a change, for example more resources or
another allocation rule, and the KPIs of the two runs are compared
\cite{Aalst2015}.

\paragraph{Discovering simulation models}
Rozinat et al.\ showed early that all five components can be extracted from an
event log \cite{PMRozinat2009}. Each component can be configured, for example by
the choice of a distribution for the execution time of an activity. Together,
these choices form the \emph{configuration} of the simulation model, and running
the configured model produces a simulated event log. Discovering a simulation
model therefore means finding the configuration whose simulated log is as close
as possible to the real one. Camargo et al.\ automate this search
\cite{Camargo2020}. L\'opez-Pintado and Dumas show that modelling resources
individually, with their own calendars and performance, brings the simulated
case durations closer to the real ones \cite{BPSdifferentiatedResources2022}.
Stochastic process models add probabilities to the paths of a model
\cite{stochastikPetriNets2013Solti}. Deep-learning generators can learn more
complex dependencies between the activities, attributes and times of a case,
but they are hard to inspect and to change for a scenario \cite{Camargo2021}.

\paragraph{Data-aware simulation}
In a classical BPS model, each choice in the control flow has fixed branching
probabilities, for example 70\,\% of the cases take one path and 30\,\% the
other, the same for every case. A \emph{data-aware} model lets routing, times or
resource choice depend on data, for example on case attributes or on the
current workload. Mannhardt et al.\ define stochastic Petri nets whose transition
weights depend on data \cite{mannhardt2023stochasticProcessesModelling}. Such a
net is called a stochastic labelled data Petri net. De Leoni et al.\ add the
history of the case to this \emph{process state} and show that the best state
depends on the process \cite{Vinci2023Inf.Activ.Prob}. L\'opez-Pintado et al.\
also model how attributes change during a case \cite{lopez2024}. ProSiT, the
simulation framework used in this thesis (Section~\ref{sec:prosit_background}),
applies data awareness to all components of the model. It learns probabilistic
decision trees for the arrivals, the routing, the resources and the times, and
it uses the routing trees as data-dependent weights on the transitions of the
Petri net.

\paragraph{Queues and physical resources}
Queues and shared resources influence the execution of a process through the
interaction of different cases. Discovered simulation models are weakest
exactly there. Among the specific risks of BPS, van der Aalst lists models that
ignore the process context, such as the number of cases that compete for the
same resources, and working speeds that depend on the workload \cite{Aalst2015}.
In the review of Sulis et al., process mining supports arrivals, durations and
the control flow well. The modelling tasks around queues and resources receive
limited or no support, partly because the data do not record them
\cite{Sulis2026SimulationReview}. Queue mining takes a queueing perspective on
event logs and predicts the delay of a case from the congestion it meets
\cite{Senderovich2015QueueMining}. In manufacturing, simulation models are
generated from event logs of the material flow. The structure is discovered from
the data, while physical elements such as buffers and capacities are added and
calibrated explicitly \cite{LugaresiMatta2021,Cimino2025ASMGReview}. Grobis and
Ihlenfeldt note that logs at the business level are usually too coarse for
queues, which need data from the operational level
\cite{Grobis2025ProductionLogistics}. RIMS computes queue features during the
simulation and simulates waiting times better where resources are congested
\cite{runtimeIntegration2024}.

\paragraph{Validation}
\label{sec:simulation_validation_background}
A simulation model consists of the discovered control flow combined with the
learned components, such as the routing weights and the time distributions. It
is valid only for an intended use \cite{Sargent2013Validation}. Sargent
separates \emph{conceptual validity} (are the assumptions made by the model
reasonable for the purpose?), \emph{verification} (is the model implemented
correctly?), \emph{data validity} and \emph{operational validity} (is the output
accurate enough for the purpose?). For discovered BPS models, operational
validity is usually checked by comparing logs generated by the model with a real
log that was not used for discovery. Chapela-Campa et al.\ recommend separate
measures for the control flow, the timing and the congestion of the generated
logs, because one aggregate score hides which component of the model is wrong
\cite{ChapelaCampa2023Trust,Chapela2025SimQuality}. Özdemir et al.\ add two
cautions \cite{Oezdemir2025RethinkingBPS}. A later test log mixes the error of
the model with changes of the process between the periods, and distances based
on the Wasserstein distance can hide temporal patterns.

Law recommends \emph{trace-driven} comparisons, in which the model receives the
observed inputs of the comparison period, for example the observed arrivals, so
that model and system face the same demand \cite{Law2015Simulation}. A comparison
with a historical log shows that the model reproduces the current process. It
does not show that the model predicts the effect of a change correctly. When the
model is used for what-if scenarios, its reliability has to be shown more
thoroughly. Its mechanisms have to be plausible, it has to respond to load as the
real system does, and people who know the process have to find it credible.
Validation by domain experts is a recognised technique
\cite{Sargent2013Validation}. It can take several forms, for example a review of
the model logic or of the simulated results. Domain rules that experts define
before the modelling serve a similar purpose, because they bring expert
knowledge into the model in advance.

\paragraph{Replications}
\label{sec:stochastic_replication_ci}
Because one run is only one possible course of events, a model is run several
times with different seeds. The mean over the runs is reported together with a
95\,\% confidence interval, which shows how much the result varies between runs
\cite{Law2015Simulation}. This interval describes the randomness of the
simulator for one fitted model. It does not cover the uncertainty of the fitted
parameters or of the model structure.

\section{ProSiT}
\label{sec:prosit_background}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

ProSiT (\textit{PROcess SImulation Tool}) discovers a data-aware simulation
model from a Petri net and an event log \cite{Vinci2026prosit,ProSiTRepository}.
Every component of the model is learned as a \emph{probabilistic decision tree},
a decision tree whose leaves hold a probability or a distribution instead of a
single value. The model is considered a white-box model, because every learned
rule can be inspected and edited, for example to create controlled what-if
scenarios. This section describes what ProSiT learns, how a tree is built and
used, how a simulated case runs, and where the framework stops.

\subsection{Inputs and Learned Components}
\label{sec:prosit_components}

ProSiT needs a sound workflow net and an event log with start and complete
timestamps per activity, a resource per event and static case attributes. Static
means that the attributes describe the case as a whole and do not change while
the case is executed, like the age of a patient or the number of planned stops
of a truck.

ProSiT first aligns every trace of the log with the net. In simple terms, it
replays each recorded case on the net, step by step. For every event, this
replay tells which transition fired, which other transitions could have fired at
that moment, and since when the transition had been possible. This is how
ProSiT learns about decisions that the log itself does not show, for example
the choices at silent transitions. From this replay, ProSiT builds one training
table per component and learns one tree from each table
(Figure~\ref{fig:bg_prosit_pipeline}). The components roughly follow the BPS
components of Section~\ref{sec:bps_models}, but every one of them is a
probabilistic decision tree that can depend on the case and on the state of the
process. ProSiT offers hyperparameters that steer this discovery, for example
the largest depth of a tree (Section~\ref{sec:prosit_hyperparameters}).
Table~\ref{tab:prosit_components} in Appendix~\ref{app:prosit_inventory} lists
every component with its features.

A few components need a short explanation. The description follows the paper
and the implementation of version 1.0.4 \cite{Vinci2026prosit,ProSiTRepository}.
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=3pt,topsep=4pt]
\item \textbf{Arrivals.} The gaps between consecutive case starts are
computed in open minutes of the arrival calendar. The calendar marks a weekday
and hour as open if enough arrivals fall into it. It decides only \emph{whether}
cases can arrive, and the tree decides \emph{how often}.
\item \textbf{Routing.} For every transition, ProSiT collects all moments in
the replay at which it was enabled. The class is 1 if it fired and 0 if another
transition fired. The tree then predicts the probability that the transition
fires, given the case attributes and how often each activity has occurred so
far. These features describe the case, not the marking of the net. Silent
transitions get a routing tree like visible ones, and when they fired is known
only from the alignment. In the simulation, these probabilities act as the
data-dependent weights of the transitions.
\item \textbf{Capacity.} By default, every resource handles one activity at a
time. ProSiT offers a hyperparameter that allows a resource to handle several
activities at once (multitasking). If it is switched on, a resource whose
activities overlap in at least a given share of cases receives a capacity equal
to the largest observed overlap plus one.
\item \textbf{Waiting time.} The waiting time is the time that a case waits
before an activity starts, although the control flow already allows it. ProSiT
measures it from the later of two moments. The first is the moment the previous
activity ended and the transition became enabled. The second is the moment the
resource became free. Waiting caused by a busy resource is therefore not part of
what the tree learns. The simulator produces this waiting itself, through the
capacity check.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Probabilistic Decision Trees}
\label{sec:prosit_trees}

A decision tree \cite{Vinci2026prosit} splits the training data step by step on
one feature at a time, for example ``hour $\le$ 21''. Each split is chosen so
that the groups become as different as possible in the target (CART). The groups
at the end are the \emph{leaves}. In ProSiT, a routing leaf stores a probability.
A time leaf stores a distribution. ProSiT fits a fixed value and a normal,
exponential, lognormal, gamma and uniform distribution to the values in the leaf
and keeps the one closest to the data in Wasserstein distance. Values drawn from
it are limited to the smallest and largest observed value.

\paragraph{Model selection}
A tree is not always better than one distribution for all cases. ProSiT
therefore chooses by five-fold cross-validation. It tries every tree depth
from one to \texttt{max\_depth\_tree} and every minimum leaf size in
\texttt{min\_samples\_leaf\_cv}, and compares them with a single distribution
without splits. For routing, the score is the log loss of the predicted
probabilities. For time models, version 1.0.4 uses the continuous ranked
probability score (CRPS). For an observed value $y$ and a predicted
distribution $F$,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{CRPS}(F,y)=\mathbb{E}\,|X-y|-\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}\,|X-X'|,
\end{equation}
where $X$ and $X'$ are independent draws from $F$ \cite{Gneiting2007CRPS}. The
first term rewards distributions close to the observation, and the second term
rewards spread where the data are spread. A lower score is better. Version 1.0.3
used the Wasserstein distance between training and test values inside each leaf.
That criterion measures only how stable a leaf is, so it almost always chose a
single distribution. In practice, version 1.0.4 keeps a tree wherever splitting
the cases predicts the values better than one distribution for all of them.
Version 1.0.3 used one distribution almost everywhere, so that waiting and
execution times could not depend on the load or the attributes of a case. This
thesis uses version 1.0.4 (Section~\ref{sec:sim_settings}).

\paragraph{Example}
Figure~\ref{fig:bg_prosit_arrival_tree} shows the arrival tree that ProSiT
learned from the training data of this thesis. It is read from the top. If the
previous truck arrived on a weekday between 5 and 21~h, the gap to the next
truck is drawn from a normal distribution, and the drawn gaps average 0.65
minutes, about 90 trucks per hour. At night, the mean gap is about two minutes.
On Saturday after 10~h, it is 7.7 minutes. The tree has depth three, so it cannot
separate the morning ramp-up, the daytime plateau and the evening. The leaf from
5 to 21~h pools them. Whether this is good enough is tested in the validation of
the simulation model (Section~\ref{sec:exp_e4_runs}).

\subsection{Execution of a Simulated Case}
\label{sec:prosit_execution}

The simulator processes all cases together and always continues with the case
whose next step is enabled earliest. A case runs as follows.
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt,topsep=4pt]
\item \textbf{Arrival.} The start time is the start of the previous case plus a
gap drawn from the arrival tree, counted in open minutes of the arrival
calendar.
\item \textbf{Attributes.} The case receives its attributes, in empirical mode
as one complete set of attributes of a randomly drawn training case.
\item \textbf{Enabled transitions.} The token sits in the source place. A
transition is enabled if all its input places hold a token. Its enabling time
is the latest time stamp of these tokens.
\item \textbf{Routing.} Each enabled transition gets the probability from its
routing tree, given the case attributes and the activities so far. The
simulator draws one transition in proportion to these weights. Silent
transitions are drawn in the same way and take no time.
\item \textbf{Resource.} For a visible transition, the candidate resources are
those that executed this activity in the log. Of these, the ones with free
capacity at the enabling time are kept, and one is drawn from the resource
trees. If none is free, the resource that becomes free first is taken and the
start moves to that moment.
\item \textbf{Times.} A waiting time is drawn from the tree of the resource
(zero for the first activity of a case). The start is the enabling time plus
the waiting time. The complete time is the start plus an execution time drawn
from the tree of the activity. Both are counted in open minutes of the
resource calendar.
\item \textbf{Token update.} The tokens move to the output places and carry the
complete time. Steps 3 to 7 repeat until the final marking is reached.
\end{enumerate}
The generated log contains, for each event, the case, activity, resource,
enabling, start and complete time and the case attributes. The firing sequence
of each case is stored as well.

\label{sec:prosit_hyperparameters}
ProSiT offers a few hyperparameters that steer the discovery. They set the
largest tree depth and the leaf sizes tried in cross-validation, whether
workload features and multitasking are used, and whether case attributes are
drawn as complete observed sets or one by one. Their defaults, the values used in

\subsection{Evidence and Limitations}
\label{sec:prosit_limits}

The ProSiT paper evaluates the framework on five case studies with a temporal
80/20 split and five simulated logs per model. ProSiT reaches an accuracy
comparable to a black-box baseline while its rules stay readable
\cite{Vinci2026prosit}. The authors also stress a boundary. The trees capture
associations in the training data, not causes. A readable rule shows which
association the simulator uses. Whether it holds after an intervention needs
other evidence.

For this thesis, seven properties of the implementation of version 1.0.4 matter
\cite{ProSiTRepository}.
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt,topsep=4pt]
\item \textbf{No hard rules.} A transition can fire whenever its input places
hold tokens and its weight is above zero. ProSiT has no guards. A rule that
the net does not encode, such as ``at least one stop'' or ``as many stops as
planned'', is followed only as often as the routing trees happen to respect it.
\item \textbf{No look-ahead.} Each routing draw is local. A sequence of likely
steps can lead to a visit that never occurs in the data.
\item \textbf{Parallel branches run in parallel.} A silent parallel split
enables several stops at the same time, and the simulator can start them at
the same time.
\item \textbf{Capacity at enabling time.} The capacity of a resource is checked
when the transition is enabled, not over the interval in which the activity
actually runs.
\item \textbf{Load enters through few features.} Workload features act on the
resource choice and the waiting time only. Execution times do not react to the
load of the terminal.
\item \textbf{The queue feature counts the activity itself.} In training, the
queue length of a resource counts the activities that are enabled and not yet
started. An activity that waited is therefore counted in the queue of the
resource that performed it, but not in the queues of the other candidates. In
the simulation the activity is not yet in any queue when the resource is
chosen.
\item \textbf{Waiting is measured against one server.} The waiting time is
measured from the moment the resource was last completely free, that is, from
the latest end of all its activities in progress at the enabling time. For a
resource that serves several cases side by side, a lane may already be free much
earlier.
\end{enumerate}
The first three properties make the control-flow model more important for a
simulator than for a descriptive model. The rule check of the discovered nets
and the first simulation of the selected net address them
(Sections~\ref{sec:exp_rules} and~\ref{sec:exp_baseline}). Properties four to
seven concern the physical constraints of the blocks. They are examined in the
validation of the simulation model (Section~\ref{sec:exp_mechanisms}).

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Research Gap and Positioning}
\label{sec:rw_positioning}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

The reviewed research leaves three gaps for a terminal like CTB. First, discovered models are usually selected by fitness
and precision \cite{Buijs2014Quality,DiscoveryBenchmark}. These measures do not
show what a simulator does with behaviour that the model permits but the log
never shows, and physical rules are not part of the discovered model. Second,
data-aware simulation discovery has been evaluated mainly on administrative and
healthcare processes. Its behaviour in a physical logistics system with shared
capacity and congestion is largely untested. The assumptions that decide it,
namely how queues, capacity and load enter the learned models, are exactly those
that process mining supports least. Third, validation of discovered simulation
models focuses on similarity to a historical log. Whether the model responds to
load like the real system, which is needed before a what-if analysis, is rarely
tested, and the judgement of domain experts is rarely documented.

Queue mining \cite{Senderovich2015QueueMining}, data-driven simulation in
manufacturing \cite{LugaresiMatta2021} and the validation literature
\cite{Sargent2013Validation} motivate a combination of learned behaviour,
explicit domain assumptions and simulation validation. The CTB case examines
whether this combination provides a model suitable for assessing a proposed
change in block allocation. The comparison of discovered nets establishes a
justified model for the case. The performance evaluation determines how well it
represents the quantities relevant to the decision. The scenario analysis is
then interpreted within those limits.

The case provides access to operational records and a concrete decision
context. It also has limitations. The log does not directly expose every
resource state, domain assumptions require scrutiny, and the data contain no
implementation of the proposed intervention. The study therefore contributes
evidence about this application of ProSiT. Mechanisms identified here are
candidates for investigation in other settings, rather than demonstrated
requirements for all capacity-limited processes.

This thesis addresses these gaps in a case study of truck operations at HHLA
Container Terminal Burchardkai. It does not propose a new discovery algorithm or
a new simulator. It compares discovery algorithms for their use in simulation,
checks the discovered nets against physical rules, and measures how the
control-flow model shapes the simulated visits. It then validates the simulation
mechanisms and their response to load, and examines one operational policy
within the limits that these checks establish.
