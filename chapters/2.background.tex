\section{Container Terminal Truck Operations}
\label{sec:bg_terminal}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{hints}
\item About two pages. One small figure of a truck visit (Gate In, stops at RMG block / LL / HO2, Gate Out) would help. CTB numbers (blocks, lanes, volumes) stay in Chapter~\ref{chp:case_study_and_data}.
\end{hints}

A container terminal connects ships, trains and trucks. Containers are stored
in a yard between the waterside and the landside. In many terminals, including
the one studied here, the yard consists of storage blocks. Automated
rail-mounted gantry cranes (RMG) move containers inside a block and hand them
over to trucks at the landside end of the block. Terminals often have further
areas, for example a depot for empty containers and a manually operated area.
Trucks enter and leave through a gate \cite{CTSteenken2004}.

\paragraph{The truck visit}
A truck visit starts when the truck passes the entry gate (\emph{Gate In}). The
truck then drives to one or more handling areas. At each \emph{stop}, a
container is delivered to the terminal, received from it, or both. A visit that
delivers and receives in one trip is called a double cycle or \emph{mixed}
visit. After the last stop, the truck leaves through the exit gate (\emph{Gate
Out}). Most visits have one stop. Visits with two or more stops are less
frequent but take longer.

\paragraph{Physical constraints}
A truck visit is a physical process. Several constraints follow from this and
hold for every visit, whether or not they are visible in the recorded data.
\begin{itemize}
\item A truck is one vehicle. Its stops happen one after another and never at
the same time.
\item A visit exists to move containers. A visit without any stop between the
gates does not occur in practice.
\item A storage block has a limited number of handover lanes and cranes. Trucks
share them with each other and with the waterside and rail operations.
\item A truck can carry a limited number of containers.
\end{itemize}
These constraints run through the whole thesis. A model that is learned only
from event frequencies does not know them. Section~\ref{sec:bg_rules} shows how
such constraints can be written down as rules, and Section~\ref{sec:prosit_limits}
explains why the simulator needs them explicitly.

\paragraph{Key performance indicators}
Truck operations are measured with key performance indicators (KPIs). The
\emph{truck turnaround time} (TTT) is the time from Gate In to Gate Out. Waiting
times isolate the delay before a gate or a yard stop, and utilisation measures
describe how much of the yard and the equipment is in use. TTT is the main
measure of service quality for truck handling, because it is the time that
trucking companies and cargo owners experience. It is one of the four main
KPIs of the HHLA container terminals \cite{Bett2024,CTprocessMiningTAS2020}.
The mean alone hides the long waits that cause complaints. This thesis
therefore also reports the 90th percentile (P90) of TTT and the number of hours
in which TTT is unusually high (Section~\ref{sec:exp_kpis}).

\paragraph{Related work: simulation in container terminals}
Terminal managers decide about truck appointments, equipment, staff and yard
operations while the terminal is running. Because the processes are
connected, a local change can affect waiting times and service elsewhere.
Simulation allows such changes to be tested without disturbing live
operations. Discrete-event simulation (DES) is the most common approach,
because the terminal can be described by events such as arrivals, gate
passages and container moves
\cite{YUN1999DESSimulation,Bruzzone2012,Muravev2019,Carboni2025}. Studies use
simulation to compare equipment numbers and operating policies
\cite{Kastner2021}, to generate scenarios \cite{Hartmann2005cenariosforCTSimulation},
and to evaluate truck appointment systems and double cycles
\cite{CTprocessMiningTAS2020,Bett2024}. These models are built by hand. This
needs domain knowledge, much manual work, and recalibration when operations
change. A hand-built model may also describe how the process is meant to work
rather than how it actually runs \cite{PMRozinat2009}.

\paragraph{Related work: process mining in logistics}
Process mining has been applied to logistics, mostly to discover and analyse
processes rather than to build executable simulation models
\cite{RWAlnahas2023Logistics}. Reconstructing a logistics process from several
information systems requires care, because timestamps and identifiers from
different systems may describe different operational stages
\cite{RWBecker2017Context}. Grobis and Ihlenfeldt use event data to prepare a
simulation model for production logistics and split the time between two
activities into transport and waiting
\cite{Grobis2025ProductionLogistics,Grobis2026SimulationDevelopment}. In a
hospital, Vinci et al.\ discover a simulation model from an event log, check it
against observed behaviour and only then use it to compare resource
configurations \cite{Hospital2026Vinci}. This thesis follows the same order.

\paragraph{Implication for this thesis}
An adequate simulation of the truck process must reproduce TTT, its tail and
its growth under load. It must also respect the physical constraints listed
above, because a policy that changes the load on the blocks is only credible
if the simulated trucks behave like real trucks.

\section{Event Logs}
\label{sec:operational_data_event_logs}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Process mining works on \emph{event logs}. An event is one recorded
occurrence of an activity. Each event belongs to a \emph{case}, the object
whose process is followed. In this thesis, one truck visit is one case. Each
event records at least the case, the \emph{activity} (for example
\texttt{Gate In} or \texttt{RMG\_receive}) and a timestamp. An event log groups
the events by case and keeps their order. The ordered list of activities of a
case is its \emph{trace}. Cases with the same trace share a \emph{variant}. The
set of variants is the observed language of the log
\cite{Aalst2016}. Table~\ref{tab:eventlogexample} shows two simplified
truck visits.

\paragraph{Timestamps}
Simulation needs more than the order of events. When an event has a
\emph{start} and a \emph{complete} timestamp, their difference is its
execution time. A third point in time, \emph{enablement}, is the moment at
which the process model allows the activity to begin. It is not recorded. It is
reconstructed by replaying the log on the process model
\cite{PMRozinat2009,Camargo2020,Vinci2026prosit}. The time between enablement
and start is called waiting time. In a physical process, this interval can
contain queueing, driving and other delays. The meaning of each timestamp must
therefore be checked. In the terminal data, a stop starts when the truck
reports ready in the handover lane. The stop interval can already contain
waiting for the crane (Section~\ref{sec:timestamp_semantics}).

\paragraph{Attributes}
Further attributes describe the case and its context, for example the number of
containers, the planned stops or the load of the terminal at Gate In. A
data-aware simulation may only use an attribute at a decision point if its
value is known at that point. Otherwise the model uses information from the
future, which is called leakage (Section~\ref{sec:entry_known_attributes}).

Event logs are exchanged between tools in the XES format \cite{Aalst2016}.
In this thesis, the same log is read by ProM \cite{vanDongen2005ProM} and by
PM4Py \cite{Berti2023PM4Py}.

\section{Process Discovery and Petri Nets}
\label{sec:bg_discovery}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Process discovery builds a process model from an event log
\cite{Aalst2016,Aalst2022Foundations}. The model shows how the process
was executed, not how it was planned. ProSiT, the simulator used in this
thesis, needs the model as a Petri net. This section introduces Petri nets,
the discovery algorithms compared in E1, and the use of rules to restrict a
discovered model.

\subsection{Petri Nets, Workflow Nets and Silent Transitions}
\label{sec:bg_petri}

A Petri net is a graph of \emph{places} (circles) and \emph{transitions}
(boxes), connected by arcs \cite{Murata1989,Aalst2016}. Formally, a
labelled Petri net is a tuple $N=(P,T,F,\lambda)$. $P$ is a finite set of
places, $T$ a finite set of transitions, and
$F\subseteq(P\times T)\cup(T\times P)$ the set of arcs. The labelling function
$\lambda: T\rightarrow A\cup\{\tau\}$ gives each transition either an activity
of the event log or the label $\tau$. The places with an arc to a transition
$t$ are its input places ${\bullet}t$. The places with an arc from $t$ are its
output places $t{\bullet}$.

The state of a net is a \emph{marking}: a number of tokens in each place. A
transition is \emph{enabled} when each of its input places holds a token.
Firing it removes one token from each input place and adds one token to each
output place. A choice is a place with several outgoing arcs: only one of the
enabled transitions can take the token. Parallelism is a transition with
several output places: it creates one token per branch, and the branches can
then fire independently.

A \emph{workflow net} has one source place $i$ and one sink place $o$, and
every node lies on a path from $i$ to $o$. A run starts with one token in $i$
and should end with one token in $o$. For a truck visit, the token in $i$ is the
truck before the gate and the token in $o$ the completed visit. A workflow net
is \emph{sound} if three conditions hold \cite{Aalst2016}. First, from
every reachable marking the final marking can still be reached (option to
complete). Second, when $o$ is marked, no other place holds a token (proper
completion). Third, every transition can fire in some run (no dead
transitions). Soundness matters for simulation, because the simulator executes
the net and must bring every case to the end.

Transitions labelled $\tau$ are \emph{silent}. They fire like other
transitions but leave no event in the log. They express routing that has no
recorded counterpart: skipping an activity, repeating a loop, or splitting and
joining parallel branches. The \emph{language} of a workflow net is the set of
visible activity sequences of all complete runs. The net in
Figure~\ref{fig:bg_petri_silent} accepts both \texttt{Gate In, RMG\_receive,
Gate Out} and \texttt{Gate In, Gate Out}.

Silent transitions are a means of representation. Two nets with very different
numbers of silent transitions can have the same language. Discovery algorithms
add them systematically. The Inductive Miner, for example, turns an optional
activity into a silent skip and a parallel block into a silent split and join
\cite{Leemans2014InductiveMiner}. They have three consequences in this thesis.
First, combinations of silent skips can admit behaviour that never occurs,
such as the visit without a stop in Figure~\ref{fig:bg_petri_silent}. Because
silent paths leave no trace, this is easy to overlook. Second, alignments
(Section~\ref{sec:astar_alignment_definition}) must decide for every silent
transition whether to fire it, which enlarges the search. Third, a simulator
has to decide them as well, although they leave no trace in the training data
(Section~\ref{sec:prosit_limits}).

\paragraph{State machines}
A \emph{state machine} is a Petri net in which every transition has exactly
one input place and one output place \cite{Murata1989}. A state machine
workflow net therefore always holds exactly one token. Each place is a state of
the process, and each transition a step from one state to the next. A state
machine cannot express parallelism. For a truck, this is not a loss: its stops
never run in parallel. Every finite set of sequences, and more generally every
language accepted by a finite automaton, can be written as a sound state
machine workflow net. Each automaton state becomes a place and each automaton
edge a visible transition. Among the deterministic automata that accept a
language there is a unique smallest one, up to renaming of states
\cite{Hopcroft2006Automata}. Its state machine is therefore a canonical
representation of the language: two nets with the same language give the same
state machine. This encoding is used in E2 (Section~\ref{sec:exp_rules}).

In such an encoding, the same activity label usually appears on several
transitions, one for each state in which the activity can happen. These are
called \emph{duplicate labels}. The four discovery families compared in E1
create one visible transition per activity. For ProSiT the difference matters,
because it learns one routing tree per transition
(Section~\ref{sec:prosit_components}). With duplicate labels, the routing of
an activity can therefore differ from state to state.

\subsection{Discovery Algorithms}
\label{sec:bg_algorithms}

Discovery algorithms differ in how they turn observed sequences into a model.
Experiment E1 compares four families.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Alpha family.} The Alpha Miner derives ordering relations
(directly follows, causality, parallelism, choice) from the log and builds
places from them \cite{vanderAalst2004AlphaMiner}. Alpha+ and Alpha++ add short
loops and non-local dependencies \cite{deMedeiros2004AlphaPlus,Wen2007AlphaPlusPlus}.
The algorithms do not handle noise and do not guarantee a sound net.
\item \textbf{Heuristics Miner.} The Heuristics Miner counts how often one
activity follows another and keeps a dependency when its dependency measure
exceeds a threshold \cite{heuristicsMiner2006,Weijters2011FHM}. The interactive
data-aware variant (iDHM) can also keep infrequent dependencies that depend on
data, and converts the resulting causal net into a Petri net
\cite{Mannhardt2017DHM,Mannhardt2017iDHM}. The result is not guaranteed to be
sound.
\item \textbf{Inductive Miner.} The Inductive Miner splits the log
recursively by sequence, choice, parallel and loop cuts. The result is a
process tree, which is converted into a sound workflow net. The variant IMf
first removes infrequent behaviour below a noise threshold
\cite{Leemans2014InductiveMiner}.
\item \textbf{ILP Miner.} Region-based miners add a place for every linear
inequality that all prefixes of the observed traces satisfy. The ILP Miner
finds these places with integer linear programming. A filter on the prefixes
controls how much infrequent behaviour is kept \cite{RWVanZelst2018ILP}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Related work}
Benchmarks show that no algorithm is best on every log and every quality
dimension \cite{DiscoveryBenchmark}. Rehse et al.\ argue that
discovery experiments need explicit assumptions and a controlled design: the
same input, the same cohorts and the same implementation of each measure
\cite{RWRehse2024Experimentation}. E1 follows this design. A further point
concerns time. Two activities that occur in either order are not necessarily
parallel; they may simply be ordered freely. Lifecycle-aware discovery uses
start and complete timestamps to make this distinction
\cite{RWLeemans2016Lifecycle}. For trucks it matters: two stops may occur in
either order, but never at the same time.

\subsection{Domain Rules and Model Repair}
\label{sec:bg_rules}

A discovered model generalises beyond the observed traces. Part of this
generalisation can contradict knowledge that is known independently of the
data, for example the physical constraints of Section~\ref{sec:bg_terminal}.
Such knowledge can be written down as rules.

\paragraph{Rules as automata}
Many rules on activity sequences can be written as Declare constraints, for
example ``Gate In occurs exactly once'' or ``mixed and delivery do not both
occur at the same area'' \cite{RWDiCiccio2022Declarative}. Every Declare
constraint can be translated into a finite automaton that accepts exactly the
sequences satisfying it \cite{RWDeGiacomo2013LTLf,RWMaggi2011Monitoring}. A
sound workflow net has a finite reachability graph, so its language is also
accepted by a finite automaton. Finite automata can be combined: the product of
two automata accepts exactly the sequences that both accept. Running the net
and the rule automata in one product therefore answers two questions exactly.
Does the net accept a sequence that breaks a rule? And which sequences of the
net satisfy all rules? The product follows the same idea as the synchronous
product used for alignments \cite{RWCarmona2022Conformance}. A net combined
with declarative constraints in this way corresponds to a hybrid process model
\cite{RWSlaats2016Hybrid}.

Not every rule is a rule on sequences. ``Stops never overlap'' is a rule on
timestamps. ``The number of stops equals the number of planned stops'' depends
on a case attribute. Such rules cannot be written in the untimed language of a
net. In data-aware Petri nets they become \emph{guards}: conditions on data
that a transition must satisfy before it may fire
\cite{Mannhardt2016Precision}. With guards, soundness also depends on the data:
every case must still be able to complete under the guards
\cite{FelliDeLeoniMontali2021}.

\paragraph{Related work: domain knowledge in discovery and repair}
Schuster et al.\ classify how domain knowledge enters process discovery
\cite{schuster2022}. Knowledge can be used before discovery, during
discovery, or \emph{a posteriori} on a discovered model. It can be explicit
or come from user feedback, and it can be applied automatically or
interactively. Explicit knowledge is written in one of two kinds of formalism.
An \emph{imperative} formalism, such as a Petri net, follows a closed-world
assumption: only the behaviour it describes is allowed. A \emph{declarative}
formalism, such as Declare, follows an open-world assumption: everything is
allowed that does not break one of its constraints \cite{schuster2022}. This
thesis uses both. The discovered net is imperative, the domain rules are
declarative, and the repair keeps only the behaviour that both allow. Schuster
et al.\ also classify approaches by the formalism of the output model. Here
the output must be a sound workflow net, because ProSiT executes Petri nets
and learns its routing per transition (Section~\ref{sec:prosit_background}).

Greco et al.\ enforce precedence constraints
during discovery \cite{RWGreco2015Precedence}. Norouzifar et al.\ integrate
rules into the cuts of the Inductive Miner \cite{RWNorouzifar2026Rules}. Dixit
et al.\ work a posteriori: they edit a discovered model at random and keep the
candidates that satisfy user-specified Declare constraints best
\cite{RWDixit2015Domain}. Revoredo discusses how domain knowledge can guide
the repair of a process model \cite{Revoredo2023DomainRepair}.

Process enhancement changes a discovered model in two ways: \emph{repair}
changes the model so that it reflects reality better, and \emph{extension} adds
a perspective such as data, time or resources \cite{Aalst2016,RWdeLeoni2022Enhancement}.
Classical model repair changes a model so that it can replay every trace of a
log while staying close to the original \cite{RWFahland2015Repair}.
This adds behaviour. The opposite direction removes behaviour that the log
does not support, for example by adding non-local places to a Petri net
\cite{RWKalenkova2020NonLocal} or by refining process trees
\cite{RWRennert2024Precision}. Vinci and de Leoni repair a model for
simulation: they simulate it, find behaviour that is missing in the generated
log and add it \cite{SimulationGuidedRepair2025}. Restricting behaviour that
the simulator generates too often is named as future work.

\paragraph{Implication for this thesis}
E2 uses explicit, declarative domain knowledge a posteriori and fully
automatically: sequence rules restrict the language, data rules become guards
(Section~\ref{sec:exp_rules}).

\section{Conformance Checking}
\label{sec:bg_conformance}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Conformance checking compares a log with a model
\cite{aalst2012ConformanceChecking,RWCarmona2022Conformance}. Four quality
dimensions are common \cite{Buijs2014Quality}. \emph{Fitness} measures how much
of the observed behaviour the model can replay. \emph{Precision} penalises
behaviour that the model allows but the log does not show.
\emph{Generalisation} rewards a model that is likely to accept new, unseen
traces of the same process. \emph{Simplicity} prefers small models. The
dimensions pull in different directions. A model that accepts every sequence
has perfect fitness and poor precision; a model that lists every observed trace
has perfect precision and poor generalisation.

\paragraph{Alignments}
\label{sec:astar_alignment_definition}
An alignment relates the trace of a case to a complete run of the model. It is
a sequence of moves of three types. A \emph{synchronous move} consumes an
event and fires a transition with the same label. A \emph{log move} consumes an
event that the model cannot follow. A \emph{model move} fires a transition
without a matching event. Model moves on silent transitions are free. The
other deviations have a positive cost. An optimal alignment $\gamma^{\star}$ of
a trace $\sigma$ with a net $N$ is
\begin{equation}
\gamma^{\star}=\arg\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\sigma,N)} c(\gamma),
\end{equation}
where $\Gamma(\sigma,N)$ is the set of all alignments and $c$ the total cost
\cite{aalst2012ConformanceChecking,Adriansyah2011CostFitness}. Optimal
alignments are usually computed with the A* search algorithm on the product of
the trace and the net. With an admissible heuristic, the first complete
alignment found is optimal \cite{Adriansyah2011CostFitness}.

\paragraph{Fitness}
Trace fitness is one minus the cost of the optimal alignment divided by the
cost of the worst case, in which every event is a log move and the model runs
its shortest path alone. Log fitness averages over all traces. A trace
\emph{fits} if its optimal alignment has no deviation.

\paragraph{Precision}
Escaping-edges precision replays the log on the model
\cite{MunozGama2010FreshLook}. For every prefix of a trace, it compares the
activities that the model allows next with the activities that the log shows
after the same prefix. An activity that the model allows but the log never shows
after that prefix is an \emph{escaping edge}. Precision is one minus the share of
escaping edges, weighted by how often each prefix occurs. The alignment-based
variant first aligns every trace with the model, so that non-fitting traces can
be included \cite{Adriansyah2012AlignPrecision,Adriansyah2015Precision}.

The measure can be read on two levels. On the level of transitions, the
automaton is built from fired transitions, silent ones included
\cite{Adriansyah2012AlignPrecision}. Two nets with the same language can then
receive different values \cite{Tax2018Imprecisions,Syring2019Propositions}. On
the level of activities, the model side after a prefix is the set of activities
that the language allows next, over every marking the prefix can reach.
Precision then depends only on the language and the log. This is what the
proposition BehPro+ asks of a quality measure: the representation of the model
must not matter \cite{vanderAalst2018Propositions}. The activity level is the
reference in this thesis, because the thesis compares nets with the same language
but different representations.

A tool has to know the state of the model after each prefix. The token-based
implementation keeps one marking per prefix. In a deterministic net without
silent transitions, this marking is unique, and the value equals the
activity-level value. With silent transitions, a prefix can reach several
markings. The tool then chooses one by a heuristic \cite{MunozGama2010FreshLook},
and the value can deviate from the activity-level value. For the same reason,
multi-perspective precision assumes that the executed silent transitions are
known, for example from alignments \cite{Mannhardt2016Precision}.
Section~\ref{sec:exp_discovery_eval} states how this is handled.

\paragraph{Generalisation}
Token-based generalisation counts how often each transition is used in the
replay. A transition that fires many times contributes little uncertainty; a
transition that fires rarely contributes much \cite{Buijs2014Quality}. The
measure looks at transitions, not at sequences. A model that allows many
unobserved sequences through frequently used transitions can still score
high.

\paragraph{Coverage}
\label{sec:bg_coverage}
Fitness gives partial credit. A trace with one deviation among six events
still reaches a trace fitness of about 0.8, and log fitness averages such
values over all traces. Coverage measures ask a stricter question about whole
cases: does the model accept the complete sequence, yes or no? This thesis uses
three of them.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Share of fitting cases.} The share of traces whose optimal
alignment has no deviation. It is the simplest fitness measure
\cite{Aalst2016} and counts every case, so frequent sequences weigh more.
\item \textbf{Share of accepted sequences.} The share of the distinct sequences
(variants) of the log that the model accepts, each counted once. This is recall
on the level of the language: the log is read as a set of sequences, not as a
collection of cases. Entropy-based recall extends the same idea to models with
infinitely many sequences \cite{Polyvyanyy2020Monotone}.
\item \textbf{Coverage per group.} The share of fitting cases within a group of
cases, for example the visits that use two RMG blocks. A dominant variant can
hide that a smaller but regular group is not accepted
\cite{RWNorouzifar2026Rules}.
\end{itemize}
On a hold-out log, a fourth view checks generalisation directly: how many of the
sequences that occur in the hold-out log but not in the training log does the
model accept?

When cases follow a long-tailed distribution, the measures can differ a lot. A
model that accepts only the few frequent sequences fits almost all cases but
accepts only a small share of the sequences. For a simulator the unweighted view
matters as well, because a sequence that the model does not accept can never be
generated.

High coverage is not the same as overfitting. A model \emph{overfits} if it
allows only the observed sequences and fails on new ones; it \emph{underfits} if
it allows much more than the log shows \cite{Aalst2016}. Precision measures the
second error. The first can only be seen on a hold-out log, which is why the
fourth view above matters. Noise filtering, as in the Inductive Miner with
filtering, removes rare behaviour because it treats it as noise
\cite{Leemans2014InductiveMiner}. Whether rare behaviour is noise or regular
behaviour cannot be decided from its frequency alone. It is a question of the
data quality and of the domain.

\paragraph{Implication for this thesis}
Fitness, precision and coverage compare the language of a model with the
observed traces. Coverage shows which observed behaviour a simulator built on the
model could never generate. None of the measures says how often a simulator will
generate each permitted sequence. A path that is permitted but never observed
may be rare in the language and still frequent in the simulation. This is why
the selected models are also simulated (E3).

\section{Business Process Simulation}
\label{sec:bps_models}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{hints}
\item One to one and a half pages. Concepts only; the measures and replications used are defined in Chapter~\ref{chp:experiments}.
\end{hints}

Business process simulation (BPS) runs an executable model of a process to
study its behaviour without disturbing the real system \cite{Aalst2015}. A
BPS model has five parts \cite{Aalst2015,PMRozinat2009}:
\begin{itemize}
\item an \emph{arrival process} that decides when new cases start;
\item a \emph{control-flow model} that decides which activities can follow,
together with \emph{routing} probabilities or rules at each choice;
\item \emph{resources} with their capacity and working calendars;
\item \emph{time models} for the execution and waiting times of activities;
\item \emph{case attributes} that describe each case.
\end{itemize}
Most BPS tools use discrete-event simulation: time jumps from one event to the
next. Because arrivals, routing and durations are drawn at random, one run is
one possible history. Several runs with different random seeds
(\emph{replications}) show how much the results vary \cite{Law2015Simulation}.
Simulation is mainly used for \emph{what-if analysis}: the same model is run
with and without a change, for example more resources or another allocation
rule, and the KPIs are compared \cite{Aalst2015}.

\paragraph{Related work: discovering simulation models}
Rozinat et al.\ showed early that all five parts can be estimated from an event
log \cite{PMRozinat2009}. Camargo et al.\ automate this and search for the
configuration whose generated log is most similar to the real log
\cite{Camargo2020,simod2024}. L\'opez-Pintado and Dumas show that modelling
resources individually, with their own calendars and performance, brings the
simulated cycle times closer to the real ones
\cite{BPSdifferentiatedResources2022,Prosimos2023}. Stochastic process models
add probabilities to the paths of a model \cite{stochastikPetriNets2013Solti,burke2020stochasticPD}.
Deep-learning generators can learn more complex dependencies, but they are hard
to inspect and to change for a scenario \cite{Camargo2021}.

\paragraph{Related work: data-aware simulation}
A classical BPS model uses the same routing probability for every case. A
\emph{data-aware} model lets routing, times or resource choice depend on data,
for example case attributes or the current workload. Mannhardt et al.\ define
stochastic Petri nets whose transition weights depend on data
\cite{mannhardt2023stochasticProcessesModelling}. De Leoni et al.\ add the
history of the case to this \emph{process state} and show that the best state
depends on the process \cite{Vinci2023Inf.Activ.Prob}. L\'opez-Pintado et al.\
also model how attributes change during a case \cite{lopez2024}. RIMS calls
predictive models at run time, so that they can use the current state of the
simulation \cite{runtimeIntegration2024}. ProSiT, introduced in
Section~\ref{sec:prosit_background}, applies data awareness to all parts of the
model.

\paragraph{Validation}
\label{sec:simulation_validation_background}
A simulation model is valid only for an intended use \cite{Sargent2013Validation}.
Sargent separates \emph{conceptual validity} (are the assumptions reasonable
for the purpose?), \emph{verification} (is the model implemented correctly?),
\emph{data validity} and \emph{operational validity} (is the output accurate
enough for the purpose?). For discovered BPS models, operational validity is
usually checked by comparing logs generated by the model with a real log that
was not used for discovery. Chapela-Campa et al.\ recommend separate measures
for the control flow, the timing and the congestion of the generated logs,
because one aggregate score hides which part of the model is wrong
\cite{ChapelaCampa2023Trust,Chapela2025SimQuality}. Özdemir et al.\ add two
cautions \cite{Oezdemir2025RethinkingBPS}: a later test log mixes the error of
the model with changes of the process between the periods, and distances based
on the Wasserstein distance can hide temporal patterns. Law recommends
\emph{trace-driven} comparisons, in which the model is fed with the observed
inputs of the comparison period, for example the observed arrivals, so that
model and system face the same demand \cite{Law2015Simulation}. A comparison with a
historical log shows that the model reproduces the current process. It does not
show that the model predicts the effect of a change correctly. Evidence for a
what-if use needs more: plausible mechanisms, a model that responds to load as
the real system does, and the judgement of people who know the process. Such
\emph{face validation} by domain experts is a recognised technique
\cite{Sargent2013Validation}. A review of 105 studies on simulation and process
mining found that 36 report no validation at all \cite{Sulis2026SimulationReview}.

\paragraph{Replications}
\label{sec:stochastic_replication_ci}
A model is run $N$ times with different seeds. For a measure with replication
values $x_1,\dots,x_N$, the mean $\bar{x}$ is reported with the interval
$\bar{x}\pm t_{0.975,N-1}\,s/\sqrt{N}$, where $s$ is the standard deviation
across replications \cite{Law2015Simulation}. This interval describes the
random variation of the simulator for one fitted model. It does not cover the
uncertainty of the fitted parameters or of the model structure.

\section{ProSiT}
\label{sec:prosit_background}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{hints}
\item Core section, three to four pages. Describes ProSiT 1.0.4 as implemented (\texttt{prosit-pm}); every statement must match the code. The values chosen for CTB are in Section~\ref{sec:exp_baseline}, the full trees in Appendix~\ref{app:prosit_inventory}.
\end{hints}

ProSiT (\textit{PROcess SImulation Tool}) discovers a data-aware simulation
model from a Petri net and an event log \cite{Vinci2026prosit,ProSiTRepository}.
Every part of the model is learned as a \emph{probabilistic decision tree}: a
decision tree whose leaves hold a probability or a distribution instead of a
single value. The model is called white-box, because every learned rule can be
read and changed. This section describes what ProSiT learns, how a tree is
built and used, how a simulated case runs, and where the framework stops.

\subsection{Inputs and Learned Components}
\label{sec:prosit_components}

ProSiT needs a sound workflow net and an event log with start and complete
timestamps, a resource per event and case attributes. It first aligns every
trace with the net. The alignment tells, for every event, which transition
fired, which transitions were enabled at that moment and since when. From this
replay, ProSiT builds one training table per component.
Table~\ref{tab:prosit_components} lists the components.

A few components need a short explanation.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Arrivals.} The gaps between consecutive case starts are
computed in open minutes of the arrival calendar. The calendar marks a weekday
and hour as open if enough arrivals fall into it. It decides only \emph{whether}
cases can arrive; the tree decides \emph{how often}.
\item \textbf{Routing.} For every transition, ProSiT collects all moments in
the replay at which it was enabled. The class is 1 if it fired and 0 if another
transition fired. The tree then predicts the probability that the transition
fires, given the case attributes and how often each activity has occurred so
far. These features describe the case, not the marking of the net. Silent
transitions get a routing tree like visible ones; when they fired is known only
from the alignment.
\item \textbf{Capacity.} By default every resource handles one activity at a
time. With \emph{multitasking} enabled, a resource whose activities overlap in
at least a given share of cases gets a capacity of the largest observed overlap
plus one.
\item \textbf{Waiting time.} The waiting time of an event is the time between
its start and the later of two points: the moment the transition was enabled,
and the moment the resource became free. Waiting caused by a busy resource is
therefore not part of the target. The simulator produces it through the
capacity check.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Probabilistic Decision Trees}
\label{sec:prosit_trees}

A decision tree splits the training data step by step on one feature at a
time, for example ``hour $\le$ 21''. Each split is chosen so that the groups
become as different as possible in the target (CART). The groups at the end
are the \emph{leaves}. In ProSiT, a routing leaf stores a probability. A time
leaf stores a distribution: ProSiT fits a fixed value, a normal, exponential,
lognormal, gamma and uniform distribution to the values in the leaf and keeps
the one closest to the data in Wasserstein distance. Values drawn from it are
limited to the smallest and largest observed value.

\paragraph{Model selection}
A tree is not always better than one distribution for all cases. ProSiT
therefore chooses by five-fold cross-validation. It tries every tree depth
from one to \texttt{max\_depth\_tree} and every minimum leaf size in
\texttt{min\_samples\_leaf\_cv}, and compares them with a single distribution
without splits. For routing, the score is the log loss of the predicted
probabilities. For time models, version 1.0.4 uses the continuous ranked
probability score (CRPS). For an observed value $y$ and a predicted
distribution $F$,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{CRPS}(F,y)=\mathbb{E}\,|X-y|-\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}\,|X-X'|,
\end{equation}
where $X$ and $X'$ are independent draws from $F$ \cite{Gneiting2007CRPS}. The
first term rewards distributions close to the observation, the second rewards
spread where the data are spread. A lower score is better. Version 1.0.3 used
the Wasserstein distance between training and test values inside each leaf.
That criterion measures only how stable a leaf is, so it almost always chose a
single distribution. This thesis uses version 1.0.4
(Section~\ref{sec:mc_hyperparameters}).

\paragraph{Example}
Figure~\ref{fig:bg_prosit_arrival_tree} shows the arrival tree that ProSiT
learned from the training data of this thesis. Read from the top: if the
previous truck arrived on a weekday between 5 and 21~h, the gap to the next
truck is drawn from a normal distribution with a mean of 0.65 minutes, about
90 trucks per hour. At night, the mean gap is about two minutes. On Saturday
after 10~h, it is 13 minutes. The tree has depth three, so it cannot separate

\subsection{Execution of a Simulated Case}
\label{sec:prosit_execution}

The simulator processes all cases in one loop, always continuing with the case
whose next step is enabled earliest. A case runs as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Arrival.} The start time is the start of the previous case plus a
gap drawn from the arrival tree, counted in open minutes of the arrival
calendar.
\item \textbf{Attributes.} The case receives its attributes, in empirical mode
as one complete tuple of a randomly drawn training case.
\item \textbf{Enabled transitions.} The token sits in the source place. A
transition is enabled if all its input places hold a token. Its enabling time
is the latest time stamp of these tokens.
\item \textbf{Routing.} Each enabled transition gets the probability from its
routing tree, given the case attributes and the activities so far. The
simulator draws one transition in proportion to these weights. Silent
transitions are drawn in the same way and take no time.
\item \textbf{Resource.} For a visible transition, the candidate resources are
those that executed this activity in the log. Of these, the ones with free
capacity at the enabling time are kept, and one is drawn from the resource
trees. If none is free, the resource that becomes free first is taken and the
start moves to that moment.
\item \textbf{Times.} A waiting time is drawn from the tree of the resource
(zero for the first activity of a case). The start is the enabling time plus
the waiting time; the complete time is the start plus an execution time drawn
from the tree of the activity. Both are counted in open minutes of the
resource calendar.
\item \textbf{Token update.} The tokens move to the output places and carry the
complete time. Steps 3 to 7 repeat until the final marking is reached.
\end{enumerate}
The generated log contains, for each event, the case, activity, resource,
enabling, start and complete time and the case attributes. The firing sequence
of each case is stored as well.

\subsection{Evidence and Limitations}
\label{sec:prosit_limits}

The ProSiT paper evaluates the framework on five case studies with a temporal
80/20 split and five simulated logs per model. ProSiT reaches an accuracy
comparable to a black-box baseline while its rules stay readable
\cite{Vinci2026prosit}. The authors also stress a boundary: the trees capture
associations in the training data, not causes. A readable rule shows which
association the simulator uses. Whether it holds after an intervention needs
other evidence.

For this thesis, seven properties of the implementation matter.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{No hard rules.} A transition can fire whenever its input places
hold tokens and its weight is above zero. ProSiT has no guards. A rule that
the net does not encode, such as ``at least one stop'' or ``as many stops as
planned'', is followed only as often as the routing trees happen to respect it.
\item \textbf{No look-ahead.} Each routing draw is local. A sequence of likely
steps can lead to a visit that never occurs in the data.
\item \textbf{Parallel branches run in parallel.} A silent parallel split
enables several stops at the same time, and the simulator can start them at
the same time.
\item \textbf{Capacity at enabling time.} The capacity of a resource is checked
when the transition is enabled, not over the interval in which the activity
actually runs.
\item \textbf{Load enters through few features.} Workload features act on the
resource choice and the waiting time only. Execution times do not react to the
load of the terminal.
\item \textbf{The queue feature counts the activity itself.} In training, the
queue length of a resource counts the activities that are enabled and not yet
started. An activity that waited is therefore counted in the queue of the
resource that performed it, but not in the queues of the other candidates. In
the simulation the activity is not yet in any queue when the resource is
chosen.
\item \textbf{Waiting is measured against one server.} The waiting time is the
time from the moment the resource was last completely free, that is, from the
latest end of all its activities in progress at the enabling time. For a
resource that serves several cases side by side, a lane may already be free much
earlier.
\end{enumerate}
The first three properties make the control-flow model more important for a
simulator than for a descriptive model; E2 addresses them
(Section~\ref{sec:exp_rules}). Properties four to seven concern the physical
constraints of the blocks and are examined in E4
(Section~\ref{sec:exp_mechanisms}).

Three gaps follow. First, discovered models are usually selected by fitness
and precision. These measures do not show what a simulator does with behaviour
that the model permits but the log never shows, and physical rules are not part
of the discovered model. Second, data-aware simulation discovery has been
evaluated mainly on administrative and healthcare processes. Its behaviour in a
physical logistics system with shared capacity and congestion is largely
untested. Third, validation of discovered simulation models focuses on
similarity to a historical log. Whether the model responds to load like the
real system, which is needed before a what-if analysis, is rarely tested, and
the judgement of domain experts is rarely documented.

This thesis addresses these gaps in a case study of truck operations at HHLA
Container Terminal Burchardkai. It does not propose a new discovery algorithm or
a new simulator. It compares discovery algorithms for their use in simulation
(E1), restricts the selected models with physical rules (E2), measures how the
control-flow model changes the simulated visits (E3), audits the simulation
mechanisms and their response to load (E4), and evaluates one operational
policy only if the model passes these checks (E5).
