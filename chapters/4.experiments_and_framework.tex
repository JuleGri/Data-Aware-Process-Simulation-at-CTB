\section{Goals of the Experiments}
\label{sec:experiment_goals}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

The experiments assess whether a data-aware process simulation model can be
constructed from historical CTB truck-operation data and used as a credible
representation of the observed process. The aim is not to optimise the
terminal or to show that a more complex model is automatically better. The
aim is to test what the discovered model reproduces, where the discovery
workflow requires domain intervention, and what can be learned from its
responses to controlled operational changes.

The first goal is to construct an executable simulation model from the event
log while preserving the physical sequence of a truck visit. The expected
workflow starts with automatic process discovery. Its output is checked not
only through process-mining quality measures but also against the case
contract of one Gate In, sequential yard handling, and one final Gate Out. If
the discovered structure violates this contract, the experiment must make the
required correction explicit instead of treating a formally valid model as an
operationally valid simulation.

The second goal is to measure how well the resulting simulation reproduces an
unseen period of terminal operation. This includes the activity sequence,
service times, truck turnaround times, and the arrival process. Three ProSiT
configurations are compared to test whether additional routing and workload
context improves held-out fidelity. This is tested directly. More context is
treated as a modelling choice, not as an expected improvement.

The third goal is to examine the model as a white-box tool for operational
what-if analysis. The selected model is exposed to a resource-pool change at
T22 and to a higher truck-arrival rate. These interventions test whether the
specified input changes reach the simulation and whether the resulting KPI
changes are interpretable within the model boundaries. They do not establish
that the same effects would occur in the real terminal.

Across these goals, robustness is assessed through a temporal hold-out,
repeated simulation runs, model-quality diagnostics, and sensitivity checks.
Together, these experiments provide the evidence required to answer the
research questions introduced in Chapter~\ref{chp:introduction}.

\subsection{Layered Validity Audit}
\label{sec:layered_validity_audit}

The experimental design treats simulation validity as a sequence of distinct
claims. First, the event semantics must preserve the recorded order and case
boundaries. Second, the process model must replay held-out behaviour without
violating the sequential truck contract. Third, the stochastic model must
reproduce held-out routing, timing, arrivals, and case outcomes. Fourth, the
transfer from the discovery period to the later period must be separated from
calibration error. Fifth, resource and location labels must be checked against
their physical meaning. Only after these layers does scenario response become
evidence relevant to counterfactual decision support. This ordering follows
the multi-perspective principle that apparently strong aggregate fit can hide
perspective-specific errors \cite{Chapela2025SimQuality,Sargent2013Validation}.

The layers are complementary rather than alternative metrics. A failure at a
later layer does not invalidate every earlier result; it limits the conclusion
that can be drawn. For example, a net may be structurally sound and replay the
hold-out while its simulator still omits physical transition time. Likewise, a
parameter can be changed reproducibly without the model containing the
mechanism through which the real terminal would react. The results chapter
therefore reports both the successful layer and the first unsupported stronger
claim instead of assigning one global validity score.

\section{Solution Architecture}
\label{sec:solution_architecture}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

The solution architecture is organised as a sequence of persistent processing
blocks. Each block produces an artefact that is used by the next block. This
makes the split, model discovery, simulation, and evaluation reproducible and
prevents information from the test period from entering model discovery.
Figure~\ref{fig:research_design} gives an overview of the complete pipeline.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth,trim=0 0 0 105,clip]{figures/schemas/experimental_pipeline_bw.png}
\caption{Solution architecture of the experimental pipeline.}
\label{fig:research_design}
\end{figure}

The input is the simulation-ready event log from
Chapter~\ref{chp:case_study_and_data}. It is divided chronologically into a
training partition and an unseen test partition. Only the training data are
used to discover the control flow and the ProSiT simulation parameters. The
training log is loaded in its explicit XES event order and an Inductive-Miner
model is discovered. A hard structural check rejects any model containing a
parallel operator because terminal experts confirmed that the activities of
one truck must be sequential. The
accepted Petri net becomes the common control-flow skeleton for all simulation
configurations.

ProSiT then discovers arrival, timing, routing, and resource parameters from
the training partition. The three configurations differ in the amount of
context made available to their decision models. Each discovered parameter
bundle is stored separately and simulated over the same time window and case
volume as the test partition. Repeated runs with different seeds produce the
simulation logs used for the fidelity comparison and confidence intervals.

The evaluation block compares the simulated logs with the held-out event log
from several perspectives. It checks control-flow coverage, timing
distributions, point statistics, arrival behaviour, and resource assumptions.
After this historical evaluation, one transparent parameter bundle is used for
the two what-if experiments. The scenario runner changes only the defined
input parameters and compares each intervention with a matched-seed baseline.
The transition-duration sensitivity study and the documented ProSiT
workarounds provide additional diagnostic evidence around the main pipeline.

\section{Held-Out Train/Test Split}
\label{sec:train_test_split}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\paragraph{Rationale}

A held-out validation set is needed to answer RQ1--RQ3. In simulation
discovery it is not sufficient to report the fit of parameters on the
data used to discover them: a well-fit ProSiT bundle can memorise the
training distribution while over-fitting the operational dynamics. The
ProSiT reference implementation \cite{Vinci2026prosit} describes an
in-memory split in its \textit{Advanced Usage} example, but does not
persist the split, does not commit to a case-level boundary and does
not report validation metrics. This thesis uses a persistent,
case-level, temporally ordered split that any downstream script can
consume unchanged.

\paragraph{Split Definition}
\label{sec:split_definition}

Let $\mathcal{L}=(c_1,c_2,\dots,c_N)$ be the set of $N=89{,}460$ cases
in the final event log. For each case $c_i$ let $\tau(c_i)$ denote its
earliest event timestamp:
\begin{equation}
    \tau(c_i)=\min\bigl\{
      \text{start}(e),\text{time}(e)
      \mid e\in c_i \bigr\}.
\end{equation}
Sort the cases by $\tau$ and a deterministic case-identifier. The first 80\,\% form the training set and the remaining
20\,\% form the test set. The cutoff $\tau^{\star}$ is recorded as the earliest arrival in the test set.

On the CTB event log the cutoff is
$\tau^{\star}=\text{2026-04-20 18:17:00}$. The split produces 71,568
training cases (220,048 events) and 17,892 testing cases (55,360
events); no events are dropped. The split is realised by
\texttt{validation/01\_train\_test\_split.py} and is documented in
\texttt{validation/results/split\_manifest.json}, which records
the seed, the cutoff and the per-activity coverage in both partitions.
The manifest is loaded by every downstream simulation script so that
$n_{\text{traces}}$ and $t_{\text{start}}$ match the test window
exactly.

\subsection{Temporal-Transfer Diagnostic}
\label{sec:temporal_transfer_method}

A temporal hold-out combines two effects: simulation mismatch and real change
between the discovery and evaluation periods. Recent evaluation work notes
that treating every discrepancy from a later log as model error can therefore
confound model quality with process drift \cite{Ozdemir2025UtilityBPS}. This
thesis adds an observed train--test audit before interpreting the simulation
distances. It compares case turnaround, per-activity service distributions,
activity and target-area composition, and the available demand and utilisation
attributes between the two real periods. Wasserstein distance describes the
distributional shift; a Welch interval is used for the mean-turnaround change,
and a case-bootstrap interval for the P90 change.

The audit is descriptive and does not refit the model. For means, the final
hold-out deficit can be decomposed exactly as
\begin{equation}
\bar{T}_{\mathrm{test}}-\bar{T}_{\mathrm{sim}}
=
(\bar{T}_{\mathrm{train}}-\bar{T}_{\mathrm{sim}})
+(\bar{T}_{\mathrm{test}}-\bar{T}_{\mathrm{train}}).
\label{eq:mean_transfer_decomposition}
\end{equation}
The first term is the discrepancy relative to the calibration period and the
second is observed temporal transfer. This identity is not applied to
Wasserstein distances, which are not additively decomposable. The implementation
is \texttt{validation/14\_temporal\_transfer\_audit.py}.

\section{Process Discovery}
\label{sec:process_discovery_methodology}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Discovery Input and Event-Order Semantics}

In a data-aware process-simulation workflow, control flow is discovered before
the timing, routing, arrival, and resource parameters are fitted. For ProSiT,
the resulting Petri net is not merely a diagram: it is the executable backbone
that determines which transition can fire next. The selected model therefore
has to balance fitness, precision, generalisation, and simplicity while also
respecting the physical meaning of a truck visit
\cite{Leemans2014InductiveMiner,PMvanDerAalst2016}.

The CTB domain restriction is deliberately narrow. Within one case,
\texttt{Gate In} must precede the yard work and \texttt{Gate Out} must follow
it; two activities of the same truck may not execute in parallel. Different
trucks may still be processed concurrently through the simulator's resource
capacities. The order among yard activities is not constrained because it
depends on the handling requirements of the visit and repetitions are
operationally possible. The no-parallelism constraint was confirmed by
operational experts from the terminal and fixed before inspecting the
discovered model.

An initial diagnostic conversion through a PM4Py DataFrame formatter sorted
events by timestamps. Because CTB start and completion timestamps can overlap
at minute resolution, this conversion did not necessarily preserve the
explicit event order stored in the XES traces and produced a model containing
parallel cuts. That model was an artefact of the import path, not the final
discovery result. The authoritative pipeline now reads the XES file directly
as a PM4Py event log, preserving the recorded within-trace order. The original
timestamps remain unchanged for all time and resource models. This distinction
is methodologically important: activity order is part of the event-log
semantics and must not be reconstructed implicitly from coarse or overlapping
timestamps.

\subsection{Discovered Process Flow}

Figure~\ref{fig:ctb_dfg_frequency} shows a Disco-style dominant-frequency view
of the order-preserving training log. It is a directly-follows graph generated
from the PM4Py result rather than a literal export from Fluxicon Disco. Each
node reports an activity's occurrence count and each arc reports how often its
target directly followed its source. To keep the process readable on the page,
the figure retains transitions observed in at least 1\,\% of training cases
($n\geq716$). The unfiltered 59-arc graph is retained in the project artifacts
for audit but is too dense to support visual interpretation.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth,height=0.66\textheight,keepaspectratio]{figures/discovery/dfg_frequency_dominant_training.png}
\caption{Dominant-frequency directly-follows graph of the order-preserving CTB
training log (71,568 cases). Node labels show activity occurrences; arc labels
show directly-follows counts and line weight indicates relative frequency.
Arcs observed in fewer than 1\,\% of cases are omitted for legibility.}
\label{fig:ctb_dfg_frequency}
\end{figure}

The frequency view confirms a stable outer process boundary: every training
case enters through \texttt{Gate In} and ends at \texttt{Gate Out}. Most visits
contain one yard activity; the prominent connection from
\texttt{LL\_delivery} to \texttt{RMG\_receive} shows the principal
multi-operation sequence.
\texttt{RMG\_receive}, \texttt{RMG\_delivery}, and \texttt{LL\_delivery}
dominate the handling volume. These directed connections describe succession,
not simultaneous execution: terminal experts confirmed that one truck does
not perform two activities in parallel. The DFG is therefore useful
descriptive evidence, but it is not used directly for simulation because it
has no marking or firing semantics and cannot itself distinguish choice from
concurrency.

The Inductive Miner expresses the same process as a sequential tree. Every case
starts with \texttt{Gate In}. It may then execute \texttt{LL\_mixed}, after
which it either enters the yard loop or follows a silent bypass. Each pass
through the loop selects one visible yard activity, and the loop may repeat to
represent visits with several handling operations. Once the loop is left, the
case ends with \texttt{Gate Out}. The tree therefore allows different yard
orders and repeated handling without allowing two activities of the same truck
to execute concurrently.

The converted Petri net in Figure~\ref{fig:ctb_inductive_petri} contains 7 places,
16 transitions, and 32 arcs. Black boxes are silent routing transitions; white
boxes are observed activities. Most importantly, the discovered process tree
contains zero parallel operators. A single case token can therefore enable
only a sequential yard path before reaching \texttt{Gate Out}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth,height=0.48\textheight,keepaspectratio]{figures/discovery/petri_net_inductive.png}
\caption{Inductive-Miner Petri net discovered from the order-preserving CTB
training log. White transitions represent observed activities, black
transitions are silent routing steps, circles are places, and the double circle
marks completion. The net contains no parallel split.}
\label{fig:ctb_inductive_petri}
\end{figure}

The model abstracts from the 70 exact training variants by permitting unseen
sequential combinations and repetitions of yard activities. This is intended
generalisation, not a defect: the domain contract constrains concurrency and
the entrance/exit boundary, not the internal order of yard services. The net
also contains a silent structural bypass of the yard loop. This makes its
formal language slightly broader than the observed log, in which every visit
has yard work. Native ProSiT transition-weight discovery nevertheless learns
routing from the training data; the potential bypass is therefore assessed by
an explicit output diagnostic rather than silently removed. Its frequency is
reported for each retained configuration as evidence of the model's
overgeneralisation.

This decision is made against an explicit model-repair alternative. De Leoni
et al. \cite{Vinci2023Inf.Activ.Prob} manually changed a source Petri net before
their simulation experiments when it allowed an unobserved one-activity trace.
Vinci and de Leoni \cite{VinciDeLeoni2025Repair} subsequently formalised an
iterative workflow in which real and simulated traces are discriminated,
explanatory directly-follows features propose repairs, and the original and
repaired nets are compared on held-out fitness, precision and simulation-log
distance. Their implemented repair operators add missing behaviour; the paper
explicitly leaves precision-oriented restriction of excessive behaviour as an
extension. The CTB defect is of the latter kind: the gate-only trace is already
permitted and would have to be removed, not added. Applying the published
repair operators unchanged would therefore not solve it. A manually restricted
net would be a legitimate secondary robustness model, but it would no longer be
the unchanged discovery result and would require the complete conformance,
simulation and scenario evaluation to be repeated.

To quantify this alternative without replacing the primary model, a separate
precision-oriented robustness repair splits the state after \texttt{Gate In}.
If \texttt{LL\_mixed} has not occurred, the silent branch is forced into the
yard loop; if it has occurred, the observed \texttt{LL\_mixed}-only visit
remains possible. The modification therefore removes only the gate-only path
identified in the structural audit. Original and repaired nets are compared on
the same held-out fitness, precision, generalisation, and simplicity measures.
The repaired PNML and executable parameter bundle are exported separately by
\texttt{validation/16\_gate\_only\_structural\_repair.py}. This is an ablation
of one structural assumption, not a claim that the published repair operators
of Vinci and de Leoni already implement precision-side pruning.

The corrected Inductive-Miner Petri net is consequently accepted as the
authoritative control-flow model and passed unchanged to ProSiT. The earlier
prefix-trie fallback is not part of the final methodology. This choice retains
automatic discovery, supports unseen but sequential variants, and produces a
substantially smaller model than memorising every observed path.

\subsection{Held-Out Conformance and Case-Contract Validation}

The discovered net is evaluated on the training and test partitions through
token-based replay fitness and precision, together with PM4Py's
\texttt{generalization\_tbr} and \texttt{simplicity\_petri\_net} measures.
Fitness tests whether observed traces can be replayed; precision penalises
behaviour allowed by the model but not supported by the log; generalisation
rewards an abstraction that is not tied to individual observations; and
simplicity describes structural economy. No single metric establishes model
suitability. Their joint interpretation is combined with the explicit
structural check that counts parallel operators and with simulation-output
contracts.

The generated logs are also checked against the sequential case contract. Each
case must contain one initial \texttt{Gate In} and one final \texttt{Gate Out};
activities inside one case may not overlap, \texttt{Gate Out} may not precede
yard work, and completion timestamps may not decrease. Gate-only paths remain
a reported overgeneralisation diagnostic because they are explicitly permitted
by the unchanged discovered net; they are not conflated with parallel
execution. This separates replay coverage from physical sequence validity. The
protocol is implemented in
\texttt{baseline/07\_run\_prosit\_discovery.py} and
\texttt{\_eventlog\_contract.py}. Its results are reported in
Section~\ref{sec:results_process_discovery}.

\section{Simulation Parameter Discovery Configurations}
\label{sec:simulation_discovery_methodology}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

ProSiT is used unchanged as the discovery and simulation engine
\cite{Vinci2026prosit}. All simulation parameters are learned by
\texttt{SimulatorParameters.discover\_from\_eventlog} from the training
partition only. Three configurations define an increasing contextual-
expressiveness ladder. The retained screening compares all three levels:
\textit{no-rules}, strict workload-blind \textit{rules}, and
\textit{rules$+$workload}. The middle level distinguishes the effect of
process-state rules from the additional effect of workload information.

\subsection{Compared Configurations}
\paragraph{Configuration \textit{no-rules}}
\label{sec:cfg_no_rules}

The \textit{no-rules} configuration uses
\texttt{max\_depth\_tree}$=0$, which disables the ProSiT decision-tree
mechanism entirely. Every parameter reduces to a single unconditional
distribution or a marginal frequency: one arrival-rate distribution,
one execution-time distribution per activity, one waiting-time
distribution per resource, one categorical distribution over routing
choices at each Petri-net decision point. This configuration is the
distribution-based reference point for RQ3.

\paragraph{Configuration \textit{rules}}
\label{sec:cfg_rules}

The \textit{rules} configuration uses the ProSiT default
(\texttt{max\_depth\_tree}$=3$,
\texttt{min\_samples\_leaf\_cv}$=[50,100,200]$,
\texttt{random\_state}$=42$). Every timing, routing and
resource-selection model is fitted as a decision tree whose leaves
carry the local distributions. Cross-validation over the leaf-size grid
selects the actual leaf size per model, and models that fail to beat
the marginal distribution on held-out CRPS collapse to the pooled
baseline (this is a ProSiT internal safeguard against overfitting to
noise \cite{Vinci2026prosit}). Case-level attributes made available to the
trees are restricted to process and case state:
\texttt{process\_flow\_type}, container and stop counts, delivery and receive
counts, hazardous and reefer indicators, full ratio, visit complexity,
\texttt{primary\_target\_area}, and \texttt{target\_area}. Demand,
utilisation, workload ranks and bins, current workload, and queue length are
removed before fitting. The configuration is workload-blind by design rather
than because a fitted tree happened not to select an available workload proxy.

\paragraph{Configuration \textit{rules$+$workload}}
\label{sec:cfg_workload}

The \textit{rules$+$workload} configuration uses the same tree
hyperparameters but restores the demand, utilisation, and derived target-load
attributes and sets
\texttt{use\_workload\_features}$=$\texttt{True}. As documented in the
ProSiT README this enables two extra features on the waiting-time and
resource-selection models: current workload (number of concurrent
tasks) and queue length (number of tasks scheduled but not yet started)
at the time each event is enabled in the process model. The intention behind including this
configuration is to let ProSiT see the queueing state that its
distribution family alone would otherwise ignore. It forms the more
expressive endpoint of the retained RQ3 comparison. The contrast from
\textit{rules} to \textit{rules$+$workload} therefore measures the combined
increment from a workload-blind state abstraction to all available workload
information; it does not identify a separate coefficient for each workload
feature family.

All three configurations receive the same training-only CTB calibration. It
preserves the empirical zero-inflated working-minute inter-arrival sample,
allows already admitted trucks to finish across the weekly gate closure, and
quantises simulated timestamps to the source log's one-minute resolution.
Routing remains the native ProSiT discovery result for the Inductive-Miner net.
Applying these common observation and calendar settings to all configurations keeps
the RQ3 comparison focused on contextual model expressiveness.

With \texttt{enable\_multitasking=True}, ProSiT can classify a resource as
multitasking when at least 5\,\% of its observed events show concurrent work.
It can then learn resource capacity from overlaps between different truck
cases. This is separate from the control-flow structure above. The discovered
Petri net keeps the activities of one truck sequential. Multitasking allows a
resource
to serve several trucks at the same time. It is also different from
\texttt{use\_workload\_features}, which only adds workload and queue state as
inputs to the decision trees.

The full pipeline is executed by
\texttt{baseline/07\_run\_prosit\_discovery.py}. The pickled
\texttt{SimulatorParameters} objects
(\texttt{prosit\_params.pkl}) and their JSON representations are stored
in the paramset folder. The three compared bundles are stored as
\path{prosit_discovery_no_rules_inductive_calibrated/},
\path{prosit_discovery_rules_only_workload_blind_inductive_calibrated/}, and
\path{prosit_discovery_workload_inductive_calibrated/}.

\subsection{Authoritative Parameter Bundle and Serialization Contract}
\label{sec:authoritative_parameter_bundle}

In this thesis, \emph{authoritative parameter bundle} is an operational
reproducibility term: it denotes the exact executable
\texttt{SimulatorParameters} state loaded by the validation and scenario
scripts. If a derived table, JSON summary and executable state disagree,
the serialized object that generated the reported simulations is
the source of truth. Two provenance layers are distinguished. The frozen,
training-only calibrated \textit{no-rules}, \textit{rules}, and
\textit{rules$+$workload} \texttt{prosit\_params.pkl} files are authoritative
for the RQ3 ablation. The latter is also the freely discovered source for the
data-aware scenario reference model, which is a deep-copied effective
bundle in which the RMG concurrency values are capped at the domain maximum
of three; it is stored as
\texttt{params\_baseline\_rmg\_max\_concurrency\_3.pkl} in the final
scenario result directory and is authoritative for its reported validation
and scenario results. The discovered overlap capacities remain
auditable without being mistaken for physically feasible capacities. All
executable bundles contain the Petri net and markings, routing and
resource-selection rules, resource mappings and capacities, calendars,
distribution objects, calibration metadata and the empirical sample arrays
used for stochastic draws.

The ProSiT JSON export is retained as a portable, human-inspectable
companion, but it is not an execution-equivalent serialization of the
calibrated object. The JSON rule nodes preserve fitted
distribution names, parameters and representative values but do not
serialize all empirical \texttt{sampled} arrays held in the Python
object. This matters because the CTB calibration modifies those arrays
and ProSiT samples from them at runtime. Reconstructing the
final simulator from JSON alone can change its stochastic behaviour. The
pickle is authoritative because the JSON does not fully
represent all empirical sample arrays; this statement concerns the actual
ProSiT serialization schema used here, not an intrinsic superiority of
the pickle format.

The companion files \texttt{prosit\_run\_summary.json},
\texttt{control\_flow\_contract.json} and
\texttt{ctb\_calibration.json} document provenance and intended changes.
Validation and scenario scripts record SHA-256 hashes of their source
pickles, and scenario bundles are produced by deep copy rather than by
mutating the frozen baseline. Pickle is Python- and class-version-specific
and must never be loaded from an untrusted source. Reproducibility
requires the recorded software environment. These limitations are the
reason for retaining the JSON companions even though they are not the
authoritative runtime state.

\section{Monte-Carlo Replication Protocol}
\label{sec:mc_protocol}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Every ProSiT simulation includes stochastic draws from the fitted
distributions. One Monte-Carlo draw is not enough to conclude that one
configuration is more faithful than
another: the observed difference may be within the sampling variance
of the simulator itself. This is the same many-possible-realisations
problem addressed by the multi-log validation design of
Chapela-Campa et al. and by classical independent-replication output
analysis \cite{ChapelaCampa2023Trust,Law2015Simulation}. This thesis
reports every fidelity metric with a Monte-Carlo confidence
interval.

\subsection{Replication Design}

The same replication design is used for the three ProSiT configurations
$c\in\mathcal{C}=\{\textit{no-rules},\textit{rules},
\textit{rules$+$workload}\}$. For each configuration, the stored parameter
bundle is loaded and a fresh simulation engine is created. Ten runs are then
generated with the seeds $s_i=42+i$ for $i=0,\ldots,9$. Each run starts at the test cutoff
$t_{\mathrm{start}}=\text{2026-04-20 18:17:00}$ and generates
$n_{\mathrm{traces}}=17{,}892$ cases. The start time and case count are read
from the split manifest so that every simulated log represents the same window
as the held-out data.

Let $m_{c,i}$ be one fidelity metric for configuration $c$ and seed $s_i$.
The reported value is the mean across the $N=10$ runs. Its 95\,\% Student-$t$
confidence interval is calculated as
\begin{equation}
    \bar{m}_c=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}m_{c,i},
    \qquad
    \mathrm{CI}_{95\%}=\bar{m}_c \;\pm\;
    t_{0.975,N-1}\frac{s_{m,c}}{\sqrt{N}},
\end{equation}
where $s_{m,c}$ is the sample standard deviation across the runs. This keeps
the comparison identical across configurations and separates the reported
mean from the simulator's run-to-run variation.

Because every configuration uses the same ten seeds, incremental effects are
also estimated as paired contrasts. For metric $m$, adjacent configurations
$c_1$ and $c_0$, and seed $s_i$, let
\begin{equation}
d_i=m_{c_1,i}-m_{c_0,i}.
\end{equation}
The mean and Student-$t$ interval are calculated over the ten $d_i$ values.
Pairing removes seed-to-seed variation shared by both configurations and
directly tests the increment from \textit{no-rules} to workload-blind
\textit{rules}, followed by the increment from \textit{rules} to
\textit{rules$+$workload}. The analysis is implemented in
\texttt{validation/15\_three\_configuration\_ablation.py}.

\paragraph{Implementation}

The protocol is implemented in
\texttt{validation/06\_multi\_seed\_ci.py}. \(N_{\text{seeds}}=10\) is
used for every configuration. This mirrors the $K=10$ design used by
Chapela-Campa et al. while remaining computationally feasible for the
17,892-case CTB test window \cite{ChapelaCampa2023Trust}. The resulting
Student-$t$ intervals quantify simulation sampling uncertainty
conditional on one frozen model and one fixed test log; with only ten
replications, they are not estimates of discovery or input-model
uncertainty. The base seed is 42.

The choice to seed Python's \texttt{random} and \texttt{numpy.random}
before each \texttt{engine.apply()} rather than the ProSiT engine
itself is intentional: ProSiT 1.0.3 does not expose a per-call seed on
its simulation engine. Seeding both random-number generators before the
call yields reproducible replications and captures the same stochastic
variability that a user would observe in production.

\section{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:evaluation_metrics}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

The comparison of a simulated event log against the real held-out log uses complementary distributional and point-statistic measures.
Each metric is computed once per Monte-Carlo replication so that a confidence interval can be attached to the point estimate.

\paragraph{Wasserstein Distance (EMD)}

For each yard activity $a$ the service-time EMD is
\begin{equation}
    \text{EMD}_a^{\text{svc}} = W_1
    \bigl(F_{a}^{\text{sim,svc}}, F_{a}^{\text{real,svc}}\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $F_{a}^{\text{sim,svc}}$ and $F_{a}^{\text{real,svc}}$ are the
empirical CDFs of $\text{time}-\text{start}$ for activity $a$ in the
simulated and real log, respectively. Gate In and Gate Out are excluded
from the service-fidelity aggregation because both have zero-duration
timestamp constructions and would mechanically improve the score without
representing terminal handling. Let $\mathcal A_Y$ be the set of yard
activities and $n_a^{\mathrm{real}}$ the number of valid held-out
observations. This thesis reports all per-activity values and two yard-level
aggregates:
\begin{align}
 \overline{\mathrm{EMD}}_{Y}
   &= \frac{1}{|\mathcal A_Y|}\sum_{a\in\mathcal A_Y}
      \mathrm{EMD}^{\mathrm{svc}}_a,\\
 \overline{\mathrm{EMD}}_{Y,w}
   &= \frac{\sum_{a\in\mathcal A_Y} n_a^{\mathrm{real}}
      \mathrm{EMD}^{\mathrm{svc}}_a}
      {\sum_{a\in\mathcal A_Y} n_a^{\mathrm{real}}}.
\end{align}
The first gives every yard-activity class equal influence; the second
describes the error experienced by the empirical mix of yard events. The
case-level turnaround EMD
is $W_1(F^{\text{sim,ttot}}, F^{\text{real,ttot}})$ where
$\text{ttot}$ is the total elapsed time per case (first start to last
complete). The inter-arrival EMD is
$W_1(F^{\text{sim,iat}}, F^{\text{real,iat}})$ where
$\text{iat}$ is the time between consecutive case-arrival timestamps.
All EMDs are reported in minutes. The main view retains durations up to
24 hours and is accompanied by an uncapped raw view so that an artificial
simulation tail cannot be hidden.

\paragraph{Kolmogorov--Smirnov Statistic}

For each distribution pair the two-sample KS statistic is computed by
\texttt{scipy.stats.ks\_2samp}. Because the CTB test log contains 55,360
events, sub-sampling is applied so that each side contributes at most
$10{,}000$ observations to the test. This limits computational cost and
the extreme sample-size sensitivity of the associated significance test;
it does not turn the $p$-value into a fidelity effect size. The KS
statistic (magnitude of the maximum vertical CDF gap) is reported
alongside the EMD as the main measure; the $p$-value is retained
only as a secondary diagnostic.

\paragraph{Point-Statistic Errors}

For each activity and each case-level KPI the mean, median and 90th
percentile of the simulated and real distributions are compared as
absolute and relative errors. This complements the distributional
metrics with operationally interpretable point statistics
(e.g.\ ``the simulator under-predicts mean turnaround by $X$ minutes'').

\paragraph{Frequency-Weighted Bottleneck Decomposition}

The held-out log is additionally decomposed to locate where elapsed truck time
accumulates. For each event, recorded service is completion minus start. The
non-negative gap between the previous event's completion and the current
event's start is defined as composite pre-service delay. Under the sequential
case contract, the sums of service and composite pre-service delay reconstruct
case turnaround exactly. Activities are ranked by accumulated minutes, equal
to event frequency multiplied by their mean contribution, and are accompanied
by median and p90 values. This prevents a rare but long event from being called
the dominant bottleneck solely because of its mean.

The gap is not interpreted as pure queueing. It may combine movement,
traffic or transfer, dispatching, and resource waiting. Resource-level overlap
at event start is reported as a separate diagnostic against the cap of three,
but overlap in a pooled block label is not treated as a physical crane count.
The procedure is implemented in
\texttt{validation/13\_bottleneck\_analysis.py}; its causal scope is limited to
identifying accumulation points that require further physical modelling.

\paragraph{Implementation}

The metric definitions are implemented in
\texttt{validation/02\_validate\_simulation.py} and reused verbatim by
\texttt{validation/06\_multi\_seed\_ci.py} to guarantee that
single-shot and multi-seed numbers are directly comparable.

\section{What-If Scenario Design}
\label{sec:what_if_design}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

The operational value of a logistics simulation lies in comparing future
decisions under conditions that cannot safely be tested in the live terminal.
The intended workflow begins with a forecast operating condition, compares
candidate policies through simulated process executions, and evaluates their
activity- and system-level consequences. A forecast of high yard utilisation could, for
example, motivate a reduction in truck slots released per hour or a temporary
policy that favours retrieval-related handling. A useful model must then show
not only total turnaround but also whether receive and delivery service or
pre-service delay change differently. These disaggregated effects are essential:
an acceptable terminal-wide average can conceal a harmful shift for one
handling pattern.

Two independent interventions are evaluated to address RQ4. Both are
derived by deep copy from the frozen, training-only calibrated
\textit{rules$+$workload} baseline. No held-out observation is used to
select or fit a scenario parameter. The experiments are deliberately simple
tests of this decision-support chain, rather than proposed operating policies.

\subsection{Scenario A: T22 Resource-Pool Reallocation}

Scenario A removes T22 from the simulated RMG resource pool. The scenario is
constructed by (i) removing T22 from every
\texttt{params.act\_to\_resources} entry that references it, after
verifying that each affected activity retains at least one eligible
resource; and (ii) setting all 168 weekly calendar slots of T22 to
unavailable. All other parameters are unchanged.

This is a resource-pool reallocation experiment, not a complete
spatial yard-closure model.
The discovered selector may use target-area attributes as statistical
predictors, but ProSiT does not impose a hard constraint that binds a
container to its historical storage block. A truck whose
historical target was T22 can be assigned to another eligible
block without an explicit container-relocation operation or a
location-dependent travel-time adjustment. This boundary is discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{Scenario B: 20\,\% Higher Truck Demand}

Scenario B changes only the empirical working-time inter-arrival
distribution. For demand multiplier $m=1.20$, every empirical
inter-arrival sample $x_i$ is replaced by $x_i/m$. This preserves the
zero mass and the empirical distribution shape while increasing the
modelled arrival intensity by exactly 20\,\%. The gate-admission
calendar, process model, duration models, routing, resource mappings,
resource calendars and case-attribute schema remain unchanged. The
scenario is independent of the T22 resource-pool reallocation. Operationally,
the admitted truck rate is a quantity a terminal can influence in advance
through the number and timing of appointment slots. Because the available log
does not contain individual slot bookings or rejection decisions, this scenario
represents a change in the effective admitted arrival intensity; it is not a
detailed model of a particular appointment-system policy.

\paragraph{Final Domain-Constrained RMG Capacity}

The freely discovered serialized source assigns maximum concurrency four
to ten RMG block identifiers and five to twelve, for an aggregate effective
upper bound of 100. ProSiT obtains these numbers from overlapping timestamps;
they are empirical concurrency proxies rather than equipment counts. They
exceed the hard physical upper bound of three RMGs per block, which was
confirmed by operational experts from the terminal. The final
effective baseline replaces every RMG value $c_r$
with $\min(c_r,3)$. The aggregate upper bound becomes $22\times3=66$. This
does not assert that three cranes normally serve landside trucks: in regular
operation two cranes may serve the waterside and at most one may serve the
landside, but that allocation depends on ship operations and is not observed
in this event log. Three is a reasonable physical ceiling, not an
estimated landside staffing level. No resource
mapping, calendar, selection rule, timing model, arrival model or
control-flow parameter is changed. The T22 and demand interventions are
then independently re-derived from this capped baseline.

The expert confirmation applies to the ceiling, not to the learned HO2 and LL
concurrency values, the assumption that all 168 weekly resource-calendar slots
are open, or the simulator's assignment and queue semantics. Those quantities
remain empirical or technical abstractions and are evaluated as such.

This is a transparent domain constraint rather than a data-driven
recalibration: the freely discovered source remains frozen as discovery
evidence, whereas the cap-three copy is the authoritative effective CTB
baseline. An uncapped run is retained only as a diagnostic sensitivity to
show what the overlap-derived parameter would imply. Both sets of
replications use identical seeds, trace counts and start time. In addition
to direct cap effects, the diagnostic comparison reports
the interaction
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\mathrm{interaction}}=
(Y_{\mathrm{scenario}}-Y_{\mathrm{baseline}})_{c\leq3}
-(Y_{\mathrm{scenario}}-Y_{\mathrm{baseline}})_{\mathrm{discovered}},
\end{equation}
so that changes in scenario response are not confused with a general
level shift caused by the cap.

\paragraph{Capacity-Pressure Diagnostic}

The demand intervention is additionally checked against block-level offered
load in the held-out log. For each observed RMG block $b$, event rate
$\lambda_b$, mean recorded service time $E[S_b]$, and the physical ceiling
$c_b=3$ define the nominal utilisation diagnostic
\begin{equation}
    \rho_b=\frac{\lambda_b E[S_b]}{c_b}.
\end{equation}
If routing and service remain fixed, the 20\,\% intervention implies
$\rho_b'=1.2\rho_b$. This calculation does not estimate queueing time and is
not used to calibrate the scenario. It tests whether the intervention even
approaches mean saturation under the capacity assumption. A separate
minute-grid overlap check reports the observed share of time at or above
capacity; it is interpreted cautiously because minute-resolved timestamps
can create or hide short overlaps and because block identifiers are not crane
trajectories. The reproducible implementation is
\texttt{validation/18\_rmg\_capacity\_pressure\_audit.py}.

\paragraph{Demand-Saturation Ladder}

Because the offered-load diagnostic places nominal mean saturation near a
realised elapsed arrival-rate ratio of 2.37, a separate stress test evaluates
$m\in\{1.0,1.2,1.5,2.0,2.4,3.0\}$. Each level is derived from the same frozen
\textit{rules$+$workload} cap-three bundle by replacing every empirical
working-time inter-arrival sample $x_i$ with $x_i/m$. The process net, case
volume, seed, resource mappings, capacities, calendars, duration models, and
case-attribute generation remain fixed. Ten matched seeds therefore produce
60 runs of 17,892 cases. Structural case contracts are enforced at every
level; duration tails above 24 hours are reported rather than rejected because
they may be a substantive outcome under deliberate overload.

The configured value $m$ scales working-time inter-arrivals. It is not assumed
to equal the arrival-rate multiplier measured over elapsed calendar time,
because gate closures remain fixed and the simulated horizon shortens as
demand rises. Both quantities are therefore reported. The capacity comparison
and response-curve x-axis use the realised elapsed-rate ratio relative to the
matched baseline; this places the response on the same scale as the independent
2.37 offered-load estimate.

This ladder is a diagnostic response-surface experiment, not a forecast that
CTB would admit two or three times its observed truck volume. It distinguishes
an intervention that is simply too modest to reach the model's capacity region
from a simulator that fails to propagate demand pressure even when the
nominal offered-load threshold is crossed. The executable implementation
is \texttt{saturation\_experiment.py} in the reviewer handover package.

\paragraph{Reporting}

The unified driver \texttt{validation/09\_multi\_seed\_scenarios.py}
executes baseline and both interventions with ten matched seeds
(42--51), $n_{\text{traces}}=17{,}892$ and
$t_{\text{start}}=\text{2026-04-20 18:17}$. All paired deltas use
common random numbers. Mean, median and 90th percentile of turnaround,
RMG service and RMG pre-service delay are reported together with the
observed arrival KPIs. For the T22 experiment, service and pre-service
effects are reported separately for
\texttt{RMG\_receive} and \texttt{RMG\_delivery}. Every one of the 30
generated logs is subjected
to the sequential case contract of
Section~\ref{sec:process_discovery_methodology}; any violation aborts
the run. A separate diagnostic invocation with \texttt{--no-rmg-cap}
reproduces the 30 uncapped overlap-capacity replications.
\texttt{validation/10\_compare\_rmg\_capacity\_sensitivity.py} verifies
identical source hashes and settings before computing the paired direct
effects and interactions. These uncapped results are diagnostic only and
are excluded from the authoritative final result table; the 30 cap-three
scenario logs form the final scenario evidence.
The 60 demand-saturation replications are stored separately and interpreted as
a diagnostic stress test rather than a third operational policy scenario.

To test whether scenario response depends on the selected state abstraction,
the same cap-three baseline and interventions are also executed with the
workload-blind \textit{rules} bundle, using the identical ten seeds. For
configuration $c$, scenario $q$, and seed $i$, the paired response is
$d_{c,q,i}=Y_{c,q,i}-Y_{c,0,i}$. The configuration sensitivity is then the
difference-in-differences
\begin{equation}
    D_{q,i}=d_{\textit{rules+workload},q,i}
             -d_{\textit{rules},q,i}.
\end{equation}
Student-$t$ intervals over the ten $D_{q,i}$ values distinguish a response
that is robust across state abstractions from one that appears only in a
particular parameter bundle. The comparison is implemented in
\texttt{validation/17\_compare\_scenario\_state\_ablation.py}.

\section{Descriptive Transition-Duration Analysis}
\label{sec:results_percentile_sens_design}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:lifecycle_events}, an earlier
pipeline version reconstructed activity enablement from route-specific
transition baselines. Although this approach was abandoned in favour of
ProSiT's internal model-derived enablement, the empirical transition
durations remain informative as descriptive evidence of the physical
separation between terminal activities.

To characterise this physical component, the same baseline table is
recomputed with the 5th, 10th, 25th and 50th percentiles as
alternative choices. For each transition $(a,b)$ with at least twenty
observations the resulting baseline duration is reported together with
the shift relative to the 5th percentile and the Pearson correlation
between percentiles. This analysis is implemented in
\texttt{validation/04\_baseline\_percentile\_sensitivity.py} and
reported in Section~\ref{sec:results_percentile_sens}. It does
\textbf{not} affect ProSiT's simulation-parameter discovery; its
purpose is to illustrate that the model-derived pre-service delay
includes a non-trivial physical-movement component that cannot be
separated from operational waiting with the available data.

\section{ProSiT Implementation Notes and Workarounds}
\label{sec:prosit_workarounds}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Applying ProSiT 1.0.3 \cite{Vinci2026prosit} to the CTB event log
uncovered several implementation issues that would have propagated as
silent misbehaviour if not addressed. This section documents them so
that the experiments can be reproduced deterministically and so that the
downstream fidelity numbers can be interpreted correctly.

\paragraph{Data-Attribute Discovery on NaN Columns}

The default \texttt{attribute\_mode='distribution'} passes each case
attribute through a per-attribute distribution fitter. In the CTB log,
\texttt{full\_ratio} and \texttt{is\_full} contain \texttt{NaN} for
cases that involve empty-container-only visits. The distribution fitter
in ProSiT 1.0.3 forwards the raw values to
\texttt{scipy.stats}, which raises
\texttt{TypeError: ufunc 'isfinite' not supported} on the mixed
dtype. Switching to \texttt{attribute\_mode='empirical'} bypasses the
per-attribute fitter and samples full attribute tuples from the
observed joint distribution instead. This is not a fidelity compromise:
the discovered simulator still preserves case-level attribute
correlations, and empirical sampling from the training distribution is
the appropriate choice given the number of categorical attributes
present in the CTB log.

\paragraph{Simulation in No-Rules Mode}

In \textit{no-rules} mode ProSiT samples the routing choice via
\texttt{random.choice(seq)} where \texttt{seq} is a
\texttt{numpy.ndarray}. Python's built-in \texttt{random.choice}
evaluates \texttt{if not seq:}, which raises
\texttt{ValueError: The truth value of an array with more than one
element is ambiguous.} on multi-element numpy arrays. The workaround is
a minimal monkey patch that intercepts \texttt{random.choice} while the
engine is running and forwards \texttt{numpy.ndarray} arguments through
\texttt{numpy.random.randint}. The patch is contained to the
simulation call and is restored immediately afterwards. It is applied
by both \texttt{baseline/07\_run\_prosit\_discovery.py} and
\texttt{validation/06\_multi\_seed\_ci.py}.

\paragraph{Duplicated Columns in the Simulated Log}

ProSiT 1.0.3 writes case-level attributes into the event-level frame
returned by \texttt{SimulatorEngine.apply()}. When a case-level
attribute has the same name as an event attribute (in the CTB log this
happens for \texttt{enabled:timestamp} because ProSiT writes case-level
attributes into the event-level frame), the resulting
\texttt{pandas.DataFrame}
carries duplicate column labels. Standard operations such as
\texttt{pd.to\_datetime(df[col])} then return a \texttt{DataFrame} slice
instead of a \texttt{Series} and fail. The workaround is a single
\texttt{df.loc[:, \textasciitilde df.columns.duplicated()]} call at the
start of every KPI-computation function. It is applied both in the
what-if scripts and in the multi-seed CI driver.

\paragraph{Temporal Features Outside the ProSiT Input}

The final discovery contract does not pass hour-of-day or day-of-week
attributes to ProSiT. It keeps the five standard event-log columns and the
approved visit-related, demand, utilisation and target attributes from
Table~\ref{tab:eventlog_attributes}. Other columns are removed before
simulation-parameter discovery. Hour and weekday are derived from timestamps
only in the separate arrival and explanatory analyses. The retained ProSiT
configurations therefore do not condition their timing rules directly on
calendar features. This is an input-model boundary when interpreting the
day/night differences reported in Section~\ref{sec:results_arrival_process}.

\paragraph{Non-Deterministic Simulation Engine}

\texttt{SimulatorEngine.apply()} does not accept a seed argument. The
Monte-Carlo protocol of Section~\ref{sec:mc_protocol} seeds
Python's \texttt{random} and \texttt{numpy.random} before every call to
\texttt{apply()}. Seeding alone is insufficient across Python processes,
however. ProSiT obtains enabled transitions from a set, while PM4Py hashes
transition objects by in-memory identity. After a model is reloaded, the same
enabled transitions can therefore be presented to the weighted sampler in a
different order and consume the same random draw differently. The reviewer
runner canonicalises this list by the stable Petri-net transition name
immediately before the existing weighted draw. It changes neither the net nor
the learned transition weights. Independent fresh-process runs with the same
seed then produce identical result tables. This stable ordering, together with
the two random seeds, is the reproducibility guarantee used for the scenario
results in Chapter~\ref{chap:results}.

\paragraph{Contribution Boundary}

The ProSiT engine itself was not modified for these workarounds. The
CTB pipeline patches its own callers so that ProSiT can be invoked
without source-code changes. The workarounds and the documented input boundary
constitute the technical adaptation of ProSiT to the CTB case study. They are
listed here so that a reader can distinguish the contribution of this
thesis (case-study specific data engineering, held-out validation
methodology, multi-seed replication, sensitivity analysis, what-if
scenario protocol) from the contribution of ProSiT itself (automatic simulation-parameter discovery from event logs \cite{Vinci2026prosit}).
