\chapter{Results}
\label{chap:results}

This chapter reports the empirical evidence produced by the pipeline
defined in Chapter~\ref{chp:experiments_and_framework}. Every number in
this chapter is traceable to a script and an artefact in the
repository; every claim is qualified by its confidence interval or its
inherent uncertainty. The chapter is organised so that the reader
encounters the evidence in the same order in which the hypotheses of
Section~\ref{sec:data_aware_hypotheses} depend on it.

Section~\ref{sec:results_eventlog_char} reports the descriptive
characteristics of the training and testing partitions produced by the
split of Section~\ref{sec:train_test_split}.
Section~\ref{sec:results_process_discovery} reports the discovered
Petri net and its conformance on both partitions (RQ2).
Section~\ref{sec:results_arrival_process} reports the temporal
structure of the arrival process (H1).
Section~\ref{sec:results_holdout_fidelity} reports the held-out
Monte-Carlo fidelity of the ProSiT configurations
(RQ1, H2, H3). Section~\ref{sec:results_per_activity} decomposes the
fidelity by activity to identify where each configuration wins or
loses. Section~\ref{sec:results_resource_capacities} reports the
discovered resource capacities and their limits.
Section~\ref{sec:results_percentile_sens} provides a descriptive
analysis of transition durations.
Section~\ref{sec:results_scenarios} reports the two what-if scenarios
(H4). Section~\ref{sec:limitations} discusses the methodological
limitations. Section~\ref{sec:results_summary} consolidates the
empirical findings against the hypotheses.

\section{Process Discovery and Held-Out Conformance}
\label{sec:results_process_discovery}

The authoritative simulation net is the sequential observed-variant
trie described in Section~\ref{sec:process_discovery_methodology}. It
contains 76 places and 144 transitions and represents 70 training
variants. Unlike the diagnostic inductive process tree, it cannot
enable two yard activities within the same truck case concurrently.
Cross-case resource multitasking remains enabled. Table~\ref{tab:conformance_train_test}
reports conformance on the frozen partitions.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering

\textbf{Metric} & \textbf{Train} & \textbf{Test} \\
\midrule

Fitting traces (\%)              & 100.000 & 99.944 \\

The test partition contains 53 variants. Ten cases follow ten variants
that were not observed in training, corresponding to only 0.0559\,\%
of the 17,892 test cases. This accounts for the small fitness deviation
from one. The lower generalisation score is expected from a strict
variant trie: the design intentionally trades permissiveness for an
auditable case language of exactly one Gate In, one or more sequential
yard activities and exactly one final Gate Out. It does not imply that
yard resources cannot work on different trucks simultaneously.

\medskip

\noindent\textbf{RQ2 (structural quality).} The authoritative net
replays 99.944\,\% of held-out traces perfectly and achieves log fitness
0.99985 while enforcing the required within-case sequence. It is
therefore a defensible simulation skeleton for this application. Its
strict variant representation, and the resulting generalisation score,
remain an explicit modelling trade-off.

\section{Temporal Structure of the Arrival Process}
\label{sec:results_arrival_process}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

The arrival process is the clearest case for time-conditioning in the
CTB event log. Table~\ref{tab:arrival_benchmark} compares a pooled
arrival-rate baseline (a single global mean) against a time-aware
decision tree (features: hour, weekday, weekend indicator) on 30-minute
arrival counts derived from the training partition. Both models are
evaluated on a chronological 80/20 split within the training partition
so that the numbers reflect the same temporal-hold-out logic used for
the simulator evaluation later.

\subsection{RQ1: Point Estimate of Held-Out Fidelity}

The frozen calibrated simulator is evaluated in ten replications of
17,892 cases using seeds 42--51 and the final cutoff
$t_{\text{start}}=\text{2026-04-20 18:17}$. Its mean service-time EMD
is $2.581\;[2.447,2.715]$ minutes, case-turnaround EMD is
$9.400\;[9.296,9.504]$ minutes and inter-arrival EMD is
$0.0724\;[0.0674,0.0774]$ minutes. Real mean turnaround is 39.742
minutes compared with a simulated $30.343\;[30.239,30.447]$ minutes;
real P90 is 71.0 minutes compared with a simulated
$51.300\;[50.954,51.646]$ minutes. The residual error is therefore a
systematic \emph{under-prediction}, not an upper-tail explosion.

All ten runs contain zero gate-only cases, zero within-case overlaps,
zero invalid case boundaries, zero decreasing completion sequences and
zero cases in which Gate Out precedes the final yard activity. No
service event or case turnaround exceeds 24 hours. The final model is
thus structurally valid and stable, while its duration bias remains an
explicit limitation.

\subsection{H2: Does the Data-Aware Configuration Improve Fidelity?}

The comparison between \textit{no-rules} and
\textit{rules$+$workload} directly tests H2. On every fidelity metric
except inter-arrival, the distribution-only configuration produces
strictly lower (better) values, and the 95\,\% confidence intervals
are disjoint:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Case turnaround EMD: \textit{no-rules}
          $13.94\;[13.15, 14.73]$ vs.\
          \textit{rules$+$workload} $21.75\;[20.60, 22.90]$. The CIs
          are disjoint; \textit{rules$+$workload} is 56\,\% worse.
    \item Service EMD: \textit{no-rules} $1.91\;[1.89, 1.93]$ vs.\
          \textit{rules$+$workload} $2.00\;[1.94, 2.06]$. The CIs
          barely overlap; the point-estimate direction favours
          \textit{no-rules}.
    \item Inter-arrival EMD: \textit{rules$+$workload}
          $0.314\;[0.313, 0.315]$ vs.\
          \textit{no-rules} $0.319\;[0.317, 0.320]$. Here the
          tree-based configuration wins, but the absolute
          difference is 0.005 minutes ($\approx 0.3$ seconds).
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{H2 is rejected.} The data-aware configuration does
not improve fidelity on the CTB event log. The distribution-only
simulator achieves a 56\,\% lower case-turnaround EMD with disjoint
confidence intervals. The only gain for the tree-based variant is on
the inter-arrival distribution and it is operationally negligible.

\subsection{H3: Does the Workload-Feature Configuration Improve
Fidelity?}

H3 cannot be tested in isolation because we compare \textit{no-rules}
(the distribution-only baseline) against
\textit{rules$+$workload} (the full data-aware variant). Since the
combined effect of enabling both trees and workload features produces
strictly worse fidelity than the no-rules baseline, H3 is subsumed by
the H2 rejection. Workload features do not compensate for the
fidelity loss introduced by the decision trees.

\noindent\textbf{H3 is rejected.} Adding queue-length and workload
features to the ProSiT waiting-time and resource-selection models does
not improve held-out fidelity on the CTB event log.

\subsection{Methodological Note on Temporal Semantics}
\label{sec:results_temporal_note}

The event log used for discovery and validation contains only observed
timestamps (\texttt{start:timestamp} and \texttt{time:timestamp}).
Activity enablement is derived internally by ProSiT via A* alignment
against the discovered Petri net.  This means:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Service-time EMD compares real vs.\ simulated
          $\text{complete}-\text{start}$ per activity — both quantities
          are directly available.
    \item Case-turnaround EMD compares real vs.\ simulated
          $\text{last\_complete}-\text{first\_start}$ per case — both
          quantities are directly available.
    \item Pre-service delay (ProSiT ``waiting time'') is available only
          from the simulated log, where ProSiT emits the model-derived
          \texttt{enabled:timestamp}. A cross-log comparison of waiting
          time is not performed because the real log does not contain
          an observed enablement timestamp.
\end{itemize}

The configuration-screening result is not the final baseline result.
In particular, its single-run tail behaviour must not be transferred to
the calibrated model, whose ten-seed validation instead shows systematic
turnaround under-prediction (Table~\ref{tab:final_kpis}).

\label{sec:results_per_activity}

The aggregate fidelity numbers of
Section~\ref{sec:results_holdout_fidelity} hide the per-activity
performance of the ProSiT trees. To locate where data-aware
conditioning does and does not add value,
Table~\ref{tab:tree_r2_train_test} reports the coefficient of

the sensitivity study \texttt{05\_data\_aware\_discovery\_evaluation.py}

\label{tab:tree_r2_train_test}
\begin{tabular}{lr}
\toprule
\textbf{Activity} & \textbf{$R^{2}$} \\

\bottomrule

Gate activities have $R^{2}=1.000$ because their service duration is
constant zero by construction: Gate In and Gate Out have identical
start and completion timestamps
(Section~\ref{sec:lifecycle_events}). For the seven yard activities the
$R^{2}$ ranges from $-0.005$ (RMG activities) to $0.263$
(\texttt{LL\_delivery}). RMG activities have essentially no
context-explainable variance under the available features, and yet the
grand mean of the CTB event log is dominated by RMG (over 60\,\% of all
yard events). This explains the H2 result of
Section~\ref{sec:results_holdout_fidelity}: the ProSiT trees are asked
to predict activities whose duration variance is not explained by the
available case-level attributes, and the resulting leaf-level
distribution fits are noisier than the empirical marginal fit that
\textit{no-rules} uses. The overall fidelity metric of
Section~\ref{sec:results_holdout_fidelity} is therefore an aggregate in
which the RMG activities (large weight, no context-explainable
variance) dominate the LL activities (small weight, meaningful
context-explainable variance).

\section{Final Discovered Resource Representation}
\label{sec:results_resource_capacities}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

The frozen parameter bundle contains 26 resource identifiers and the
maximum-concurrency values in Table~\ref{tab:capacities_train80}. These
are the parameters actually used by the final validation and scenario
runs. The earlier pool-level report produced by
\texttt{baseline/04\_resource\_concurrency\_discovery.py} is an
exploratory diagnostic and is not the authoritative simulator
configuration.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Resource representation in the frozen calibrated baseline.}
\label{tab:capacities_train80}
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\toprule
\textbf{Family} & \textbf{Identifiers} & \textbf{Eligible activities} &
\textbf{Maximum concurrency} \\
\midrule
Gate In  & \texttt{Res.GateIn}  & Gate In & 1 \\
Gate Out & \texttt{Res.GateOut} & Gate Out & 1 \\
HO2      & \texttt{HO2} & three HO2 activities & 15 \\
LL       & \texttt{LL}  & three LL activities  & 22 \\
RMG      & \texttt{T06}--\texttt{T27} (22 blocks) & three RMG activities & 4--5$^{a}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\parbox{0.94\textwidth}{\footnotesize $^{a}$Ten RMG block identifiers
have maximum concurrency four and twelve have maximum concurrency five;
their aggregate upper bound is therefore 100 simultaneous RMG tasks.
This exceeds the nominal physical upper bound of 66 implied by at most
three cranes at each of 22 blocks.}
\end{table}

All 168 weekly slots of every final resource calendar are available;
temporal gate closure is represented separately by the arrival
calendar. Each RMG activity can select all 22 block identifiers, and the
resource-selection rules supply statistical weights. Closing T22 thus
leaves 21 eligible identifiers and removes five units of maximum
concurrency. The comparatively high aggregate RMG upper bound also helps
motivate the domain-constrained capacity sensitivity reported in
Section~\ref{sec:results_scenarios}; that experiment tests rather than
assumes whether the upper bound explains the absent congestion effect.

These values are learned effective server capacities, not verified
numbers of cranes, workers or physically interchangeable storage
positions. The 100-versus-66 discrepancy demonstrates that concurrent
event intervals assigned to a block cannot be interpreted one-to-one as
simultaneously operating cranes. In addition, an RMG block label
functions as a selectable resource; it does not create
container-location state. Consequently, the T22 reassignment is
independent of whether the corresponding container could physically be
stored or reached at the replacement block. These are central
boundaries for interpreting Scenario~A and are carried forward
explicitly in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\section{Descriptive Analysis of Transition Durations}
\label{sec:results_percentile_sens}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

As noted in Section~\ref{sec:abandoned_baseline}, an earlier pipeline
version used route-specific transition baselines to reconstruct
activity enablement.  Although this approach was superseded by
ProSiT's internal model-derived enablement, the empirical transition
durations remain informative because they characterise the
physical-movement component that is embedded within ProSiT's
model-derived pre-service delay.

The analysis of Section~\ref{sec:results_percentile_sens_design}
recomputes the baseline table using percentiles $\{5, 10, 25, 50\}$
and reports the mean shift and the rank correlation.
Table~\ref{tab:percentile_sens} summarises the result over the 48
transitions with at least twenty observations.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Empirical transition duration under alternative percentiles
(seconds; 48 transitions with $\ge 20$ observations).}
\label{tab:percentile_sens}
\begin{tabular}{cccccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Percentile} &
\textbf{Mean (s)} &
\textbf{Median (s)} &
\textbf{Mean shift (s)} &
\textbf{P90 shift (s)} &
\textbf{Corr vs.\ p5} \\
\midrule
p5   & 225.8 & 223.5 & $0.0$   & $0.0$   & 1.000 \\

Two properties are relevant for interpreting the model-derived
pre-service delay.  First, the ranking of transitions by duration is
almost invariant to the percentile choice: $\rho$ between p5 and p10
is $0.990$, and even between p5 and p50 it is $0.812$.  Second, the
absolute shift grows quickly: moving from the 5th to the 50th
percentile shifts the mean transition duration by $3.5$\,minutes.
Because physical truck movement is not modelled as a separate activity,
this shift is entirely absorbed into ProSiT's model-derived
pre-service delay.

Figure~\ref{fig:percentile_shift} shows the CDF of the per-transition
shift across the 48 retained transitions.

\begin{figure}[htbp]

\medskip

\noindent\textbf{Interpretation.} The transition-duration analysis
confirms that the model-derived pre-service delay reported in
Section~\ref{sec:results_holdout_fidelity} includes a non-trivial
physical-movement component (at least 3.5\,minutes on average at the
median percentile).  This does not invalidate the ProSiT waiting-time
model --- it identifies a domain-specific limitation: the ``waiting
time'' reported in the CTB context is broader than pure queueing and
should be interpreted as pre-service delay.  This limitation is
discussed in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{Held-Out Baseline Interpretation}

The final baseline reproduces service distributions with a mean
activity-level EMD of 2.581 minutes and arrivals with an EMD of 0.072
minutes. Its case-turnaround EMD is 9.400 minutes. This discrepancy is
systematic rather than Monte-Carlo noise: mean turnaround is
under-predicted by 9.400 minutes and P90 by 19.700 minutes. The narrow
confidence intervals quantify simulation sampling uncertainty for this
one frozen model; they do not quantify model-discovery uncertainty.
Crucially, all ten validation replications satisfy the sequential case
contract and contain no service event or turnaround above 24 hours.

\subsection{Scenario A: T22 Closure}

T22 handles on average 613.2 events per baseline replication, or
4.47\,\% of RMG events. The intervention reduces this count to zero.
Under the discovered capacities, mean RMG pre-service delay increases by 0.070 minutes
($+1.09\,\%$), with a paired 95\,\% interval of
$[+0.012,+0.128]$ minutes. The corresponding mean-turnaround change
is only $+0.092$ minutes and its interval crosses zero. Thus the
simulator detects a small capacity-substitution effect in this
configuration, but it does not support a quantitatively material
turnaround claim.

The pre-service result is not robust to the physical capacity cap. With
all RMG resources limited to three, the pre-service change is
$-0.008\;[-0.076,+0.060]$ minutes and the turnaround change is
$+0.077\;[-0.071,+0.225]$ minutes. The pre-service interaction between
capacity model and T22 response is
$-0.078\;[-0.166,+0.010]$ minutes. Its interval still crosses zero, but
the sign reversal means the small original pre-service effect must not
be treated as a stable operational prediction.

The effect must not be interpreted as a physically complete yard-block
closure study. All three RMG activities retain 21 eligible blocks, with
maximum concurrency four or five in the discovered model and three in
the sensitivity model. More importantly, the
resource selector is not a container-location state model: removal of
T22 causes statistical reassignment to another eligible resource even
when the historical target area was T22. Container relocation, block
compatibility and the additional physical travel caused by relocation
are not represented. The result therefore measures robustness of the
\emph{abstract discovered resource pool}, not the full operational
consequence of closing the physical T22 storage block.

\subsection{Scenario B: 20\,\% Higher Demand}

The white-box parameter intervention is exact in working time: the
empirical mean inter-arrival changes from 0.6830 to 0.5692 minutes and
the implied intensity from 87.842 to 105.411 arrivals per working hour.
The observed calendar-time rate rises by 15.910\,\%, rather than
20\,\%, because closed gate periods occupy fixed elapsed time and the
event log is observed after flooring timestamps to minutes. The same
flooring maps additional sub-minute positive intervals to simultaneous
observations, raising the observed zero-inter-arrival share while the
zero mass of the input distribution itself remains unchanged.

Despite the higher demand, neither mean turnaround nor RMG service or
pre-service delay changes significantly. Within the discovered model,
the resource pool therefore has enough parallel capacity to absorb this
increase. Reducing aggregate RMG capacity from 100 to 66 does not change
that conclusion: capped mean turnaround changes by only
$+0.070\;[-0.038,+0.179]$ minutes. The cap-by-demand interaction is
$+0.058\;[-0.120,+0.236]$ minutes. Hence the excessive discovered
capacity alone does not explain the missing congestion response.

This is a legitimate scenario result, but also a warning:
because spatial container constraints and explicit equipment movements
are absent, it cannot by itself establish that the physical terminal
would absorb 20\,\% more demand without congestion.

\medskip

\noindent\textbf{H4 is partially supported.} Both direct interventions
are executed as specified: T22 assignments fall to zero and the demand
parameter increases working-time arrival intensity by exactly 20\,\%.
The T22 closure produces a small statistically detectable increase in
mean RMG pre-service delay only under the discovered capacity model; the
effect disappears and reverses sign under the physical cap. Neither
intervention produces a statistically detectable mean-turnaround effect
under either capacity abstraction, so H4 does not support a strong
operational decision recommendation.

\section{Methodological Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Model-Derived Waiting Time Includes Physical Movement}

ProSiT follows process-model semantics in which an activity becomes
enabled when the control-flow state permits its execution.  In the CTB
process, however, consecutive operational activities occur at spatially
separated terminal locations.  Physical truck movement between these
locations is not represented as a separate activity in the discovered
process model.  Consequently, the model-derived interval between
enablement and activity start includes both physical transition time
and operational waiting:
\begin{equation}
    D_{\mathrm{pre\text{-}service}} \approx
    T_{\mathrm{physical}} +
    T_{\mathrm{traffic}} +
    T_{\mathrm{queue}} +
    T_{\mathrm{other}}.
\end{equation}
These components are not individually identifiable from the available
data.  Individual truck travel times are not directly observed; the
scheduling-system transition durations combine movement with congestion
and other operational effects.  Separating the components would require
position or trajectory data not available in the CTB systems.  The
thesis deliberately retains the standard ProSiT semantics rather than
adding an unobserved transition-time model.

Waiting-time results should therefore be interpreted as
\textit{pre-service delay} rather than as a direct measurement of
queueing or equipment-induced waiting.

\subsection{Resource Assignment Is Not Spatial Container Relocation}

The RMG resource selected by ProSiT is an abstract capacity assignment.
Its aggregate learned maximum concurrency is 100, whereas the stated
physical maximum of three cranes per block implies at most 66 across 22
blocks. This mismatch is evidence that the discovered overlap capacity
also absorbs event-log and process abstractions; it is not a physical
crane count. The cap-3 sensitivity nevertheless shows no significant
direct baseline shift and no significant cap-by-demand interaction.
Therefore the excessive discovered capacity is not, by itself, an
empirically supported explanation for the absence of congestion in the
20\,\% demand scenario.

Although target-area attributes can enter a discovered decision tree,
they are predictors rather than a hard container-inventory constraint.
The simulator does not maintain a state that binds each container to
its actual storage block, nor does it create a relocation activity when
the selected block differs from that historical location. Consequently,
closing T22 redistributes its statistical resource demand across the
remaining eligible blocks without modelling relocation feasibility,
yard-crane repositioning or the resulting change in physical truck
travel. The T22 experiment is valid as a white-box test of the
discovered resource-pool abstraction, but not as a stand-alone forecast
of the physical closure of yard block T22.

\subsection{Systematic Turnaround Under-Prediction}

The final calibrated model under-predicts mean turnaround by 9.400
minutes and P90 by 19.700 minutes. The narrow cross-seed intervals show
that this is systematic model bias rather than simulation noise. The
available event log cannot identify how much of the missing time is
physical movement, traffic, queueing or an unrecorded operational
state. This limits absolute KPI forecasting even though the generated
case order and duration tails satisfy the formal validation contract.

\subsection{Scenario Confidence Intervals Exclude Structural Uncertainty}

The matched-seed intervals in Table~\ref{tab:final_kpis} quantify random
simulation variation conditional on one frozen discovered model. They
do not cover uncertainty from repeating discovery on another training
window or adding the missing spatial container state. The cap-3
sensitivity explicitly tests one alternative resource-capacity
abstraction, but not resource-location feasibility or equipment
movement. Increasing the number of seeds would narrow sampling intervals
but would not resolve those remaining structural sources of uncertainty.

\section{Interpretive Conclusion of the Results Chapter}
\label{sec:results_interpretive_conclusion}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

The main empirical contribution of this thesis is therefore not the
discovery of a better simulator through data-aware modelling, but the
demonstration that on this specific dataset the additional modelling
machinery of ProSiT does not translate into higher fidelity. The
observation, together with the multi-seed protocol that reveals it and
the workload-feature variance-reduction result, is the operational
finding that Chapter~\ref{chp:conclusion} builds upon.
