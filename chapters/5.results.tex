\chapter{Results}
\label{chap:results}

This chapter reports the results for the four research questions from
Chapter~\ref{chp:experiments_and_framework}. RQ2 comes first. The control-flow
contract must be usable before timing can be evaluated. RQ1 then compares the
final model with the temporal hold-out. RQ3 compares the two retained ProSiT
configurations. The next sections examine context, arrivals, resources, and
transition times. These diagnostics help interpret the results, but they do
not prove their causes. RQ4 evaluates the two model interventions.

The chapter uses three types of evidence: real-versus-simulated hold-out
comparisons, comparisons between configurations, and paired scenario changes.
They answer different questions. The limitations are discussed before the
final answers to RQ1--RQ4.

\section{Control-Flow Stability Across the Held-Out Period}
\label{sec:results_process_discovery}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

The final simulator uses the raw prefix trie from
Section~\ref{sec:process_discovery_methodology}. It has 76 places and 144
transitions for 70 training variants. It is not a general process abstraction.
It stores the observed sequence language. Training fitness and precision of one
follow from this construction. They are not results. Table~\ref{tab:conformance_train_test}
also reports the held-out coverage and structural measures.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Prefix-trie conformance and structural descriptors on train and test.}
\label{tab:conformance_train_test}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Metric} & \textbf{Train} & \textbf{Test} \\
\midrule
Log fitness                      & 1.0000 & 0.9998 \\
Fitting traces (\%)              & 100.000 & 99.944 \\
Average trace fitness            & 1.0000 & 0.9990 \\
Token-based precision            & 1.0000 & 0.9605 \\
Generalisation                   & 0.5767 & 0.3732 \\
Simplicity                       & 0.6179 & 0.6179 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The held-out result is more useful. The trie accepts only training variants.
The test log contains 53 variants, of which 43 were seen in training. These 43
variants cover 17,882 of 17,892 test cases. The other ten cases each follow a
different unseen variant. Only 0.0559\,\% of test cases lie outside the stored
training language.

The high fitness is not the main result here. The trie is intentionally designed to fit known variants. The main result is the repetition in the CTB data. A model that
cannot create a new route still covers almost the full test period. Activity frequencies change between train and test, as shown in
Section~\ref{sec:results_eventlog_char}. The complete activity orders hardly change.

This restriction has a clear cost. Test generalisation is 0.37327 and
simplicity is 0.61798. The trie is not compact and cannot generate unseen combinations. It does provide a strict case-order contract: one Gate In, one or more sequential yard activities, and one final Gate Out. Different trucks can still use resources at the same time. The path of each case must have occurred in training.

The model can test timing, resources, and arrivals on these historical paths. It cannot support a claim that a general CTB process model was discovered. It also cannot create a new exception path, handling step, or activity order. A structural response to a disruption is missing unless the same sequence already happened during training.

\medskip

\noindent\textbf{RQ2 answer.} The trie covers 99.944\,\% of test cases and
enforces the required order. This shows that the recorded CTB trace language is
highly repetitive. It does not show that the trie generalises. The trie is a
useful execution contract for simulations on known paths. It does not cover new
truck-process structures.

\subsection{Deliberate Choice of the Scenario Model}

\textit{No-rules} is the better fidelity reference in this screening. The
scenario experiment has another purpose. It tests whether the data-aware
white-box model can be changed and inspected. The calibrated
\textit{rules$+$workload} model is used for that reason. It is not used because
it predicted the hold-out better. RQ4 tests white-box model experiments and
their limits. It is not a test of the best CTB forecaster.

\noindent\textbf{RQ3 answer.} More contextual expressiveness does not improve
fidelity in the retained comparison. The tree-and-workload endpoint has worse
turnaround results and more seed variation. The missing middle configuration
prevents a separate conclusion about workload features.

\subsection{Meaning of the Time Measures}
\label{sec:results_temporal_note}

The real log has start and completion timestamps, but no enablement timestamp.
Service-time EMD uses $\text{complete}-\text{start}$. Turnaround EMD uses
$\text{last\_complete}-\text{first\_start}$. Both can be calculated for both
logs. ProSiT derives enablement internally and writes it only to the simulated
log. Simulated $\text{start}-\text{enabled}$ is called pre-service delay. It
cannot be validated against real waiting time.

All 168 weekly slots in each resource calendar are open. Gate closures are
handled by the arrival calendar. Every RMG activity can select any of the 22
block identifiers. The learned rules provide the weights. Removing T22 leaves
21 identifiers and removes three capacity units from the final model.

The raw RMG capacities sum to 100. The stated physical limit is 66: three
cranes at each of 22 blocks. This mismatch is important. The discovered values
describe overlapping event intervals. They are not verified crane counts.
The cap removes the implausible upper bound. It does not prove that 66 is the
effective capacity of the terminal.

This is the second main domain correction in the pipeline. The prefix trie was
needed to enforce valid case order. The resource cap was needed to enforce a
physical upper bound. The final simulator is a hybrid of discovered
parameters and domain constraints. It is not an unchanged output of automatic
discovery.

An RMG block is still only a selectable resource label. It is not container
location state. After T22 is removed, the model can assign work elsewhere
without checking storage or travel feasibility. This limit is central to
Scenario~A and is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{Scenario A: T22 Resource-Pool Reallocation}

T22 receives 604.8 assignments per baseline run. This is 4.41\,\% of RMG
events. After removal, it receives none. The intervention executes as
intended. The mean-turnaround change is
$+0.012\;[-0.102,+0.127]$ minutes. Its paired interval includes zero. The
intervals for mean RMG service and pre-service delay also include zero.

Receive service changes by $-0.043\;[-0.110,+0.024]$ minutes and receive
pre-service by $+0.016\;[-0.039,+0.071]$ minutes. Delivery service changes
by $+0.035\;[-0.078,+0.147]$ minutes and delivery pre-service by
$-0.036\;[-0.087,+0.015]$ minutes. All four paired intervals include zero.
The experiment does not show a clear difference between receive and delivery.
This is not evidence that a physical T22 closure has no effect.

The experiment is not a full block-closure study. The remaining 21 blocks stay
eligible for all RMG activities. Each keeps capacity three. The selector moves
T22 work to another statistical resource. It does not check container
location, block compatibility, relocation, or added travel. The result only
tests the abstract resource pool.

\subsection{Scenario B: 20\,\% Higher Demand}

The working-time intervention is exact. Mean inter-arrival time changes from
0.6830 to 0.5692 minutes. The implied rate rises from 87.842 to 105.411 arrivals
per working hour, or 20\,\%. The rate measured over elapsed calendar time rises
by 15.910\,\%. Closed gate periods remain fixed. Timestamps are also floored to
minutes. This flooring turns more short positive intervals into simultaneous
observations. It does not change the zero mass in the input distribution.

Mean turnaround changes by $-0.033\;[-0.128,+0.063]$ minutes. The interval
includes zero. The same applies to mean RMG service, mean RMG pre-service
delay, and the receive measures. Delivery pre-service falls by
$-0.090\;[-0.157,-0.022]$ minutes, or about 5.4 seconds. This small change is
clear only for simulation-seed variation in this fixed model. The experiment
does not identify its cause. It is not evidence of delivery prioritisation.

The missing congestion response is itself a model result. The cap of three
does not create enough system interaction to raise turnaround under higher
demand. The model has no explicit queue state, container location, or
ship-side crane allocation. It cannot show that the real terminal could absorb
20\,\% more demand without congestion.

\medskip

\noindent\textbf{RQ4 answer.} Both interventions execute as specified. T22
assignments fall to zero. Working-time arrival intensity rises by exactly
20\,\%. Both mean-turnaround intervals include zero. The model supports clear
and repeatable experiments. It does not support a physical counterfactual or
an operational recommendation from these two results. Executability is not
counterfactual validity.

\section{Methodological Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Pre-Service Delay}

ProSiT enables an activity when the process model allows it. Two consecutive CTB activities can occur at different locations. Truck movement in this Event log set up, is not a separate activity. The time from enablement (finishing a previous activity) to start can combine:
\begin{equation}
    D_{\mathrm{pre\text{-}service}} \approx
    T_{\mathrm{physical}} +
    T_{\mathrm{traffic}} +
    T_{\mathrm{queue}} +
    T_{\mathrm{other}}.
\end{equation}
The log cannot identify these parts separately. The percentile check shows
that a reconstructed baseline is sensitive to its assumption. It does not
measure any one part. This would require position, trajectory, queue-entry, or
similar state data. These data are not available. The results use
the term \textit{pre-service delay}, not queueing time.

\subsection{Resource Assignment Is Not Spatial Container Relocation}

An RMG resource in ProSiT is an abstract capacity assignment. The discovered
overlap capacity is 100. The stated physical cap is 66 across 22 blocks. This
mismatch shows that timestamp overlap is not a crane count. The final baseline
uses the cap of three per block.

The cap does not add spatial state. Target-area attributes are predictors, not
hard inventory constraints. The simulator does not bind a container to a
storage block. It also does not create relocation work after reassignment.
Closing T22 spreads its statistical demand across the remaining blocks. It
does not model feasibility, crane movement, or added truck travel. Scenario~A
tests the resource-pool abstraction. It does not forecast a physical T22
closure.

\subsection{Scenario Control Flow Is Restricted to Historical Variants}

The prefix trie fixes control flow to the 70 training variants. This works for
almost all cases in the later hold-out. It is more restrictive for scenarios.
Every scenario case must follow a known path. A detour, repeated handling step,
exception, or new activity order cannot occur. The two experiments only test
parameter changes inside the historical trace language. They do not test a
structural process response. Missing structural changes may also remove real
sources of delay.

\subsection{Systematic Turnaround Under-Prediction}

The final model under-predicts mean turnaround by 9.306 minutes and P90 by
19.200 minutes. The narrow seed intervals show that this is model bias, not
simulation noise. The log cannot show how much missing time comes from
movement, traffic, queueing, or an unrecorded state. Correct case order does
not remove this limit on KPI forecasts.

\subsection{Scenario Confidence Intervals Exclude Structural Uncertainty}

The matched-seed intervals in Table~\ref{tab:scenario_kpis} cover random
variation in one fixed model. They do not cover a new training period, another
discovery run, or missing spatial state. The cap sets a ceiling but adds no
location feasibility or equipment movement. More seeds would narrow these
intervals. They would not reduce structural uncertainty. The experiments also
use only the \textit{rules$+$workload} configuration. They do not show whether
the better-fitting \textit{no-rules} endpoint would react differently.

\subsection{External Validity Is Limited to One Terminal and Time Window}

The temporal hold-out is one later period from the same terminal and source
systems. The 99.944\,\% trie coverage shows repeated paths in this period. It
does not show transfer to another process. The results do not cover another
terminal, season, gate regime, or infrastructure setup. That would require
another full discovery and validation run.
