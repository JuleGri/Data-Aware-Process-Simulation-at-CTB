\chapter{Results}
\label{chap:results}

This chapter reports the results for the four research questions from
Chapter~\ref{chp:experiments_and_framework}. RQ2 comes first. The control-flow
contract must be usable before timing can be evaluated. RQ1 then compares the
data-aware reference configuration with the temporal hold-out. RQ3 compares three ProSiT
configurations. The next sections examine temporal transfer, context, arrivals,
resources, and transition times. These diagnostics help interpret the results, but they do
not prove their causes. RQ4 evaluates two policy interventions and a
demand-saturation response surface.

The chapter uses six types of evidence: observed train--test transfer,
real-versus-simulated hold-out comparisons, paired configuration contrasts,
a structural robustness repair, paired policy changes, and a saturation stress
test.
They answer different questions. The limitations are discussed before the
final answers to RQ1--RQ4.

\section{Control-Flow Stability Across the Held-Out Period}
\label{sec:results_process_discovery}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

The final simulator uses the Inductive-Miner Petri net from
Section~\ref{sec:process_discovery_methodology}. The order-preserving XES log
produces a compact model with 7 places, 16 transitions, 32 arcs, and no
parallel operator. It abstracts from 70 training variants rather than storing
each path separately. Table~\ref{tab:conformance_train_test} reports its
training and held-out quality measures.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Metric} & \textbf{Train} & \textbf{Test} \\
\midrule
Log fitness                      & 1.000000 & 0.999992 \\
Fitting traces (\%)              & 100.000 & 99.994 \\
Average trace fitness            & 1.000000 & 0.999993 \\
Token-based precision            & 0.7843 & 0.7558 \\
Generalisation                   & 0.9723 & 0.9413 \\
Simplicity                       & 0.5610 & 0.5610 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Inductive-Miner conformance and structural descriptors on train and test.}
\label{tab:conformance_train_test}
\end{table}

The test log contains 53 variants, ten of which are absent from training and
together account for ten of 17,892 cases (0.0559\,\%). Unlike a prefix trie,
the Inductive-Miner model can replay unseen sequential combinations. It fits
17,891 held-out cases completely and attains a log fitness of 0.999992. The
near-perfect score is therefore substantive held-out evidence rather than a
consequence of memorising the construction log.

The remaining measures show the expected abstraction trade-off. Held-out
precision is 0.7558 because the loop permits more sequential combinations and
repetitions than were observed. This broader language is acceptable for the
stated CTB contract: internal yard order is not prescribed, while concurrency
within one visit is prohibited. Generalisation remains high at 0.9413, and the
7-place net is small enough to inspect directly. A hard operator check confirms
zero parallel cuts, and ten context-aware simulation seeds produce neither
within-case overlap nor a \texttt{Gate Out} before the final yard activity. Although the
formal net contains a silent yard bypass, none of the 178,920 simulated cases
from this endpoint uses it to create a gate-only visit. The distribution-only
endpoint is examined separately under RQ3.

\medskip

\noindent\textbf{RQ2 answer.} The automatically discovered Inductive-Miner net
is suitable as the simulation backbone once the explicit XES event order is
preserved. It combines near-perfect held-out fitness with sequential
generalisation and passes the terminal-expert-validated no-concurrency
contract. Its lower
precision primarily reflects broader yard combinations rather than parallel
execution, but the gate-only bypass is a genuine precision defect.

order or number of yard activities. It adds one place and one silent transition
to distinguish a visit that has already executed \texttt{LL\_mixed} from a
visit that has not yet performed yard work. Table~\ref{tab:gate_bypass_repair}
compares the unchanged discovery result with this repaired robustness model on
the held-out log.

The repair raises precision by 0.0381 without losing held-out replay fitness

and scenario rerun before operational use. A two-seed, 100-case execution
smoke test confirms that the repaired bundle loads, simulates, produces no
gate-only cases, and satisfies the hard sequential case contract. This checks
technical executability only; it is deliberately not reported as a statistical
validation of the repaired model.

\section{Historical Fidelity and Model Screening}

\textit{rules$+$workload} model with the temporal hold-out. RQ3 compares three
equally calibrated configurations: \textit{no-rules}, strict workload-blind
\textit{rules}, and \textit{rules$+$workload}.
All configurations use the same discovered Inductive-Miner net, temporal split,
arrival calibration, and ten-seed protocol. Their difference is therefore the
way routing, duration, and resource behaviour use contextual information.

The paired contrasts resolve effects that the endpoint comparison concealed.
Moving from \textit{no-rules} to workload-blind \textit{rules} lowers
turnaround EMD by 0.115 minutes, but its 95\,\% paired interval
$[-0.266,0.037]$ includes zero. The resolved improvement is instead in
activity incidence: yard-activity-rate L1 error falls by 0.0103
($[-0.0197,-0.0009]$). Mean turnaround moves 0.318 minutes upward
($[0.177,0.460]$), towards the real mean. Weighted service-time EMD changes by
only $+0.005$ minutes ($[-0.071,0.082]$), and P90 by $-0.1$ minutes
($[-0.726,0.526]$); neither difference is resolved.

The next increment, from \textit{rules} to
\textit{rules$+$workload}, does not improve weighted service-time EMD:
the paired change is effectively zero, with a 95\,\% interval of
$[-0.058,0.053]$ minutes. Instead, turnaround EMD rises by 0.303 minutes
($[0.181,0.424]$), mean turnaround falls by 0.501 minutes
($[-0.664,-0.338]$), and activity-rate L1 error rises by 0.0614
($[0.0568,0.0659]$). Thus the available workload state degrades three held-out
dimensions while providing no resolved service-time gain.

The gate-only diagnostic adds a different interpretation. The unchanged net
formally permits the silent yard bypass. Independent optional routing in
\textit{no-rules} samples it in 10.5 of 17,892 visits on average
(0.059\,\%), while both rule-based configurations sample none across 178,920
visits each. This is not a parallelism or case-order violation, and the net is not
physically constrained to suppress it. Instead, it is reported as a small
instance of discovered-language overgeneralisation whose realised frequency
depends on the data-aware routing layer.

These intervals describe seed variation for three fixed bundles. They do not
include discovery uncertainty or another training period. They isolate the
incremental feature sets, but they do not identify the effect of each
individual demand, utilisation, or live-workload predictor.

\paragraph{Deliberate Choice of the Scenario Model}

The workload-blind \textit{rules} configuration is the strongest historical
screening model overall, but the scenario experiment
has another purpose: it tests whether the data-aware
white-box model can be changed and inspected. The calibrated
\textit{rules$+$workload} model is used for that reason. It is not used because
it is an unambiguously better forecaster. RQ4 tests white-box model experiments
and their limits.

\noindent\textbf{RQ3 answer.} Context is beneficial only when the selected
state abstraction carries stable information for the modelled decision.
Process- and case-state rules improve activity incidence and suppress the
rarely sampled silent bypass; their small turnaround-EMD improvement is not
resolved by the paired interval. Adding the available workload state
afterward produces no resolved yard-service improvement and worsens activity
incidence and complete-visit timing. More state is therefore not automatically
more valid state.

\paragraph{Meaning of the Time Measures}
\label{sec:results_temporal_note}

The real log has start and completion timestamps, but no enablement timestamp.
Service-time EMD uses $\text{complete}-\text{start}$. Turnaround EMD uses
$\text{last\_complete}-\text{first\_start}$. Both can be calculated for both
logs. ProSiT derives enablement internally and writes it only to the simulated
log. Simulated $\text{start}-\text{enabled}$ is called pre-service delay. It
cannot be validated against real waiting time.

\section{Temporal Transfer and Error Decomposition}
\label{sec:results_temporal_transfer}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

The observed process changes between discovery and evaluation. Mean truck
turnaround rises from 37.293 minutes in training to 39.742 minutes in the
hold-out, a 2.450-minute increase (95\,\% Welch CI $[2.003,2.896]$).
The median rises from 29 to 32 minutes and P90 from 67 to 71 minutes; the
bootstrapped 95\,\% interval for the P90 increase is $[3,6]$ minutes. The
Wasserstein distance between the two real turnaround distributions is 3.235
minutes. The hold-out is therefore not an exchangeable sample from an
unchanged period.

The change is not confined to case composition. Mean service time increases
for all nine yard activities, with activity-specific increases between 1.20
and 4.01 minutes. Using held-out activity frequencies, their mean shift is
2.120 minutes and their aggregate Wasserstein distance is 2.131 minutes.
At the same time, process-flow composition changes by a total-variation
distance of 0.072 and target-area composition by 0.102. The receive share
falls by 7.22 percentage points.

This drift also challenges a simple congestion interpretation of the available
state variables. Gate and RMG utilisation proxies are lower in the hold-out
(standardised shifts $-1.095$ and $-0.675$), while all yard-service means and
complete turnaround are higher. Several demand measures rise, but the
direction of the utilisation proxies is inconsistent with treating them as a
stable causal measurement of terminal congestion. They remain predictive
features whose relationships must transfer empirically, not mechanistic queue
state. This finding is consistent with recent work on online simulation
discovery, which gives greater weight to recent data while retaining historical
information to handle evolving processes
\cite{vinci2025onlinediscoverysimulationmodels}.

Equation~\ref{eq:mean_transfer_decomposition} separates the final mean deficit
without pretending that the two sources can be identified case by case. For
the cap-three scenario baseline,
\begin{equation}
39.742-31.572=(37.293-31.572)+(39.742-37.293)
=5.721+2.450\ \text{minutes}.
\end{equation}
Approximately 70\,\% of the mean deficit is therefore already present relative
to the training-period mean; the remaining 30\,\% is the later-period mean
shift. For the strict \textit{rules} model, the corresponding calibration
period deficit is 5.200 minutes and the held-out deficit is 7.649 minutes.
This decomposition applies to the means only and is not an additive
decomposition of EMD.

The service-time comparison points in the same direction. The strict
\textit{rules} simulations have a frequency-weighted yard-service EMD of 0.595
minutes against the training data, compared with 2.354 minutes against the
hold-out. Much of the held-out service mismatch is therefore temporal transfer.
Complete turnaround still has a substantial training-period deficit, which
indicates an additional model boundary beyond drift. The distinction is
important because a direct future-log comparison alone can conflate simulator
error with changing process behaviour \cite{Ozdemir2025UtilityBPS}.

\label{sec:results_resource_capacities}

Table~\ref{tab:capacities_train80} shows their maximum concurrency. The source

diagnostic. It is not the simulator configuration.

\label{tab:capacities_train80}

All 168 weekly slots in each resource calendar are open. Gate closures are
handled by the arrival calendar. Every RMG activity can select any of the 22
block identifiers. The learned rules provide the weights. Removing T22 leaves
21 identifiers and removes three capacity units from the final model.
The HO2 and LL concurrency values, fully open resource calendars, and
interchangeable-block assignment mechanism were not covered by the expert
confirmation and remain model abstractions.

The raw RMG capacities sum to 100. The expert-validated physical ceiling is 66:
three RMGs at each of 22 blocks. This mismatch is important. The discovered values
describe overlapping event intervals. They are not verified crane counts.
The cap removes the implausible upper bound. It does not prove that 66 is the
effective capacity of the terminal.

This is the material expert-supported domain correction in the executable parameter bundle.
Preserving XES event order and checking for parallel operators corrects and
guards the discovery input without replacing the discovered control flow. The
resource cap, by contrast, changes the learned capacities to enforce a stated
physical upper bound. The final simulator therefore combines an automatically
discovered Petri net and parameters with one explicit resource-domain
constraint.

An RMG block is still only a selectable resource label. It is not container
location state. After T22 is removed, the model can assign work elsewhere
without checking storage or travel feasibility. This limit is central to
Scenario~A and is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\section{Sensitivity of Transition-Time Baselines}
\label{sec:results_percentile_sens}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

An older pipeline version used route-specific transition baselines to
reconstruct enablement. Section~\ref{sec:lifecycle_events} explains why this
was replaced by ProSiT's own enablement. The old analysis is kept as a
sensitivity check. It shows how much an assumed baseline changes with the
chosen percentile. It does not separate movement, traffic, queueing, or other
unrecorded delay.

The check uses percentiles $\{5, 10, 25, 50\}$ and compares them with p5.

\noindent\textbf{Interpretation.} The lower-tail route values are evidence that
time between recorded activities is physically meaningful and too large to be
treated as zero. They do not identify its mechanism. The data do not separate
movement, traffic, queueing, and other delay, and the real log has no
enablement timestamp. This is consistent with Vinci and de Leoni's
\cite{VinciDeLeoni2025Repair} distinction between repairing duration or
inter-activity time and first resolving resource-driven versus exogenous
waiting. The present model output is consequently called pre-service delay; it
is not validated queueing time.

The raw timetable source contains 433 positive stop-to-stop observations for
multi-stop visits. No individual internal route pair reaches the twenty-case
threshold used for the route table. A route-specific internal-movement model
would therefore be unstable. Pooling those observations gives a p5 of
186.8 seconds (3.11 minutes), a p10 of 237.8 seconds (3.96 minutes), and a
median of 558 seconds (9.30 minutes). The pooled lower tail supports a
non-zero internal movement component, but the sparse route cells do not
support a matrix of yard-to-yard distributions. This is why the present
analysis uses the boundary routes as a scale check and does not insert an
unvalidated duration for every possible loop transition.

Hard-coding these values into silent Petri-net transitions is not a valid
shortcut in the present engine. ProSiT fires silent transitions immediately;
they do not hold a token for a sampled duration. A timed movement step would
therefore require either a visible auxiliary transition retained in the
simulation log or an extension of the simulator's firing semantics. More
importantly, the current pre-service distribution was fitted to the complete
observed inter-activity gap. Adding a transit draw on top would count part of
the same time twice.

A defensible extension would first estimate a non-negative, route-dependent
movement component and then refit the residual:
\begin{equation}
D_{\mathrm{pre\text{-}service}}
=T_{\mathrm{transit}}(r_{\mathrm{from}},r_{\mathrm{to}},z)
+D_{\mathrm{access/queue}}(s,z),
\end{equation}
where $z$ contains only state available at the transition and $s$ is the
current process/resource state. The components need not be statistically
independent: traffic can affect both movement and queue arrival. Independence
would be a simplifying hypothesis to test, not a property implied by the log.
The existing p5 values can initialise or bound the movement component, but
trajectory or position data are required to identify it. State-dependent
transition weights in an SLDPN decide what fires; they cannot supply this
missing timed movement mechanism by themselves
\cite{Vinci2023Inf.Activ.Prob}.

\section{Held-Out Bottleneck Analysis}
\label{sec:results_bottlenecks}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

A bottleneck analysis must distinguish a long individual activity from a
frequent activity at which a large amount of system time accumulates. The
held-out log is therefore decomposed at event level. Recorded handling service
is completion minus start. The non-negative gap from the previous event's
completion to the current event's start is called \emph{composite pre-service
delay}. For the sequential truck process, the sum of both components exactly
reconstructs each case turnaround. Composite pre-service delay can contain
movement, traffic and transfer, dispatching, and queueing; it is not interpreted
as a direct queue measurement.

Across the 17,892 held-out visits, mean turnaround is 39.742 minutes. Recorded
handling contributes 14.755 minutes per visit and composite pre-service delay
contributes 24.987 minutes. The decomposition error is zero for every case.
Table~\ref{tab:bottleneck_burden} ranks activities by their frequency-weighted
contribution rather than their mean duration alone.

\subsection{Scenario A: T22 Resource-Pool Reallocation}

T22 receives 588.4 assignments per baseline run. This is 4.10\,\% of RMG
events. After removal, it receives none. The intervention executes as
intended. The mean-turnaround change is
$+0.072\;[-0.092,+0.235]$ minutes. Its paired interval includes zero. Mean RMG
service changes by $-0.001\;[-0.062,+0.060]$ minutes, whereas mean RMG
pre-service delay changes by $+0.081\;[-0.011,+0.173]$ minutes.

Receive service changes by $-0.002\;[-0.103,+0.099]$ minutes and receive
pre-service by $+0.094\;[-0.003,+0.191]$ minutes. Delivery service changes
by $+0.029\;[-0.034,+0.091]$ minutes and delivery pre-service by
$+0.074\;[-0.091,+0.239]$ minutes. None of these mean-effect intervals
excludes zero. The model therefore verifies the reassignment mechanism but
does not resolve a mean performance effect at case, service or pre-service
level. That distinction is important: removing an eligible resource in the
statistical pool is observable, while a physical closure effect is not
established.

The experiment is not a full block-closure study. The remaining 21 blocks stay
eligible for all RMG activities. Each keeps capacity three. The selector moves
T22 work to another statistical resource. It does not check container
location, block compatibility, relocation, or added travel. The result only
tests the abstract resource pool.

\subsection{Scenario B: 20\,\% Higher Demand}

The working-time intervention is exact. Mean inter-arrival time changes from
0.6830 to 0.5692 minutes. The implied rate rises from 87.842 to 105.411 arrivals
per working hour, or 20\,\%. The rate measured over elapsed calendar time rises
by 15.910\,\%. Closed gate periods remain fixed. Timestamps are also floored to
minutes. This flooring turns more short positive intervals into simultaneous
observations. It does not change the zero mass in the input distribution.

Mean turnaround changes by $+0.005\;[-0.136,+0.147]$ minutes, and mean RMG
pre-service by $+0.009\;[-0.072,+0.090]$ minutes. Both intervals include
zero. Mean RMG service increases by $+0.091\;[+0.037,+0.146]$ minutes, driven
mainly by receive service at $+0.073\;[+0.038,+0.108]$ minutes; delivery
service and both handling-specific pre-service intervals remain unresolved.
The higher arrival intensity therefore produces a small response in the
workload-conditioned service-time component, about 5.5 seconds on average,
but no resolved propagation into total turnaround or accumulated pre-service
delay. The experiment does not identify a physical cause and does not by
itself justify favouring one handling type.

The missing congestion response is itself a model result. The cap of three
does not create enough system interaction to raise turnaround under higher
demand. The model has no explicit queue state, container location, or
ship-side crane allocation. It cannot show that the real terminal could absorb
20\,\% more demand without congestion.

by 1.20. If block routing and service times remained unchanged, the smallest

changes mean turnaround by $+0.025\;[-0.083,+0.134]$ minutes and the demand

increases by $+0.146\;[+0.016,+0.276]$ minutes, whereas the receive pre-service
interval includes zero. Conversely, its 20\,\% demand run has no resolved mean
RMG service or pre-service response.

The matched difference-in-differences is more important than whether one
configuration happens to cross a 95\,\% threshold. For mean turnaround, the
\textit{rules$+$workload} response minus the workload-blind response is
$+0.067\;[-0.087,+0.220]$ minutes for T22 removal and
$+0.159\;[-0.060,+0.377]$ minutes for higher demand. None of the corresponding
headline turnaround, RMG service, receive/delivery service, or RMG pre-service
contrasts exclude zero. Thus the unresolved end-to-end response is robust to
this state-abstraction choice, while the small component-level effects are not
stable enough to attribute to workload features. This is direct evidence of
input-model uncertainty that the within-configuration Monte-Carlo intervals
alone do not show.

\medskip

\noindent\textbf{RQ4 answer.} Both policy interventions execute as specified. T22
assignments fall to zero. Working-time arrival intensity rises by exactly
20\,\%. Both mean-turnaround intervals include zero. The model supports clear
and repeatable experiments and exposes separate receive and delivery effects;
in this run, only the higher-demand intervention produces a resolved mean RMG
service response. That component response is not statistically distinguishable
from the workload-blind configuration's response, whereas the unresolved mean
turnaround is reproduced by both configurations. The capacity-pressure audit
also shows that 20\,\% higher arrivals leave the most-loaded held-out block at
only 0.506 nominal mean utilisation under the three-task ceiling. The extended
demand ladder resolves the ambiguity: at a realised elapsed-rate ratio of
2.367, mean turnaround rises by 4.644 minutes and mean RMG pre-service by 5.878
minutes; stronger levels produce still larger responses. The simulator can
therefore propagate abstract resource pressure, but the threshold and
propagation mechanism have not been validated as physical CTB behaviour.
That capability is the operational reason for simulation: candidate truck-slot
rates and handling policies can be compared before they are applied. The
present model, however, does not support a physical counterfactual or an
operational recommendation from these results because its spatial assignment,
movement and queue semantics are not yet adequate. Executability and even a
plausible stress response are not counterfactual validity.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Interpretation: From Process Mining to Data-Aware Simulation}
\label{sec:results_interpretation}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

The results become most informative when the structural, temporal and
scenario findings are interpreted together. They do not describe one model
that is simply ``good'' or ``bad.'' Instead, they show that different forms of
process-mining evidence answer different questions. The compact Inductive-Miner
net reproduces the held-out control flow while generalising across sequential
yard combinations, the arrival model reproduces an important part of the
workload timing, and the simulator remains too fast at the level of complete
truck visits. These findings are compatible because control-flow conformance,
arrival fidelity, and end-to-end temporal fidelity are distinct properties
\cite{Camargo2021,ChapelaCampa2023Trust,Chapela2025SimQuality}.

\paragraph{Meaning for traditional Process Mining}
Traditional Process Mining is strongest in this study as a language for
reconstructing and checking observed process behaviour. Here, its most
important diagnostic is not merely the near-perfect held-out fitness. It is the
discovery that the apparent parallelism vanished when the explicit XES event
order was preserved. The earlier parallel model represented a timestamp-based
reordering artefact; the final process tree has no parallel operator. This
demonstrates a classical Process-Mining point with direct simulation
consequences: the event-log ordering relation is part of the model input, and a
formally sound discovery can still encode the wrong semantics when coarse
timestamps are treated as an unambiguous order
\cite{PMRozinat2009,PMvanDerAalst2016}.

The final model makes the fitness--precision--generalisation trade-off visible.
It fits 99.994\,\% of held-out traces and retains a held-out generalisation of
0.9413, including behaviour not stored as an exact training path. Precision is
lower at 0.7558 because the loop admits additional yard combinations and
repetitions. In this case, that broader language is not equivalent to physical
parallelism and is not automatically an error: the terminal process allows
varying sequential activity orders. The domain check must therefore be
semantic and operator-specific. Penalising every unseen order would collapse
discovery into memorisation, while accepting a parallel cut would violate the
truck-visit contract.

The silent gate-only path makes the same trade-off concrete. It is permitted
by the discovered language and retained as an auditable finding rather than
removed through an undocumented guard. Its rarity in simulation---0.059\,\%
for \textit{no-rules} and zero cases for \textit{rules$+$workload}---shows why
formal language analysis and realised-output validation answer complementary
questions. A permitted path need not be common, yet it remains relevant when
the model is interpreted beyond the observed period.

This is a precision-side model-repair problem. The simulation-guided framework
of Vinci and de Leoni \cite{VinciDeLeoni2025Repair} provides a rigorous way to
identify differences between real and simulated control flow and to compare an
original and repaired net on held-out data. Its published operators currently
add missing behaviour, however, whereas the CTB bypass requires restricting
excess behaviour. De Leoni et al. \cite{Vinci2023Inf.Activ.Prob} demonstrate
that an explicit manual restriction before simulation can be defensible when a
source model permits a minimal trace absent from the log. For CTB, such a
restricted net should therefore be evaluated as a separate robustness model,
not substituted silently for the discovered model. The rare realised frequency
does not by itself make the formal defect harmless.

The same separation applies to time. Good control-flow coverage does not
account for the 8.170-minute mean turnaround deficit or the 17.310-minute P90
deficit. The simulator can put activities in the correct order and still omit
time between them. In the CTB setting, this missing time may contain movement,
traffic, resource interaction, queueing or operational states that are not
represented as events. The temporal bias is therefore not a contradiction of
the conformance result; it identifies a different abstraction boundary. This
is why evaluating an event-log-derived simulator only through replay fitness
or variant frequencies would have produced an overly positive conclusion.

The transition-baseline diagnostic makes the scale of that abstraction
boundary concrete. Route-specific p5 values lie between 2.16 and 5.28 minutes,
and the frequency-weighted inbound and outbound lower-tail components sum to
7.62 minutes per visit. Their proximity to the 8.170-minute mean deficit is
consistent with omitted physical movement being a major contributor. It does
not prove that all residual time is travel, because the baseline also contains
unseparated traffic and operational delay and because a percentile summary
cannot be subtracted case by case. The fair conclusion is therefore stronger
than merely saying that ``some time is missing,'' but weaker than claiming a
validated travel-time mechanism.

\paragraph{Meaning for data-aware Process Mining}
Recent data-aware methods extend conventional discovery by conditioning
routing, duration or resource behaviour on case attributes and process state
\cite{mannhardt2023stochasticProcessesModelling,Vinci2023Inf.Activ.Prob,Vinci2026prosit}.
The CTB results show both the value and the limit of that extension. Its value
is selective rather than universal. Calendar context contains a strong and
stable signal for arrivals: the time-aware benchmark reduces arrival-count
MAE by 67.5\,\% and RMSE by 51.2\,\%, and the day arrival rate is about 8.7
times the night rate. In this part of the process, conditioning on information
available at case creation captures a real predictive regularity.

The duration results point in the other direction. For the two dominant RMG
activities, the diagnostic decision trees have slightly negative held-out
$R^2$. The strict ablation makes the source of the endpoint result clearer.
Process- and case-state rules improve turnaround EMD and activity incidence,
but adding workload information afterward leaves weighted service-time EMD
unchanged within a wide paired interval and worsens turnaround and routing
measures. These observations are consistent with limited stable duration
signal: conditional branches may be interpretable without adding useful
later-period predictive information. They are not, however, a formal causal
chain or proof that workload never matters. They show that the available CTB
workload representation does not transfer as useful state in this experiment.

This qualification is central to the interpretation of data awareness.
Adding attributes increases representational capacity, but useful capacity
depends on information timing, measurement quality and a relationship that is
stable across the training and application regimes. Case-level target demand
or utilisation may be correlated with an observed duration without measuring
the physical mechanism that caused it. Conversely, a weak marginal
relationship can hide omitted event-level states such as the actual queue,
truck position or equipment movement. The white-box character of ProSiT is
valuable here because the learned conditions can be inspected and challenged;
interpretability makes the association auditable, but does not convert it into
causality or guarantee predictive validity
\cite{Vinci2026prosit,Sargent2013Validation}.

This result sharpens rather than contradicts the state-abstraction findings of
Vinci et al. \cite{Vinci2023Inf.Activ.Prob}. Their best-performing abstraction
varies between the two evaluated processes, and an unsuitable history state can
create problematic simulated behaviour. The CTB ablation supplies the missing
application-specific test: case and process state help selected routing
measures, whereas the available workload state does not improve the dominant
timing error. It also goes beyond data-aware routing alone. The framework of
López-Pintado et al. \cite{lopez2024} demonstrates the value of case data for
control-flow decisions but identifies data-dependent activity duration as a
separate extension. CTB shows why that separation matters in physical
logistics: state-dependent firing probabilities cannot by themselves create
travel, queues, or spatially constrained service.

The resource result is equally consistent with the differentiated-resource
evidence of López-Pintado and Dumas \cite{BPSdifferentiatedResources2022}.
Treating resources as statistically interchangeable can preserve event counts
while distorting timing. In CTB, capping nominal RMG concurrency does not make
target assignment spatially sticky and does not model crane or truck movement.
By contrast, physical container-terminal simulation represents travel,
equipment interaction and allocation policies explicitly
\cite{Kastner2021}. The current hybrid research opportunity is therefore to
retain the discovered white-box process and state rules while supplying a
mechanistic movement, queue, and resource-assignment layer.

\paragraph{Correlation, scenarios, and operational meaning}
The results therefore distinguish predictive association from intervention
response. The temporal arrival features show a strong association with when
trucks enter the terminal, but the 20\,\% arrival intervention produces no
resolved mean-turnaround increase. This combination does not imply that real
CTB turnaround is independent of demand. It shows that the fitted simulator
contains an arrival relationship, but the chosen change remains below mean
block saturation. The extended ladder then shows that the same model develops
a strong pre-service and turnaround response when realised arrivals reach the
independently estimated capacity region. In the same way, eliminating T22
assignments verifies that the resource rule was
changed, while the near-zero turnaround effect reflects reallocation within an
abstract interchangeable resource pool. It is not evidence that closing a
physical block would have no operational effect.

Taken together, the scenario results act as stress tests of the model's
semantics. A null effect can be a substantive operational finding only when
the mechanisms through which the intervention should propagate are represented
and validated. Here, the saturation experiment demonstrates a capacity-driven
response inside the abstract resource model, but it does not validate the
physical route through which that response would arise. Location feasibility,
movement, differentiated crane availability and spatial queue state remain
absent. The contribution of the
data-aware white-box model is consequently twofold: it supports reproducible
and inspectable changes, and it makes the boundary between an implemented
association and a credible physical mechanism visible. That boundary is the
main result for decision support. The model is suitable for analysing
conditional consequences inside its documented abstraction, but not yet for
claiming causal terminal effects from the scenarios or stress ladder.

The target application nevertheless remains concrete. Before publishing truck
appointments, a terminal could combine a utilisation forecast with candidate
hourly slot limits, simulate each policy, and compare turnaround together with
receive- and delivery-specific service and pre-service KPIs. If forecast
conditions threaten retrieval performance, a candidate policy could reduce
the admitted truck rate or prioritise retrieval-compatible resources; the
simulator would quantify both the intended benefit and any transferred delay
to delivery operations. This prospective comparison is the benefit that a
validated logistics simulation should provide. The CTB experiments establish
the transparent experimental interface and show exactly which additional
queue, location, and equipment states must be represented before that interface
can be used prescriptively.

The boundary is systematic. Traditional Process Mining supports the first two
layers particularly well: it reveals ordering semantics, generalisation, and
excess language. Data-aware simulation adds auditable conditional routing and
timing rules and supports the third layer where suitable state is available.
The later layers require mechanisms that are not implied by correlation alone.
Recent work is moving in the same direction: evolving-process discovery
addresses drift \cite{vinci2025onlinediscoverysimulationmodels}, and temporal
deviation analysis seeks to attribute simulation mismatch to arrival,
processing, and resource inputs rather than reporting magnitude alone
\cite{Wang2026TemporalDeviations}. The CTB study contributes a physical-
logistics instance in which those layers fail at different points.

This framing also clarifies the role of expert intervention. The hospital
application of Vinci et al. combines automatic discovery with manual structural
changes and expert plausibility assessment before optimisation
\cite{Hospital2026Vinci}. The CTB structural repair experiment follows the same
principle of explicit, separately evaluated domain knowledge. A comparable
expert validation of queue, travel and resource semantics remains necessary
before slot-rate or handling-priority recommendations are presented as terminal
counterfactuals.

\section{Methodological Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\paragraph{Pre-Service Delay}

ProSiT enables an activity when the process model allows it. Consecutive CTB
activities can occur at different locations, but truck movement is not a
separate activity in the event-log design. The time from completion of one
activity to the start of the next can therefore combine
\begin{equation}
    D_{\mathrm{pre\text{-}service}} \approx
    T_{\mathrm{physical}} +
    T_{\mathrm{traffic}} +
    T_{\mathrm{queue}} +
    T_{\mathrm{other}}.
\end{equation}
The log cannot identify these parts separately. The percentile check shows
that a reconstructed baseline is sensitive to its assumption. It does not
measure any one part. This would require position, trajectory, queue-entry, or
similar state data. These data are not available. The results use
the term \textit{pre-service delay}, not queueing time.

\paragraph{Resource Assignment Is Not Spatial Container Relocation}

An RMG resource in ProSiT is an abstract capacity assignment. The discovered
overlap capacity is 100. The stated physical cap is 66 across 22 blocks. This
mismatch shows that timestamp overlap is not a crane count. The scenario reference model
uses the cap of three per block.

The cap does not add spatial state. Target-area attributes are predictors, not
hard inventory constraints. The simulator does not bind a container to a
storage block. It also does not create relocation work after reassignment.
Closing T22 spreads its statistical demand across the remaining blocks. It
does not model feasibility, crane movement, or added truck travel. Scenario~A
tests the resource-pool abstraction. It does not forecast a physical T22
closure.

\paragraph{Scope of Expert Validation}

Operational experts from the terminal confirmed two constraints used as hard
eligibility conditions: one truck does not execute activities in parallel, and
no block can use more than three RMGs concurrently. This confirmation supports
the operator check and the cap-three correction. It does not validate the
discovered HO2 or LL capacities, the fully open resource calendars, arrival and
duration distributions, block-interchangeability assumption, pre-service-delay
semantics, or any scenario response. These elements therefore remain subject
to the empirical and structural limitations reported above.

\paragraph{Scenario Control Flow Generalises Only Within a Fixed Activity Set}

The Inductive-Miner loop can generate sequential combinations and repetitions
that were not present as complete training variants. This is less restrictive
than the earlier trie representation, but it is not an open-ended terminal
model. A scenario cannot create a new activity type, an unrecorded exception
mechanism, or a structurally different response to disruption. The two policy
experiments and the demand ladder therefore test parameter changes inside the
discovered activity language. Missing structural mechanisms may still remove
real sources of delay.

\paragraph{Systematic Turnaround Under-Prediction}

The data-aware reference model under-predicts mean turnaround by 8.170 minutes and P90 by
17.310 minutes. The narrow seed intervals show that this is model bias, not
simulation noise. The log cannot show how much missing time comes from
movement, traffic, queueing, or an unrecorded state. Correct case order does
not remove this limit on KPI forecasts.

\paragraph{Scenario Intervals Cover Only Part of Structural Uncertainty}

The matched-seed intervals in Tables~\ref{tab:scenario_kpis},
\ref{tab:demand_scenario_kpis}, and
\ref{tab:demand_saturation_ladder} cover random
variation in one fixed model. They do not cover a new training period, another
discovery run, or missing spatial state. The cap sets a ceiling but adds no
location feasibility or equipment movement. More seeds would narrow these
intervals. They would not reduce structural uncertainty. The matched
state-configuration ablation addresses one part of that uncertainty: it shows
that the unresolved mean-turnaround response persists with workload-blind
rules. It still does not cover a new training period, another discovered net,
alternative resource semantics, or the missing spatial and queueing state.

\paragraph{External Validity Is Limited to One Terminal and Time Window}

The temporal hold-out is one later period from the same terminal and source
systems. Near-perfect replay fitness shows structural stability in this
period, but the observed 2.450-minute mean-turnaround shift shows that temporal
stationarity does not hold even within the study window. The results do not
cover another terminal, season, gate regime, or infrastructure setup. That
would require another full discovery and validation run; an adaptive or
rolling-window simulator would additionally require prospective evaluation.
