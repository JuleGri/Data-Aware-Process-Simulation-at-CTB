This chapter describes the data of the case and the event log built from them:
which records are used, what a case and an event are, which case attributes the
simulation may use and why, what the log shows about truck turnaround, and which
domain rules every truck visit must satisfy. The log is the input of all
experiments (Chapter~\ref{chp:experiments}).

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Case and Data Sources}
\label{sec:cs_environment}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

HHLA Container Terminal Burchardkai (CTB) in the Port of Hamburg handles about
2.6 million TEU per year on 315 hectares \cite{hhla2025}. External trucks deliver
or receive containers. A truck passes the entry gate (\emph{Gate In}), drives to
one or more handling areas and leaves through the exit gate (\emph{Gate Out}).
There are three kinds of handling areas: 22 storage blocks served by automated
rail-mounted gantry cranes (RMG, blocks T06--T27), the empty-container depot (LL,
from the German \emph{Leerlager}) and the manually operated area HO2. An RMG block
has three handover lanes for trucks, and its cranes also serve the waterside and
the rail. The physical constraints that follow from this are introduced in
Section~\ref{sec:bg_terminal}.

\label{sec:cs_data_sources}
Four operational systems record parts of a truck visit
(Table~\ref{tab:ctb_data_sources}). They use different identifiers and are linked
through the truck schedule. The extract covers March and April 2026, about
104,000 truck visits and 113,466 container moves.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Event Log}
\label{sec:event_log_engineering}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\paragraph{Case.}
\label{sec:visit_reconstruction}
A case is one completed truck visit from Gate In to Gate Out, identified by its
schedule record. Container transactions are attached to the visit through the
schedule, and container properties through the container number.

\paragraph{Activities and resources.}
\label{sec:activities_order}
\label{sec:resource_identities}
The log has eleven activities: Gate In, Gate Out and the nine combinations of
three handling areas (RMG, LL, HO2) with three kinds of stop (\emph{delivery}, the
truck brings a container; \emph{receive}, it collects one; \emph{mixed}, it does
both at the same area). The block of an RMG stop is not part of the activity
label but its resource (T06--T27); LL and HO2 are one resource each. A generic
label keeps the control flow independent of the block, and the block allocation,
which the scenario of this thesis changes, stays a separate decision.

\paragraph{Event order and timestamps.}
\label{sec:timestamp_semantics}
Every stop has a start and a complete timestamp: it runs from the moment the truck
reports ready in the handover lane to the fulfilment of the order
(Figure~\ref{fig:timestamps}). A stop therefore contains the waiting for the crane
and is time at the block, not pure service time. Driving, and any queueing before
the truck reports ready, fall into the approach. What happens between these
points, when a lane becomes free or when the crane starts, is not recorded.

\end{figure} Gate events are instantaneous, so the turnaround time (TTT) splits
exactly into the approach to the first stop, the stops, the transfer between stops
and the exit after the last stop. All timestamps have minute resolution. The order
of the events within a visit is taken from the schedule (attribute
\texttt{case:event:order}), not from the timestamps: the stops of a visit never
overlap, but at the empty-container depot a stop can end a few minutes after Gate
Out (6 training and 5 test visits). Methods that read the time intervals would
treat this as concurrency; Section~\ref{sec:exp_discovery_input} shows the effect
on discovery.

\paragraph{Population.}
\label{sec:cleaning_population}
Visits with missing timestamps, visits longer than twelve hours, which indicate
recording errors, and 184 visits with an activity outside the eleven labels are
removed. Visits that handle containers at several RMG blocks are kept: they are a
regular part of the process (1.7\,\% of the training visits) and the visits that
a block allocation affects most. The final log has 91,153 visits.

\paragraph{Split.}
The log is split once in time, at the weekly closure on Monday 13 April 2026.
The training log (2 March to 11 April, 59,139 visits) is used to discover and fit
every model and to make every selection; the test log (13 to 30 April, 32,014
visits) is used to evaluate them. No visit spans the boundary, so the test period
starts with an empty terminal. Section~\ref{sec:exp_partitions} describes how the
experiments use the two logs.

\section{Case Attributes: the Planned Manifest}
\label{sec:context_enrichment}
\label{sec:entry_known_attributes}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

A data-aware simulation learns its decisions and durations from case attributes.
This thesis gives the simulation the attributes of the planned visit manifest
and nothing else (Table~\ref{tab:manifest_attributes}). They are aggregated per
visit from the schedule and the container master data.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{L{3.3cm}Y}
\toprule
\textbf{Attribute} & \textbf{Definition} \\
\midrule
\texttt{process\_flow\_type} & delivery, receive or mixed visit \\
\texttt{n\_stops} & planned number of yard stops \\
\texttt{n\_deliveries}, \texttt{n\_receives} & planned numbers of delivered and received containers \\
\texttt{n\_containers} & number of different containers of the visit \\
\texttt{has\_hazardous}, \texttt{has\_reefer} & at least one hazardous or refrigerated container \\
\texttt{full\_ratio} & share of full containers \\
\texttt{visit\_complexity} & additive score of the planned work: stops and containers, one point for each full and each received container, two for each reefer or hazardous container, and extra points for mixed visits and mixed stops \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\caption{Case attributes of the simulation: the planned visit manifest, known at Gate In.}
\label{tab:manifest_attributes}
\end{table}

Three reasons decide this choice.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Known when the decision is taken.} The manifest is fixed before the
truck enters the terminal, and the yard position of a container is assigned when
the truck arrives (Section~\ref{sec:prob_statement}). An allocation rule, and a
simulation that evaluates it, can only use what is known at that moment. The
attributes are taken from the planned schedule, not from the recorded execution.
The block of a planned RMG stop is read from the planned stop of the order, which
is also known at entry.
\item \textbf{They describe the work of the visit.} The number, kind and
properties of the containers determine which areas a truck visits and how long
its handling takes. These are the dependencies the routing and duration models of
the simulator are meant to learn.
\item \textbf{The state of the terminal is computed, not attached.} Values that
describe the terminal at Gate In, such as the recent gate demand or the
occupancy of a block, are case attributes only in the recorded log. In a
simulation, ProSiT draws the attributes of a generated case as the attribute tuple
of a random training visit, independent of the simulated time
(Section~\ref{sec:prosit_components}). A recorded demand or occupancy would then
describe another day than the simulated one, and it would not change when a
scenario changes the load. The load that a truck meets is instead computed by
the simulator from its own state: the trucks at a block and their workload
(Section~\ref{sec:prosit_execution}). This is the quantity a block allocation
changes.
\end{enumerate}

The yard snapshots of Table~\ref{tab:ctb_data_sources} are therefore used only to
describe the terminal. Two attributes of the manifest also bound the
control flow: the planned number of stops is enforced as a domain rule
(rule~6 in Section~\ref{sec:domain_rules}), and the flow type separates the
first stop of a visit (Section~\ref{sec:sim_components}).

\section{Observed Truck Turnaround}
\label{sec:observed_behaviour}
\label{sec:obs_structure}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Table~\ref{tab:obs_descriptives} summarises the two logs. Nine out of ten visits
have one yard stop, and visits with three or more stops are rare. About a quarter
of the visits use no RMG block. The TTT depends on the type of visit: receive
visits take longer than delivery visits, mixed visits longest, and a visit with
two RMG blocks about 17 minutes longer than a visit with one. The stops account
for about one third of the mean TTT; the approach and the exit together for about
two thirds. Because a stop starts only when the truck is ready in the handover
lane, driving and queueing before that point fall into the approach.

\input{tables/obs_descriptives}

\label{sec:obs_summary}
The TTT grows with the number of trucks in the terminal, and the growth is not
linear. In the training log, the median TTT is 24 minutes with fewer than 50
trucks in the terminal at Gate In, 28 and 34 minutes with 50 to 99 and 100 to
149 trucks, and 56 minutes with 150 or more. Trucks arrive on weekdays and, in
smaller numbers, on Saturdays; on weekdays between 7 and 16~h, 126 to 152 trucks
arrive per hour in training. Hours with a median TTT of at least 37 minutes, the 90th percentile of
the training hours, are called \emph{delay peaks}. The test period is more
congested than the training period: 95 instead of 77 trucks in the terminal on
average, and 25.7 instead of 10.4\,\% delay-peak hours. The sequences of stops,
in contrast, hardly change: 16 test sequences do not occur in training, and they
account for 0.05\,\% of the test visits. A model fitted on the training log is
therefore expected to underestimate the TTT of the test period, independently of
its control flow. Further figures on arrivals, load and delay peaks are in
Appendix~\ref{app:observed_behaviour}.

\paragraph{What a simulation has to reproduce.}
\label{sec:obs_implications}
For the purpose of this thesis, a simulation has to reproduce:
\begin{enumerate}
\item the stop structure and the TTT by type of visit;
\item the arrival profile over the week and the day;
\item the growth of the TTT with the number of trucks in the terminal, and of the
approach and the stops with the load of a block, because a block allocation acts
on this load;
\item the share of visits above the turnaround corridor and the delay peaks
(Section~\ref{sec:exp_kpis}).
\end{enumerate}
These requirements define the measures of Chapter~\ref{chp:experiments}.

\section{Domain Rules}
\label{sec:domain_rules}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

The physical constraints of a truck visit are written down as seven rules
(Table~\ref{tab:domain_rules}). They come from the author's knowledge of the
terminal operations

and are checked against the recorded log: every training and test visit satisfies
rules 1 to 5 (Section~\ref{sec:res_rules}); rule~6 holds by construction, because
the planned and the recorded stops come from the same schedule. Their review by the terminal is
reported in Section~\ref{sec:disc_stakeholders}. Rule 7 cannot be checked,
because the truck type and its capacity are not recorded; it is not used.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{L{0.5cm}YL{2.6cm}L{3.2cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{\#} & \textbf{Rule} & \textbf{Type} & \textbf{Needs} \\
\midrule
1 & Gate In occurs exactly once and is the first event. & sequence & activity labels \\
2 & Gate Out occurs exactly once and is the last event. & sequence & activity labels \\
3 & A visit has at least one yard stop between Gate In and Gate Out. & sequence & activity labels \\
4 & The stops of a visit never overlap in time. & time & start and complete timestamps \\
5 & A mixed stop handles all moves of the visit at its unit. The visit has no separate delivery or receive at the same unit. & sequence (LL, HO2); data (RMG) & activity labels; block of each RMG stop \\
6 & The number of stops equals the number of planned stops in the manifest (at most four in the data). & data & planned stops (case attribute) \\
7 & The load of the truck stays within its capacity after every stop. & data & truck type (not recorded) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}

\label{tab:domain_rules}

For LL and HO2, the area is the handling unit, so rule~5 can be read from the
activity labels. For RMG, the unit is the block, so the RMG part of rule~5 needs
the resource of each stop.

\section{Chapter Summary}
\label{sec:cs_summary}

The event log contains 91,153 completed truck visits with eleven activities, the
block of every RMG stop as resource, and start and complete timestamps for every
stop. The simulation uses only the planned visit manifest as case attributes,
because it is known when a truck arrives and because the load of the terminal has
to come from the simulated state, not from a recorded value. The log shows a TTT
that grows non-linearly with the load and a test period that is more congested
than the training period. Seven domain rules state what a truck visit can
physically do; Chapter~\ref{chp:experiments} uses them to select and check the
control-flow model.
