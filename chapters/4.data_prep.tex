This chapter describes the data of the case and their preparation, which results
in an event log that is ready for both the discovery of the process model and the
discovery of the simulation parameters. It introduces the terminal and its
operational systems, defines the cases, activities, resources and timestamps of
the event log, and explains how the raw records are cleaned and split. It then
describes the case attributes that the simulation may use, what the log shows
about the truck turnaround, and the domain rules that every truck visit must
satisfy.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Case and Data Sources}
\label{sec:cs_environment}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

HHLA Container Terminal Burchardkai (CTB) in the Port of Hamburg handles about
2.6 million TEU per year on 315 hectares \cite{hhla2025}. External trucks deliver
or receive containers. A truck passes the entry gate (\emph{Gate In}), drives to
one or more handling areas and leaves through the exit gate (\emph{Gate Out}).
There are three kinds of handling areas. The first are 22 storage blocks served
by automated rail-mounted gantry cranes (RMG, blocks T06--T27). The second is the
empty-container depot (LL, from the German \emph{Leerlager}), and the third is the
manually operated area HO2. An RMG block has three handover lanes for trucks, and
its cranes also serve the waterside and the rail. The physical constraints that
follow from this are introduced in Section~\ref{sec:bg_terminal}.

\label{sec:cs_data_sources}
Four operational systems record different stages of a truck visit
(Table~\ref{tab:ctb_data_sources} in Appendix~\ref{app:observed_behaviour}).
They use different identifiers and are linked through the truck schedule, which
holds the timestamps and resources of every handover operation and the total time
of the visit. The extract covers March and April 2026, with 104,807 schedule
records of 94,705 truck visits and 113,466 container moves.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Event Log}
\label{sec:event_log_engineering}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\paragraph{Case}
\label{sec:visit_reconstruction}
A case is one completed truck visit from Gate In to Gate Out, identified by its
schedule record. Container transactions are attached to the visit through the
schedule, and container properties through the container number.

\paragraph{Activities and resources}
\label{sec:activities_order}
\label{sec:resource_identities}
The log has eleven activities, namely Gate In, Gate Out and the nine combinations
of the three handling areas (RMG, LL, HO2) with three kinds of stop. In a
\emph{delivery}, the truck brings a container. In a \emph{receive}, it collects
one. In a \emph{mixed} stop, it does both at the same handover point and with the
same resource. Each event names the resource that performed it. The gates are one
resource each. LL and HO2 are recorded as one resource pool each, because no data
on the schedules of their manual equipment are available, due to restrictions set
by the works council. The automated storage blocks are represented as individual
resources of the RMG area, T06 to T27. The block of an RMG stop is therefore not
part of the activity label but its resource. This generic label keeps the control
flow independent of the block. The simulator can then learn the choice of the
block as a separate resource decision, and a block allocation policy, which the
scenario of this thesis changes, stays a separate decision as well.

Every event of the log has seven fields, namely the case, the activity, the
resource, the start and the complete timestamp, the position of the event in the
visit and the case attributes of Section~\ref{sec:context_enrichment}.
Table~\ref{tab:eventlog_excerpt} shows one recorded visit. The truck delivers a
container to block T12 and then receives one from block T07, so the visit is a
mixed visit with two planned stops. Its turnaround time is 35 minutes.

\paragraph{Event order and timestamps}
\label{sec:timestamp_semantics}
Every stop has a start and a complete timestamp. It runs from the moment the truck
reports ready in the handover lane to the fulfilment of the order
(Figure~\ref{fig:timestamps}). A stop therefore contains the waiting for the crane
and is time at the block, not pure service time. The time before the truck reports
ready is not part of the stop. This is the drive to the resource and any waiting
for a free lane. Before the first stop, it forms the \emph{approach}, and between
two stops the \emph{transfer}. The same holds for LL and HO2. Their handover areas
have many more parking spaces than the three lanes of a block, and trucks wait
there for the manual equipment within the recorded stop. The log shows when a
stop ends, and thus when the lane of that truck becomes free again. It does not
show which of the three lanes a truck uses or when the crane starts to handle it.

The model places a free lane at the start of the recorded stop. The data cannot
show whether a truck sometimes reports ready before a lane is free. Gate events
are instantaneous, so the turnaround time (TTT) splits exactly into the approach
to the first stop, the stops, the transfers between stops and the time to the
exit after the last stop. All timestamps are provided in minute resolution. The
order of the events within a visit is taken from the schedule (attribute
\texttt{case:event:order}), not from the timestamps. At the empty-container depot,
a stop can end a few minutes after Gate Out (6 training and 5 test visits).
Methods that read the time intervals would treat this as concurrency.
Section~\ref{sec:exp_discovery_input} shows the effect on discovery.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Preprocessing and Cleaning}
\label{sec:cleaning_population}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Real operational data from a mix of automated and manually maintained systems
contain errors, which have to be filtered out before the data can describe the
process. The cleaning removes visits with errors in their records that result
from human input errors or system failures. The cleaning rules were set up
together with the terminal during the inspection of the raw data
(Table~\ref{tab:stakeholder_phases} in Section~\ref{sec:exp_review}).

\paragraph{Cleaning as a modelling decision} For data from a physical process, the
cleaning is more than a technical step. Every removed record is classified as a
recording error rather than as behaviour that the terminal can show. Some of these
decisions follow directly from the physics. A truck cannot perform two stops at
the same time, so overlapping stop records cannot describe a real visit. Others
draw a line within plausible behaviour. A visit of more than twelve hours may be a
recording error, but it may also be a truck that really stayed, for example during
a disruption. The cleaning thus decides which behaviour enters the study as
physical behaviour of the terminal. Together with the coverage threshold of the
process discovery (Section~\ref{sec:exp_discovery_eval}), which decides which of
the remaining rare sequences a net must accept, it bounds what the discovered net
can represent and what the simulation learned from it can generate. Behaviour that
is removed here can never be reproduced later, and behaviour that is kept, such as
the visits to several blocks, has to be represented by the net.

\paragraph{Cleaning steps}
Table~\ref{tab:cleaning} lists how the 94,705 visits of the extract become the
91,153 visits of the event log. A visit is counted under the first reason that
applies to it. The first group of steps removes visits whose records cannot
describe a real visit, namely visits with a missing timestamp, overlapping stop
records or a negative duration, and visits that stay longer than twelve hours or
wait more than ten hours before Gate Out. After this cleaning, the stops of a
visit never overlap. The second step removes visits with a stop whose containers
cannot be identified. Such a stop cannot be classified as a delivery, receive or
mixed stop, and the attributes of the visit cannot be computed. Visits that handle
containers at several RMG blocks are kept. They are a regular part of the process
(1.7\,\% of the training visits), and they are the visits that a block allocation
affects most.

\paragraph{Split}
The log is split once in time into a training log with 65\,\% of the visits
(59,139 visits, 2 March to 11 April) and a test log with 35\,\% (32,014 visits, 13
to 30 April). The boundary is placed on a Sunday, as heavy trucks are not allowed
to drive on Sundays in Germany by law \cite{StVO30}. No visit therefore spans the
boundary, and the test period starts with no trucks in the terminal.
Section~\ref{sec:exp_partitions} describes how the experiments use the two logs.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Case Attributes of the Planned Visit}
\label{sec:context_enrichment}
\label{sec:entry_known_attributes}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

A data-aware simulation learns its decisions and durations from the case
attributes provided in the event log. This thesis gives the simulation the
attributes of the planned visit and nothing else
(Table~\ref{tab:manifest_attributes}). ProSiT needs attributes that describe the
case as a whole (Section~\ref{sec:prosit_components}). They are therefore
aggregated per visit, combining features of the visit from the schedule with the
records of the handled containers.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{L{3.3cm}Y}
\toprule
\textbf{Attribute} & \textbf{Definition} \\
\midrule
\texttt{process\_flow\_type} & delivery, receive or mixed visit \\
\texttt{n\_stops} & planned number of yard stops \\
\texttt{n\_deliveries}, \texttt{n\_receives} & planned numbers of delivered and received containers \\
\texttt{n\_containers} & number of different containers of the visit \\
\texttt{has\_hazardous}, \texttt{has\_reefer} & at least one hazardous or refrigerated container \\
\texttt{full\_ratio} & share of full containers \\
\texttt{visit\_complexity} & additive score that rates the effort of the visit for the terminal. It counts the stops and containers, one point for each full and each received container, two for each reefer or hazardous container, and extra points for mixed visits and mixed stops \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\caption{Case attributes of the simulation.}
\label{tab:manifest_attributes}
\end{table}

Several findings from the design of the event log motivate this choice.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Known when the decision is taken.} The properties of the planned
visit are fixed before the truck enters the terminal, because the haulier sends
them to the terminal in a pre-advice. The yard position of a container that is
delivered to the terminal is assigned when the truck arrives at the gate
(Section~\ref{sec:prob_statement}). An allocation rule, and a simulation that
evaluates it, can only use what is known at that moment. The attributes are
therefore taken from the pre-advised plan of the visit, not from what was recorded
during the visit. The number of planned stops, for example, comes from the
pre-advice, not from the number of stops that the truck actually made. The block
of an RMG stop is also known at Gate In. For a receive, it is the block in which
the container is stored. For a delivery, the yard planning assigns it when the
truck passes the gate.
\item \textbf{They describe the effort behind each visit.} The number, kind and
properties of the containers determine which areas a truck visits and how long
its handling takes. These are the dependencies that the routing and duration
models of the simulator are meant to learn.
\item \textbf{The state of the terminal is computed, not attached.} Values that
describe the terminal at Gate In, such as the recent gate demand or the
occupancy of a block, are case attributes only in the recorded log. In a
simulation, ProSiT draws the attributes of a generated case as the attribute set
of a random training visit, independent of the simulated time
(Section~\ref{sec:prosit_hyperparameters}). A recorded demand or occupancy would
then describe another day than the simulated one, and it would not change when a
scenario changes the load. The load that a truck meets is instead computed by
the simulator from its own state, namely the trucks at a block and their workload
(Section~\ref{sec:prosit_execution}). This is the quantity that a block allocation
changes.
\end{enumerate}

The yard snapshots of Table~\ref{tab:ctb_data_sources} are therefore not used as
case attributes. They serve to describe the occupancy of the blocks and to mark
the blocks that were out of use (Section~\ref{sec:obs_drift}). Two of the
attributes are also used to keep the simulated visits physically possible. The
planned number of stops limits how many stops a simulated truck performs
(Section~\ref{sec:sim_adaptations}), and the flow type decides in the routing
trees which kind of stop a visit starts with (Section~\ref{sec:sim_components}).

\section{Observed Truck Turnaround}
\label{sec:observed_behaviour}
\label{sec:obs_structure}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Table~\ref{tab:obs_descriptives} in Appendix~\ref{app:observed_behaviour}
summarises the two logs. Nine out of ten visits have one yard stop, and visits
with three or more stops are rare. About a quarter of the visits use no RMG block.
The TTT depends on the type of visit. Receive visits take longer than delivery
visits, mixed visits longest, and a visit with two RMG blocks about 17 minutes
longer than a visit with one. The stops account for about one third of the mean
TTT, while the approach and the exit together account for about two thirds. By
design, a stop starts only when the truck is ready in the handover lane, so the
driving and queueing before that point fall into the approach.

Figure~\ref{fig:obs_ttt_corridor} shows how the TTT of the single visits is spread
around the turnaround corridor of 25 to 35 minutes that the terminal aims at
(Section~\ref{sec:prob_statement}). Most visits take between 15 and 35 minutes,
and a long tail reaches beyond two hours. In the training log, 25\,\% of the
visits lie in the corridor, 39\,\% below it and 36\,\% above it. In the test log,
the share above the corridor rises to 47\,\%. The terminal judges the corridor by
the mean TTT of each hour, not by single visits (Section~\ref{sec:exp_kpis}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/observed/ttt_corridor.pdf}
\caption{Turnaround time per visit and the corridor.}
\label{fig:obs_ttt_corridor}
\end{figure}

\label{sec:obs_summary}
The TTT grows with the number of trucks in the terminal, and the growth is not
linear. In the training log, the median TTT is 24 minutes with fewer than 50
trucks in the terminal at Gate In, 28 and 34 minutes with 50 to 99 and 100 to
149 trucks, and 56 minutes with 150 or more. Trucks arrive on weekdays and, in
smaller numbers, on Saturdays. On weekdays between 7 and 16~h, 126 to 152 trucks
arrive per hour in training. Hours with a median TTT of at least 37 minutes, the
90th percentile of the training hours, are identified as \emph{delay peaks} in the
design of the experiments (Section~\ref{sec:exp_kpis}). The test period is more
congested than the training period, with 95 instead of 77 trucks in the terminal
on average and 25.7 instead of 10.4\,\% delay-peak hours. The sequences of stops,
in contrast, hardly change. Only 16 test sequences do not occur in training, and
they account for 0.05\,\% of the test visits. A model fitted on the training log is
therefore expected to underestimate the TTT of the test period, independently of
its control flow. Further figures on arrivals, load and delay peaks are in
Appendix~\ref{app:observed_behaviour}.

\paragraph{What a simulation has to reproduce}
\label{sec:obs_implications}
For the purpose of this thesis, a simulation has to reproduce four properties of
the observed visits.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The stop structure, that is, the sequences of stops and how often each
occurs, and the TTT by type of visit.
\item The arrival profile over the week and the day.
\item The growth of the TTT with the number of trucks in the terminal. In
addition, the approach and the stop at a block must grow with the number of
trucks at that block, because a block allocation changes exactly this number.
\item The share of hours above the turnaround corridor and the delay peaks
(Section~\ref{sec:exp_kpis}).
\end{enumerate}
These requirements define the measures for the construction and the quality of
the simulation model, elaborated in Chapter~\ref{chp:experiments}.

\section{Domain Rules}
\label{sec:domain_rules}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

The physical constraints of a truck visit, introduced from a theoretical
viewpoint in Section~\ref{sec:bg_terminal}, are written down as seven rules
(Table~\ref{tab:domain_rules}). They were worked out with the project team of
the terminal, from terminal development and the data warehouse team, as part of
the use case of this study, and are checked against the recorded log. Every
training and test visit satisfies rules 1 to 6 (Section~\ref{sec:res_rules}).
Their review by the terminal is reported in Section~\ref{sec:disc_stakeholders}.
Rule 7 cannot be checked, because the truck type and its capacity are not
recorded. It is not used. The type of a rule decides where it can be enforced
(Section~\ref{sec:exp_rules}).

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{L{0.5cm}YL{2.6cm}L{3.2cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{\#} & \textbf{Rule} & \textbf{Type} & \textbf{Needs} \\
\midrule
1 & Gate In occurs exactly once and is the first event. & sequence & activity labels \\
2 & Gate Out occurs exactly once and is the last event. & sequence & activity labels \\
3 & A visit has at least one yard stop between Gate In and Gate Out. & sequence & activity labels \\
4 & The stops of a visit never overlap in time. & time & start and complete timestamps \\
5 & A mixed stop handles all moves of the visit at its unit. The visit has no separate delivery or receive at the same unit. & sequence for LL and HO2, data for RMG & activity labels and the block of each RMG stop \\
6 & A visit has at most eight stops, because a truck delivers and receives at most four containers each. The data show at most four stops. & sequence (bound) & activity labels \\
7 & The load of the truck stays within its capacity after every stop. & data & truck type (not recorded) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\caption{Domain rules for truck visits.}
\label{tab:domain_rules}
\end{table}

For LL and HO2, the area is the handling unit, so rule~5 can be read from the
activity labels. For RMG, the unit is the block, so the RMG part of rule~5 needs
the resource of each stop.

\section{Chapter Summary}
\label{sec:cs_summary}

The event log contains 91,153 completed truck visits with eleven activities, the
block of every RMG stop as resource, and start and complete timestamps for every
stop. The simulation uses only the attributes of the planned visit as case
attributes, because they are known when a truck arrives and because the load of
the terminal has to come from the simulated state, not from a recorded value. The
log shows a TTT that grows non-linearly with the load and a test period that is
more congested than the training period. Seven domain rules state what a truck
visit can physically do. Chapter~\ref{chp:experiments} uses them to select and
check the control-flow model.
