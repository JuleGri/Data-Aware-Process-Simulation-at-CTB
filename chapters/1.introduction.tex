\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chp:introduction}

Process mining can learn executable simulation models from event logs. Data-aware
simulation goes a step further and learns how decisions and durations depend on
the data of a case and on the state of the process. Such models have been built
and evaluated mainly for business processes, in which a case waits because a
person is busy \cite{Sulis2026SimulationReview,Vinci2026prosit}. This thesis examines how far the approach carries over to a
physical logistics process, using truck handling at HHLA Container Terminal
Burchardkai (CTB) as a case study: whether a simulation learned with ProSiT from
the operational event data of the terminal can support the assessment of a
different allocation of truck deliveries to the yard blocks.

\section{Motivation}

Simulation allows terminal operators to investigate changes before implementing
them in live operations. Conventional terminal simulation models are constructed
and calibrated manually, drawing on operational data and domain knowledge
\cite{CTSteenken2004,OptimizingCTs2023}. Process mining offers a way to derive such
models from event logs, including the control flow, activity durations, arrival
patterns and resource assignments \cite{PMRozinat2009}. Data-aware approaches
additionally learn how decisions and durations depend on case attributes and the
state of the process \cite{mannhardt2023stochasticProcessesModelling,Vinci2023Inf.Activ.Prob}.
ProSiT, the framework used in this thesis, combines a Petri net with learned
probabilistic decision trees, which can be read and changed \cite{Vinci2026prosit}.

A container terminal is a demanding test for such models, because its process has
physical limits. A truck is one vehicle: its stops happen one after another, never
at the same time, and every visit exists to move containers. A storage block has
three handover lanes, so it can serve at most three trucks at once, and its cranes
are shared with the waterside and the rail. These limits are not written in the
event log, and a model learned only from the recorded sequences and durations does
not know them. A discovered control-flow model may allow a truck to perform two
stops at once; a learned simulator may send more trucks to a block than it can
serve, or let the waiting fall when a block gets busier. A simulation that
reproduces the common sequences can therefore still fail where the physical
limits shape the process, and these are exactly the places that a change in the
allocation of trucks to blocks affects. The model has to be checked for the
decision it is meant to support.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:prob_statement}

The primary performance measure in this study is truck turnaround time (TTT),
defined as the time between Gate In and Gate Out. In the test period, the
observed median is 33 minutes, the mean 41 minutes and the 90th percentile
75 minutes. The data and performance measures are described in
Chapters~\ref{chp:case_study_and_data} and~\ref{chp:experiments}.

The terminal aims at a TTT between 25 and 35 minutes for every truck. The TTT of
each truck is the figure shown on the management dashboard of the terminal,
without a breakdown by the number of containers

Both bounds matter. A truck that stays longer than 35 minutes has waited too long
from the customer's point of view. A turnaround below 25 minutes is not wanted
either, because the cranes of a block also serve the waterside and the rail, and
fast truck handling can take capacity from them. In the test period, 47\,\% of the
visits take longer than 35 minutes, and on average a visit spends 12.5 minutes
above the corridor (Section~\ref{sec:exp_kpis}). This thesis asks why these visits
take longer and whether a different allocation of deliveries to the yard blocks
can shorten them. The visits below the corridor are reported, but the waterside
work is not modelled, so the study does not optimise the balance between truck
handling and waterside operations.

The improvement measure studied is a load-aware allocation of deliveries to the
yard blocks: a delivery whose block is full goes to a nearby block with a free
lane. The yard position of a container is assigned when the truck arrives, so that
the driver knows where to go. An applicable rule must therefore use information
available at that point and respect the blocks that may take the container.

This leads to two connected problems. First, the simulation must reproduce the
relevant truck behaviour, the TTT and its response to load, within the physical
limits of the terminal. Second, an improvement found in a simulated scenario must
be interpreted in light of the validation of the model and of the operational
feasibility of the measure.

\section{Research Objective and Contributions}

The objective is to develop and evaluate a simulation of truck handling at CTB that
is learned from event data, and to determine what it can support about the effect
of a load-aware block allocation on the turnaround time. CTB is the case under
study, and the allocation question is the intended use against which the model is
assessed. The limitations of the simulation are results of this assessment and
determine the scope of the conclusions for the terminal.

The thesis makes two connected contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(C1)] \textbf{A justified simulation model for the CTB case.} The study
compares discovered process models with conformance metrics, checks them against
the domain rules of the truck visit, and documents the selected model, its learned
decision trees and parameters, and the extensions that the physical limits require
(Chapters~\ref{chp:experiments}--\ref{chp:results}).
\item[(C2)] \textbf{An empirical assessment of validity and decision-support
potential.} The study compares observed and simulated performance, locates the
causes of the discrepancies component by component, and evaluates the improvement
measure within the resulting limits. It distinguishes findings about the simulated
system from conclusions justified for CTB (Chapters~\ref{chp:results}
and~\ref{chp:discussion}).
\end{enumerate}

The case study builds on existing discovery and simulation methods; it does not
propose a new algorithm. Findings from this terminal can motivate further
investigation in other processes with physical limits, but do not establish general
limitations of all data-aware simulators.

\section{Research Questions}

The objective is addressed through three research questions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{RQ1:} Which discovery and rule treatment yields a sound,
executable control-flow model that preserves the observed truck-visit
behaviour, and do conformance metrics anticipate its behaviour in simulation?
\item \textbf{RQ2:} To what extent does the ProSiT simulation reproduce truck
turnaround time and its response to load at the capacity-limited yard blocks,
and which model components explain the observed discrepancies?
\item \textbf{RQ3:} To what extent can the simulation be used to evaluate a
load-aware block allocation for deliveries under the physical limits of the
yard blocks, and what does it indicate about the effect on truck turnaround
time?
\end{enumerate}

Experiments E1 to E3 address the selection of the control-flow model and its
behaviour in the simulator (RQ1). E3 and E4 assess the performance of the
simulation and investigate its discrepancies (RQ2). E5 evaluates the improvement
measure (RQ3); its interpretation depends on the evidence from the validation.

\section{Thesis Outline}
\label{sec:thesis_outline}

Chapter~\ref{chp:background} introduces the process-mining and simulation
concepts and positions the study in the literature.
Chapter~\ref{chp:case_study_and_data} describes CTB, the operational records and
the event log. Chapter~\ref{chp:experiments} explains the experiments, the data
setting and the measures, and Chapter~\ref{chp:process_discovery_methodology}
describes the simulation model on which they operate.
Chapter~\ref{chp:results} reports the results and answers the research questions.
Chapter~\ref{chp:discussion} assesses what they mean for CTB and where the
conclusions stop, and Chapter~\ref{chp:conclusion} summarises the findings. The
appendices provide additional results, the complete model inventory and the
reproduction details.
