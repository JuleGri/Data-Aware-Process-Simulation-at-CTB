\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chp:introduction}

Container terminals connect maritime and hinterland transport networks. Higher
container volumes and larger vessels require terminal operators to make many
tactical and operational decisions under uncertainty. These decisions concern
truck dispatching, yard-block allocation, resource use, congestion, equipment
failures, and temporary infrastructure outages. Experiments in an operating
terminal are often costly, disruptive, or not feasible. Simulation offers a way
to assess decisions without interrupting terminal operations.

Simulation is used for decision support in container-terminal planning and
operations research. Its operational purpose is prospective: a terminal can
compare how alternative process executions and operating conditions affect
specific handling patterns before changing live operations. For example, when
high yard utilisation is forecast, a terminal may compare lower truck-slot
release rates, alternative resource allocations, or policies that favour
receive or delivery operations, and evaluate their effects on activity-specific
service, pre-service delay, and total truck turnaround. Simulation can likewise
assess infrastructure investments, scheduling approaches, truck appointment
systems, and terminal redesigns before implementation. Traditional approaches mostly use manually
developed Discrete Event Simulation (DES) models, agent-based models, or
digital twins. Building and maintaining these models requires domain knowledge,
manual work, and calibration \cite{YUN1999DESSimulation,CTSteenken2004,OptimizingCTs2023,Bett2024}.

Process Mining research has studied how simulation models can be built
from event logs. Earlier work showed that control-flow behaviour, activity
durations, arrival patterns, resource allocations, and routing probabilities
can be discovered from process data and used in executable simulation models
\cite{PMRozinat2009}. \textit{Data-aware process simulation} extends this idea.
It uses context attributes, case information, process states, resource
characteristics, and decision logic
\cite{DaSLeno2019,mannhardt2023stochasticProcessesModelling,Vinci2023Inf.Activ.Prob,lopez2024}.

Machine-learning and deep-learning methods can learn complicated behavior,
but their results are often difficult to inspect, modify, and validate. Hybrid
and white-box approaches combine interpretable models with data-driven
behaviour discovery \cite{runtimeIntegration2024,Vinci2026prosit}. ProSiT uses
probabilistic white-box predictors to build simulation models that can be
inspected and configured for what-if analysis \cite{Vinci2026prosit}.

Evidence on automatically discovered simulation models in logistics systems is
still limited. A recent review of 105 studies that combine simulation and
process mining found one in logistics and one in transportation; most concern
healthcare, manufacturing and administration \cite{Sulis2026SimulationReview}.
The same review finds that process mining supports arrivals, durations and the
control flow well, but gives little support for queues and resources. Container
terminal truck operations are shaped by exactly these: trucks share blocks with
a few handover lanes, congestion builds up in busy hours, and the space and the
equipment limit what can happen at the same time. It is not yet clear how well a
discovered data-aware simulation model can reproduce these conditions, which of
its assumptions break, and what must be added before it can support decisions.
This thesis studies these questions with event logs from real container terminal
truck operations.



\section{Problem Statement}

\begin{hints}
\item The concrete operational problem at CTB: delay peaks in truck turnaround time. Use the observed numbers from Chapter~\ref{chp:case_study_and_data} (10\,\% of training hours, 26\,\% of validation hours are delay peaks).
\item The KPI: truck turnaround time (median, P90) and share of delay-peak hours. Say where the KPI comes from (HHLA practice, terminal contact).
\item Why a simulator is needed to evaluate a procedural change against this KPI.
\item The scientific problem: a discovered simulator can reproduce the visit sequences without reproducing the KPI and its response to load. Nobody has checked this layer by layer for a physical logistics process.
\item Remove ``TBC''.
\end{hints}
Container terminal operators make decisions about capacity, truck handling,
yard use, policies, and infrastructure changes. Experiments in a real terminal
are often not feasible or involve operational risk, so these decisions are
often studied with simulation \cite{CTSteenken2004,OptimizingCTs2023}. At HHLA
Container Terminal Burchardkai (CTB), the truck turnaround time is the main key
performance indicator of truck handling. It has a long tail and rises in busy
hours: in the test period of this thesis, the median is 33 and the 90th
percentile 75 minutes, and in 26\,\% of the busy Gate-In hours the median
turnaround time reaches the delay-peak threshold of 37 minutes (10\,\% in the
training period). A measure against such delay peaks, for example a different
allocation of deliveries to the yard blocks, cannot be tried out in the running
terminal.

Data-aware process simulation discovers simulation models from historical
event logs. This may reduce modelling work and keep the model closer to the
recorded process \cite{Camargo2020,lopez2024,Vinci2023Inf.Activ.Prob}. Before
such a model can support decisions, it must reproduce observed operations,
remain configurable, produce stable results, and represent the mechanisms
through which a proposed intervention can affect the relevant KPI. A model
intended to recommend a truck-slot rate under forecast congestion must, for
example, propagate changed arrivals through resource contention and must keep
receive and delivery effects distinguishable. Earlier studies have shown
that data-aware simulation can be applied to business processes
\cite{Vinci2023Inf.Activ.Prob,Hospital2026Vinci}. Its use in logistics systems
with congestion, competing resources, and context-dependent behaviour is less
clear. The scientific problem is that a discovered simulator can reproduce the
sequences of the visits and still miss the KPI and its response to load. Queues,
capacity and load enter the learned models through assumptions that were made
for business processes, where a resource is a person and waiting means that the
person is busy. Whether these assumptions hold when the resource is a block with
three handover lanes has not been checked layer by layer. This thesis evaluates
the fidelity, use and limitations of such a model for container-terminal truck
operations.

\section{Research Objective and Contributions}

\begin{hints}
\item Objective in one sentence: investigate whether, and under which conditions, a data-aware process simulation learned from event logs can represent a physically constrained logistics process, using truck handling at CTB as the case. The aim is a scientific statement about the technique, not a recommendation for the terminal.
\item Contributions as a short list, each pointing to a chapter:
  (C1) controlled control-flow selection with rule audit and exact repair, and evidence that coverage and the encoding, not precision, anticipate the simulated behaviour (Ch.~6/7);
  (C2) a mechanism-level validation of a ProSiT model for a capacity-limited logistics process: which assumptions of the technique break under physical limits, why, and what is needed to repair them (Ch.~6/7);
  (C3) a demonstration of what-if use under physical constraints: what the validated simulation can and cannot show about one allocation rule, with its conditions of validity (Ch.~7);
  (C4) a reproducible pipeline with frozen inputs and documented runs.
\item Say explicitly that this is an empirical case study, not a new algorithm. The limits of the technique and the approaches to overcome them are part of the result (Chapter~\ref{chp:discussion}).
\end{hints}

The objective of this thesis is to investigate whether, and under which
conditions, a data-aware process simulation learned from an event log can
represent a logistics process with physical capacity limits. Truck handling at
CTB serves as a critical case: the data are favourable and the physical limits
are known, so the case shows clearly what transfers from business processes,
what breaks and what must be added (Section~\ref{sec:rw_positioning}). The aim is
a statement about the technique and about how it can be checked, not a
recommendation for the terminal. The thesis is an empirical case study; it
proposes no new discovery algorithm and no new simulator. Its contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(C1)] \textbf{Model selection for simulation.} A selection procedure with
gates, a rule audit and a check in the simulator, and the evidence that what a
net permits is not what a simulator generates: precision does not anticipate the
simulated behaviour over a wide range, coverage of the frequent behaviour does,
nets with parallel branches and silent skips produce impossible visits, and a
data-aware rule has to look ahead because the decisions of a discovered net sit
on silent transitions (E1--E3, Chapter~\ref{chp:results}).
\item[(C2)] \textbf{A mechanism-level validation of a discovered simulator.} Six
assumptions of ProSiT~1.0.4 that do not hold under physical capacity limits, each
tested with a control run, and four changes to the queue and the service that
restore the response to load. They concern every process in which cases share a
resource of limited capacity (E4).
\item[(C3)] \textbf{A validation design for discovered simulation models.} The
model is checked layer by layer, with thresholds taken from differences between
real periods, criteria for the response to load and an extreme-condition test,
and a separation of model error from change between the periods
(Chapter~\ref{chp:experiments}).
\item[(C4)] \textbf{A what-if demonstration under physical limits.} One allocation
rule for deliveries, evaluated with paired runs and robustness runs, with the
conditions under which its result holds (E5).
\item[(C5)] \textbf{A reproducible pipeline} with frozen inputs, scripts for every
table and figure, and documented runs.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Thesis Outline}
\label{sec:thesis_outline}

\begin{hints}
\item One or two sentences per chapter, in the final order: 2 Background and Related Work, 3 Data Preparation, 4 Methodology and Experimental Setup, 5 Results, 6 Discussion, 7 Conclusion. Mention the appendices (observed behaviour, ProSiT parameters and trees, reproduction).

Chapter~\ref{chp:background} introduces the concepts that the thesis uses:
truck operations in a container terminal and their key performance indicator,
event logs, process discovery with Petri nets, domain rules and model repair,
conformance checking, business process simulation and the simulation
framework ProSiT. Related work is discussed together with each concept. The
chapter ends with the research gap.

Chapter~\ref{chp:case_study_and_data} introduces HHLA Container Terminal
Burchardkai and its data. It explains how records from several terminal
systems are turned into one truck-visit event log, gives the key facts of
this log and states the domain rules that every truck visit must satisfy.

Chapter~\ref{chp:process_discovery_methodology} describes the final simulation
model: how it is built from the event log, what it has learned, which parts are
added to respect the physical limits, and how it produces one simulated visit.

Chapter~\ref{chp:experiments} describes the five experiments: control-flow
discovery (E1), rule-based improvement (E2), simulation of the shortlist (E3),
mechanism audit (E4) and what-if demonstration (E5), together with the outcome
measures and the domain review.

Chapter~\ref{chp:results} presents the results of the experiments in the
same order and answers the three research questions.

Chapter~\ref{chp:discussion} judges the results: what the case shows about
data-aware simulation of a physical logistics process, what depends on
assumptions, and the threats to validity.

Chapter~\ref{chp:conclusion} summarises the answers and the contributions and
outlines directions for future work, particularly richer spatial and
resource-state representations, object-centric event logs, and validation in
other capacity-limited processes. The appendices give the observed behaviour,
the conformance of all discovered nets, the control-flow replica, the learned
ProSiT models and the steps to reproduce the results.
