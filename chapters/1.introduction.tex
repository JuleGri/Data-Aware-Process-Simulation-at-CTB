\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chp:introduction}

Truck handling at a container terminal involves a trade-off between service to
road transport and the use of resources shared with other terminal operations.
At HHLA Container Terminal Burchardkai (CTB), this thesis examines whether a
simulation learned from operational event data can support the assessment of a
different allocation of truck deliveries to yard blocks. The intended benefit
is a reduction of truck delays. Establishing whether the model is reliable
enough for this purpose is part of the investigation.

\section{Motivation}

Simulation allows terminal operators to investigate changes before implementing
them in live operations. Conventional terminal simulation models are often
constructed and calibrated manually, drawing on operational data and domain
knowledge \cite{CTSteenken2004,OptimizingCTs2023}. Process mining offers a way to
derive parts of such models from event logs, including the control flow,
activity durations, arrival patterns and resource assignments
\cite{PMRozinat2009}. Data-aware approaches additionally learn how decisions
and durations depend on case attributes and the state of the process
\cite{mannhardt2023stochasticProcessesModelling,Vinci2023Inf.Activ.Prob}.

This thesis uses ProSiT, which combines a Petri net with learned decision models
for the stochastic behaviour of the simulation. Its probabilistic white-box
models make learned relationships available for inspection and modification
\cite{Vinci2026prosit}. This is useful in a case study: a model can be checked
against the recorded process and the assumptions underlying a proposed
operational change can be made explicit.

For CTB, the relevant question is whether these learned relationships represent
truck handling well enough to assess a change in block allocation. Trucks
share yard facilities, and their turnaround times depend on handling times,
delays and concurrent operations. A simulation may reproduce common activity
sequences while representing these dependencies inadequately. The model must
therefore be evaluated for the particular decision it is intended to support.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:prob_statement}

The primary performance measure in this study is truck turnaround time (TTT),
defined as the time between Gate In and Gate Out. In the test period, the
observed median is 33 minutes, the mean 41 minutes and the 90th percentile
75 minutes. The data and performance measures are described in
Chapters~\ref{chp:case_study_and_data} and~\ref{chp:experiments}.

The terminal aims at a TTT between 25 and 35 minutes for every truck. The TTT of
each truck is the figure shown on the management dashboard of the terminal,
without a breakdown by the number of containers

Both bounds matter. A truck that stays longer than 35 minutes has waited too long
from the customer's point of view. A turnaround below 25 minutes is not wanted
either, because the cranes of a block also serve the waterside and the rail, and
fast truck handling can take capacity from them. In the test period, 47\,\% of the
visits take longer than 35 minutes, and on average a visit spends 12.5 minutes
above the corridor (Section~\ref{sec:exp_kpis}). This thesis asks why these visits
take longer and whether a different allocation of deliveries to the yard blocks
can shorten them. The visits below the corridor are reported, but the waterside
work is not modelled, so the study does not optimise the balance between truck
handling and waterside operations.

The candidate intervention is a load-aware allocation of deliveries to yard
blocks. The operational account supplied for this study places the assignment
of the container's yard position at truck arrival, so that the driver knows
where to go. An applicable allocation rule must therefore use information
available at that point and respect the eligible storage locations. The
simulation experiment examines a specified allocation rule; its decision time,
information requirements and omitted restrictions must be checked before its
results can be interpreted as evidence for implementation.

This leads to two connected problems. First, the simulation must reproduce the
relevant truck behaviour, TTT and response to load. Second, an apparent
improvement in a simulated scenario must be interpreted in light of the
model's validation results and the operational feasibility of the intervention.
Finding a favourable difference between simulated runs alone does not resolve
the second problem.

\section{Research Objective and Contributions}

The objective is to develop and evaluate an event-log-derived simulation of
truck handling at CTB and determine what it can support about the effect of a
load-aware block allocation on truck turnaround time. CTB is the case under
study, and the allocation question provides the intended use against which the
model is assessed. Limitations of the simulation are results of this assessment
and determine the scope of the conclusions for the terminal.

The thesis makes two connected contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(C1)] \textbf{A justified simulation model for the CTB case.} The study
compares alternative discovered process models, evaluates their coverage and
conformance, and checks the rules relevant to truck visits. It documents the
selected model, the learned simulation parameters and the assumptions used to
represent the terminal. Concrete decision trees and parameter settings make
the model available for inspection
(Chapters~\ref{chp:process_discovery_methodology}--\ref{chp:results}).
\item[(C2)] \textbf{An empirical assessment of validity and decision-support
potential.} The study compares observed and simulated performance, investigates
discrepancies through controlled component comparisons, and evaluates a block
allocation scenario within the resulting validity limits. It distinguishes
findings about the simulated system from conclusions justified for CTB
(Chapters~\ref{chp:results} and~\ref{chp:discussion}).
\end{enumerate}

The empirical case study builds on existing discovery and simulation methods.
Documented inputs, configurations and evaluation procedures support the two
contributions. The account of the workflow distinguishes standard ProSiT
configuration from custom diagnostic or simulation extensions; results that
depend on an extension must be identified as such. Findings from this terminal
can motivate further investigation in other settings, but do not establish
general limitations of all data-aware simulators.

\section{Research Questions}

The objective is addressed through three research questions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{RQ1:} Which discovery and rule treatment yields a sound,
executable control-flow model that preserves the observed truck-visit
behaviour, and do conformance metrics anticipate its behaviour in simulation?
\item \textbf{RQ2:} To what extent does the ProSiT simulation reproduce truck
turnaround time and its response to load at the capacity-limited yard blocks,
and which model components explain the observed discrepancies?
\item \textbf{RQ3:} To what extent can the simulation be used to analyse a
load-aware block allocation for deliveries under the physical limits of the
yard blocks, and what does it indicate about the effect on truck turnaround
time?
\end{enumerate}

Experiments E1--E3 address model selection and simulated visit behaviour (RQ1).
E3 and E4 assess performance and investigate discrepancies (RQ2). E5 examines
the allocation scenario (RQ3). Its interpretation depends on the evidence from
the preceding validation: where that evidence is insufficient, the conclusion
identifies the remaining requirements for decision support.

\section{Thesis Outline}
\label{sec:thesis_outline}

Chapter~\ref{chp:background} introduces the relevant process-mining and
simulation concepts and positions the study in the literature.
Chapter~\ref{chp:case_study_and_data} describes CTB, the operational records and
their transformation into an event log. Chapter~\ref{chp:process_discovery_methodology}
documents the simulation model and its parameters, while
Chapter~\ref{chp:experiments} explains the selection, validation and scenario
experiments.

Chapter~\ref{chp:results} reports the evidence and answers the research
questions. Chapter~\ref{chp:discussion} assesses the implications for CTB, the
role of domain knowledge and the limits of the conclusions.
Chapter~\ref{chp:conclusion} summarises the findings and the work needed to
strengthen their operational use. The appendices provide additional results,
model inventories and reproduction details.
