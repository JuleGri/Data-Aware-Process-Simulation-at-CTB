\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chp:introduction}

Container terminals connect maritime and hinterland transport networks. Increasing
container volumes and larger vessels require terminal operators to make many
tactical and operational decisions under uncertainty. These decisions concern
truck dispatching, yard-block allocation, resource planning and use, congestion, equipment
failures, and temporary infrastructure outages. Experiments in an operating
terminal are often costly, disruptive, or not feasible. Simulation offers a way
to assess decisions without interrupting a terminals continuous operations.

\section{Motivation}
Simulation is used for decision support in container-terminal planning and
operations research. From an operations management perspective, a terminal can
compare how alternative process executions and operating conditions affect
specific handling patterns before changing live operations. For example, when
high yard utilisation is forecasted, a terminal may compare lower truck-slot
release rates, alternative resource allocations, or policies that favour
receive or delivery operations, and evaluate their effects on activity-specific
service, pre-service delay, and total truck turnaround. Simulation can likewise
assess infrastructure investments, scheduling approaches, truck appointment
systems, and terminal redesigns before implementation. Traditional approaches mostly use manually
developed Discrete Event Simulation (DES) models, agent-based models, or
digital twins. Building and maintaining these models requires domain knowledge,
manual work, and calibration \cite{YUN1999DESSimulation,CTSteenken2004,OptimizingCTs2023,Bett2024}.

Process Mining research has studied how simulation models can be discovered and built
from event logs. Earlier work showed that control-flow behaviour, activity
durations, arrival patterns, resource allocations, and routing probabilities
can be discovered from process data and used in executable simulation models
\cite{PMRozinat2009}. \textit{Data-aware process simulation} extends this idea.
It uses context attributes, case information, process states, resource
characteristics, and decision logic
\cite{DaSLeno2019,mannhardt2023stochasticProcessesModelling,Vinci2023Inf.Activ.Prob,lopez2024}.

Machine-learning and deep-learning methods can learn complicated behavior,
but their results are often difficult to inspect, modify, and validate. Hybrid
and white-box approaches combine interpretable models with data-driven
behaviour discovery \cite{runtimeIntegration2024,Vinci2026prosit}. ProSiT uses
probabilistic white-box predictors to build simulation models that can be
inspected and configured for what-if analysis \cite{Vinci2026prosit}.

Evidence on automatically discovered simulation models in logistics systems is
still limited. Within the literature reviewed for this thesis, this
intersection remains under-evaluated, with most studies still centred on
business processes in healthcare, financial services, and administration
\cite{Hospital2026Vinci,Vinci2023Inf.Activ.Prob}. Container terminal truck
operations involve physical resource constraints, congestion,
infrastructure dependencies, spatial interactions, and changing process
contexts. It is not yet clear how well a discovered data-aware simulation model
can reproduce these conditions and support decisions. This thesis studies its
fidelity, structural applicability, and limitations using event logs from real
container terminal truck operations.

This problem is also visible in the wider research landscape. A recent review
of 105 studies at the intersection of Process Mining and simulation finds that
validation remains heterogeneous, that queue and resource modelling often
requires information outside conventional event logs, and that fully automated
model generation remains uncommon \cite{Sulis2026SimulationReview}. Work in
production and logistics similarly stresses that processing timestamps alone
do not reliably expose transport, waiting, inventory, and physical resource
interactions. Simulation-ready reconstruction may require harmonised data from
several operational levels and an object-oriented view
\cite{Grobis2025ProductionLogistics,Grobis2026SimulationDevelopment}. The CTB
case therefore provides a demanding empirical test of both the promise and the
support boundary of automatic simulation discovery.
Adjacent ASMG research has already shown that physical-system structure and
parameters can be reconstructed from manufacturing data, while emphasising
purpose-dependent abstraction and multi-object material flows
\cite{Reinhardt2019ASMG,Cimino2025ASMGReview,LugaresiMatta2021,LugaresiMatta2023}.
Within the literature reviewed for this thesis, the unresolved intersection
remains a temporally validated, data-aware executable model discovered from
real terminal event data and subjected to explicit physical and what-if
validity checks.

\section{Problem Statement}
Container terminal operators make decisions about capacity, truck handling,
yard use, policies, and infrastructure changes. Experiments in a real terminal
are often not feasible or involve operational risk, so these decisions are
often studied with simulation. Most container-terminal simulation models are
built manually. Their construction and maintenance require domain knowledge
and modelling work. They may include subjective assumptions about process
behaviour, resource interactions, and routing logic
\cite{CTSteenken2004,OptimizingCTs2023}.

Data-aware process simulation discovers simulation models from historical
event logs. This may reduce modelling work and keep the model closer to the
recorded process \cite{Camargo2020,lopez2024,Vinci2023Inf.Activ.Prob}. Before
such a model can support decisions, it must reproduce observed operations,
remain configurable, produce stable results, and represent the mechanisms
through which a proposed intervention can affect the relevant KPI. A model
intended to recommend a truck-slot rate under forecast congestion must, for
example, propagate changed arrivals through resource contention and must keep
receive and delivery effects distinguishable. Earlier studies have shown
that data-aware simulation can be applied to business processes
\cite{Vinci2023Inf.Activ.Prob,Hospital2026Vinci}. Its use in logistics systems
with congestion, competing resources, and context-dependent behaviour is less
clear. This thesis evaluates the model's fidelity, use, and limitations for
container-terminal truck operations.

\section{Research Objective and Contributions}

The objective of this thesis is to evaluate the fidelity, structural
applicability, and limitations of data-aware process simulation for container terminal truck operations. The study investigates how far a simulation model derived from historical event data using ProSiT can reproduce unseen truck-handling behaviour at the Container Terminal Burchardkai (CTB), and where the transfer of process-simulation discovery to a physical logistics environment reaches its limits. Trustworthiness motivates this evaluation, but it is not treated as a property that can be established from a single historical case study.

More specifically, the thesis evaluates whether the control-flow
representation derived from the training data remains applicable to a later
held-out period while respecting the physical sequence of a truck visit. It
then assesses the historical fidelity of the resulting simulator, examines
whether increasing the contextual expressiveness of the ProSiT configuration
improves this fidelity, and investigates how the data-aware model responds to
transparent operational interventions. Together, these analyses are intended
to distinguish between reproducing historical behaviour, constructing an
executable simulation model, and supporting credible what-if reasoning.
The chronological development period is divided once more into discovery and
validation partitions. The four ProSiT configurations are discovered on the
first partition and compared on the next. The configuration selected for
historical fidelity is then rediscovered on the combined development data and
evaluated on the final 20\,\% temporal test period. A separate
purpose-specific audit asks whether the model used for demand scenarios
contains an endogenous state through which changed arrivals can affect delay.
This distinction prevents historical replay quality from being treated as
sufficient evidence of intervention validity. The test results remain
retrospective evidence from one terminal and should not be confused with
prospective field validation of an intervention.

The thesis contributes an empirical evaluation of data-aware process
simulation in container terminal truck operations. First, it examines the
transfer of a recent process-simulation discovery approach to a logistics
setting characterised by physical sequencing, shared resources, temporal
variation, and congestion. Second, it identifies methodological challenges
that arise when automatically inferred process and resource abstractions do
not fully correspond to known physical constraints, requiring explicit
validation and, where justified, documented domain adaptations. Third, it
compares different levels of
contextual model expressiveness on unseen temporal data rather than assuming
that additional data awareness necessarily improves simulation quality.
Finally, two operational interventions and a six-level demand-saturation
stress test are used to investigate both the capabilities and the boundaries
of the resulting white-box model for what-if analysis and decision support.
The experiment separates a parsimonious historical-fidelity reference from a
minimal state-aware scenario model instead of claiming one globally best
configuration for both objectives.
The intended decision-support logic is made
explicit, when forecast terminal conditions define a scenario, candidate slot or
resource policies are simulated, and their consequences are compared at both
activity and case level. The case study evaluates how far the discovered model
can support that logic without treating mere scenario executability as evidence
of physical counterfactual validity.

The central methodological contribution is a layered validity audit. Rather
than reducing simulation quality to one distance between a simulated log and a
future log, the audit separates event-order semantics, control-flow language,
state-conditioned stochastic behaviour, temporal transfer, physical
resource--space semantics, and scenario response. A model can pass an earlier
layer and fail a later one. Then, a near-perfect replay fitness, for example, does not
show that travel, queues, or an intervention mechanism are represented. This
separation turns residual errors and null scenario responses into diagnostic
results and defines the strongest claim that the current model can support.
This operationalises, for one physical-logistics application, the recent call
for validation procedures that jointly consider process-model quality,
historical outcomes, and stakeholder-relevant semantics rather than relying on
an isolated fit measure \cite{Sulis2026SimulationReview}.

The empirical study is based on operational CTB truck-event data and combines
process analysis, simulation-model generation, temporal hold-out validation,
multi-seed simulation, and controlled scenario experiments. The corresponding
research questions specify how these objectives are evaluated in the
remainder of the thesis.

\section{Research Questions} To achieve the research objective, the thesis addresses the following research questions:

\begin{enumerate} 
\item \textbf{RQ1:} To what extent can a ProSiT simulation model discovered from historical CTB truck-operation data reproduce the observed behaviour of an unseen temporal hold-out?
\item \textbf{RQ2:} To what extent can the discovered and transparently domain-repaired process structure represent held-out CTB truck-handling behaviour while respecting the physical sequential constraints of a truck visit?
\item \textbf{RQ3:} How does increasing the contextual expressiveness of the simulation model affect held-out simulation fidelity in the CTB truck process?
\item \textbf{RQ4:} How does the discovered simulation model respond to transparent operational interventions, and what do these responses reveal about its suitability and limitations for what-if decision support?
\end{enumerate} 

\section{Thesis Outline}
\label{sec:thesis_outline}

Chapter~\ref{chp:background} establishes the theoretical and methodological
background of the thesis. It first introduces container-terminal operations
and truck handling as the application domain before explaining how operational
information systems can be transformed into event logs for Process Mining.
The chapter then reviews Process Mining, Business Process Simulation,
simulation discovery, and the development towards data-aware and white-box
simulation approaches. Particular attention is given to ProSiT and to the
validation of automatically derived simulation models. The chapter concludes
by defining the research gap concerning the transfer of these approaches to
physical logistics systems with spatial and resource constraints.

Chapter~\ref{chp:case_study_and_data} introduces the HHLA Container Terminal
Burchardkai as the empirical case study and describes the operational data
used in the analysis. It explains how records from several terminal systems
are cleaned, matched, and transformed into a process-oriented truck event log.
The chapter defines the case and activity structure, the lifecycle timestamps
used for service-time modelling, and the contextual attributes required for
data-aware simulation, including demand, utilisation, container, and
target-area information. It concludes with the structure and descriptive
characteristics of the final simulation-ready event log.

Chapter~\ref{chp:experiments_and_framework} defines the experimental and
evaluation framework used to answer the research questions. It introduces the
temporal train/validation/test split and describes the control-flow discovery procedure,
including the preservation of the ITS schedule-derived stop order stored in
XES, the complementary
directly-follows and Petri-net views, and the structural domain check applied
to the raw Inductive-Miner model. It then defines the expert-supported
sequential source-model repair used consistently for parameter discovery.
The chapter then defines four ProSiT parameter-discovery configurations in a
$2\times2$ feature-source design and documents the executable parameter
bundles used in the experiments. In addition, it specifies the
Monte-Carlo replication protocol, the distributional fidelity measures, the
paired ablation and temporal-transfer audits, the two what-if interventions, and a
descriptive transition-time scale check. It also defines the separate
adequacy criteria for a scenario model that must propagate changed demand
through endogenous state.
The final section documents the technical adaptations required to apply
ProSiT to the CTB event log reproducibly.

Chapter~\ref{chap:results} presents the empirical findings following the logic
of the four research questions. It first examines the temporal
train/validation/test design and the stability of the observed control-flow
language. It then compares the four ProSiT configurations on validation data,
rediscovers the historical reference on the complete development period, and
evaluates its historical fidelity once on the final test period. The comparison
separates the effects of static workload proxies and ProSiT's native workload
state. A matched stress test then compares the workload-blind historical
reference with the minimal native-state model used for the demand scenario.
Further diagnostic analyses examine
the predictive signal contained in the available context attributes, the
strong temporal structure of truck arrivals, the discovered resource
capacities, and the interpretation of model-derived pre-service delays. The
chapter finally reports the two what-if experiments and discusses the
methodological limitations that constrain their interpretation.

Chapter~\ref{chp:conclusion} brings together the empirical findings and answers
the four research questions. It evaluates what the case study demonstrates
about the transfer of data-aware process simulation to container-terminal truck
operations and distinguishes historical fidelity and model executability from
the stronger requirements of physical what-if decision support. The chapter
concludes by outlining directions for future work, particularly concerning
richer spatial and resource-state representations, alternative event-log
designs, and further validation in other operational settings.
