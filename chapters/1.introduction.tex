\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chp:introduction}

Process mining can learn executable simulation models from event logs. Data-aware
simulation goes a step further and learns how decisions and durations depend on
the data of a case and on the state of the process. Such models have been built
and evaluated mainly for processes in which a resource handles one case at a
time \cite{Vinci2026prosit}. Logistics and transportation are rare among their
applications, and the modelling of queues and shared resources is the task that
process mining supports least \cite{Sulis2026SimulationReview}. This thesis examines how far the approach carries over to a
physical logistics process, using truck handling at HHLA Container Terminal
Burchardkai (CTB) as a case study. It asks whether a simulation learned with ProSiT
from the operational event data of the terminal can support the assessment of a
different allocation of truck deliveries to the yard blocks.

\section{Motivation}

Simulation allows terminal operators to investigate changes before implementing
them in live operations. Conventional terminal simulation models are constructed
and calibrated manually, based on operational data and domain knowledge
\cite{CTSteenken2004,OptimizingCTs2023}. Process mining offers a way to derive such
models from event logs, including the control flow, activity durations, arrival
patterns and resource assignments \cite{PMRozinat2009}. Data-aware approaches
additionally learn how decisions and durations depend on case attributes and the
state of the process \cite{mannhardt2023stochasticProcessesModelling,Vinci2023Inf.Activ.Prob}.
ProSiT, the framework used in this thesis, combines a Petri net with learned
probabilistic decision trees, which can be read and changed \cite{Vinci2026prosit}.

A container terminal is a demanding test for such models, because its process has
physical limits. A truck is one vehicle, so its stops happen one after another and
never at the same time, and every visit includes container handling. A storage block has
three handover lanes, so it can hold at most three trucks at once, its crane
handles them one after another, and its cranes are shared with the waterside and
the rail. These limits are not written in the
event log, and a model learned only from the recorded sequences and durations does
not know them. A discovered control-flow model may allow a truck to perform two
stops at once. A learned simulator may send more trucks to a block than it can
serve, or let the waiting fall when a block gets busier. A simulation that reproduces the common sequences can therefore still fail where the physical limits shape the process. These places matter most when a simulation is used to evaluate an operational change, for example a different allocation of trucks to the storage blocks. A simulation model therefore has to be validated for the decision it is meant to support.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:prob_statement}

The primary performance measure in this study is the truck turnaround time (TTT),
defined as the time from the moment a truck enters the terminal until it leaves. Over the whole event log of 91,153 visits, the observed median is 30
minutes, the mean 38 minutes and the 90th percentile 69 minutes. The data and
performance measures are described in Chapters~\ref{chp:case_study_and_data}
and~\ref{chp:experiments}.

The terminal reports the TTT per hour. The mean TTT of the trucks that leave
through the gate within an hour should lie between 25 and 35 minutes, a corridor
that balances the efficiency of the terminal and the satisfaction of its customers.
If an hour with a mean below the corridor can be foreseen, the terminal can shift
capacity from the trucks to the waterside, so that trucks are served more slowly while
the waterside, the operational focus of a terminal, gains capacity. This trade-off
exists in every handling area, because the trucks share the cranes and the equipment
with the waterside. Over the whole event log, the mean TTT exceeds 35 minutes in 44\,\% of
the hours with at least 20 trucks, 38\,\% in the training period and 56\,\% in the
test period (Section~\ref{sec:exp_kpis}). This thesis asks why the trucks take
longer in these hours and whether a different allocation of deliveries to the yard
blocks can shorten them. The improvement measure is evaluated against the upper bound of the corridor, because
most hours that leave the corridor lie above it, 56\,\% against 8\,\% below it in
the test period. The share of hours below 25 minutes is reported. What the terminal
would gain from such hours lies outside the model, because the model does not contain
the waterside or the allocation of cranes and equipment between the waterside and the
trucks.

The improvement measure studied is a load-aware allocation of deliveries to the
yard blocks. When a truck with a delivery arrives and three or more trucks are
already at its planned block or heading to it, the delivery is moved to the block
with the fewest such trucks among those that may take the container. The yard position of a container is assigned when the truck arrives, so that
the driver knows where to go. An applicable rule must therefore use information
available at that point and respect the blocks that may take the container. Today,
the block assignment of the terminal does not consider how many trucks are already heading to a block,
so the suggested measure adds this information to the decision.

This leads to two connected problems. First, the simulation must reproduce the
relevant truck behaviour, the TTT and its response to load, within the physical
limits of the terminal. Second, an improvement found in a simulated scenario must
be interpreted in light of the validation of the model and of the operational
feasibility of the measure.
\section{Research Objective}
The objective is to develop and evaluate a simulation of truck handling at CTB that
is learned from event data, and to determine what it can support about the effect
of a load-aware block allocation on the turnaround time. The Container Terminal Burchardkai is the case under
study, and the allocation question is the intended use against which the model is
assessed. The limitations of the simulation are results of this assessment and
determine the scope of the conclusions for the terminal.

\section{Research Questions}

The objective is addressed through three research questions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{RQ1} Which discovery and rule treatment yields a sound,
executable control-flow model that preserves the observed truck-visit
behaviour, and do the conformance metrics anticipate its behaviour in simulation?
\item \textbf{RQ2} To what extent does the ProSiT simulation reproduce truck
turnaround time and its response to load at the capacity-limited yard blocks,
and which model components explain the observed discrepancies?
\item \textbf{RQ3} To what extent can the simulation be used to evaluate a
load-aware block allocation for deliveries under the physical limits of the
yard blocks, and what does it indicate about the effect on truck turnaround
time?
\end{enumerate}

A first group of experiments selects the control-flow model and examines its
behaviour in the simulator (RQ1). A second group assesses the performance of the
simulation and investigates its discrepancies (RQ2). A third evaluates the
improvement measure (RQ3), and its interpretation depends on the evidence of the
second.

\section{Contributions}

The main contributions of this thesis are as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A domain-constrained simulation model of CTB truck handling.} The
model adds domain rules, a data-aware stop guard and the capacity of the yard blocks, knowledge
that the event log does not contain, to a discovered control flow and data-aware
parameters learned from the log. The simulation thereby respects the physical limits of the terminal.
\item \textbf{An analysis of simulation errors and congestion sensitivity.} The
simulation is validated per component and by its response to load, not only by its
similarity to the log. This shows how the implementation, the resource semantics and
the event data shape congestion at resources that serve several cases at once.
\item \textbf{A conditional evaluation of a load-aware block allocation.} The
allocation rule is evaluated in six scenarios of flexibility and demand, with criteria
for interpreting the results fixed in advance. This gives conditional evidence of how such a rule
changes the frequency and severity of hours above the corridor and which trucks bear
the delays.
\end{enumerate}


The selected model, its learned decision trees and parameters and the extensions are
documented in full, so that the model can be inspected
(Chapter~\ref{chp:process_discovery_methodology} and
Appendices~\ref{app:prosit_inventory} and~\ref{app:model_inspection}).




The case study builds on existing discovery and simulation methods. It does not
propose a new algorithm. Findings from this terminal can motivate further
investigation in other processes with physical limits, but do not establish general
limitations of all data-aware simulators.


\section{Thesis Outline}
\label{sec:thesis_outline}

Chapter~\ref{chp:background} introduces the setting of the case study and the
process-mining and simulation concepts, and positions the study in the literature.
Chapter~\ref{chp:case_study_and_data} describes the data sources, the preprocessing and cleaning and
the event log construction. It describes the performance measure and explains the given domain rules. 
Chapter~\ref{chp:experiments} explains the design of the five experiments that answer
the research questions. It introduces the split into a training and a test log, and the number of simulation runs, and then describes for each
experiment its goal, its conditions and the criteria by which its results are evaluated.
Chapter~\ref{chp:process_discovery_methodology} describes the simulation model and its parameters and their construction, the
settings with which it is fitted, and the extensions that add the physical limits of the yard blocks.
Chapter~\ref{chp:results} reports the results and answers the research questions.
Chapter~\ref{chp:discussion} assesses what the results mean for the intended use and
for CTB. It discusses what conformance measures reveal about a model for simulation,
which parts of data-aware simulation transfer to a physical logistics process, the
threats to validity, how domain knowledge entered the study, and what the terminal can
take from it. Chapter~\ref{chp:conclusion} summarises the answers to the research
questions and the contributions, and names the limitations and directions for future
work.

The appendices provide the material behind the main text. They describe the observed
truck-visit behaviour in more detail (Appendix~\ref{app:observed_behaviour}), give all
discovered nets with their conformance values, the rule audit and a comparison of
control-flow representations (Appendix~\ref{app:discovery_results}), report
additional simulation results
(Appendix~\ref{app:sim_results}), document the complete ProSiT model
(Appendix~\ref{app:prosit_inventory}) and describe the implementation and the source of every result
(Appendix~\ref{app:model_inspection}).
