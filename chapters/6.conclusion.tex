\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{chp:conclusion}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

This thesis investigated how far an automatically discovered, data-aware
process simulation can be transferred to a physically constrained logistics
environment. Two months of operational truck-handling data from the HHLA
Container Terminal Burchardkai were transformed into a simulation-ready event
log and used to discover an executable ProSiT model. The evaluation did not
set successful execution equal with validity. It examined the discovered control
flow, historical and temporal fidelity, the effect of different state
representations, physical constraints and the response to  operational
interventions as alternative scenarios as separated claims.

The strongest result concerns the control flow. Preserving the ITS
schedule-derived stop order stored in XES allows Inductive Miner to discover a
compact Petri net without parallel behavior within a truck visit. Only 11 of
89,460 visits change sequence under completion-time sorting and yet these rare
inversions are sufficient to produce an incorrect parallel cut when timestamps
are treated as the ordering relation. The raw Inductive-Miner result therefore
requires an explicit domain repair. On the 64\,\% discovery partition it
contains three parallel operators, and the 80\,\% rediscovery retains a
gate-only bypass (fitness 0.999994, precision 0.7558). Both properties
conflict with the terminal expert validated case contract. The common executable
control-flow model is the expert-repaired sequential Petri net, which enforces sequential yard
handling and removes the bypass. On the temporal hold-out it achieves a fitness of 
1.0 and test precision of 0.7641.  This is the single model used by all four
ProSiT configurations and for every further reported simulation result.

The discovered unusual \texttt{LL\_mixed} branch has a narrow empirical basis. In 57 of
64 observed visits containing \texttt{LL\_mixed}, it is the only yard activity.
Of the seven combined visits, six training cases place it first before
\texttt{RMG\_receive}, while one held-out case places it after
\texttt{RMG\_delivery}. An empty-container exchange performed first for routing
reasons is therefore a plausible interpretation, but not a validated universal
rule. Its rarity also means that \texttt{LL\_mixed} contributes only
0.0328\,\% of held-out turnaround burden and does therefore not constitute a
frequency-weighted bottleneck.

The answer to RQ1 is mixed. The selected cap-three \textit{visit-only}
historical-reference model
reproduces arrivals closely, with an inter-arrival EMD of 0.072 minutes. Its
yard-service EMD is 3.15 minutes without frequency weighting and 2.34 minutes
when weighted by the real activity frequencies. Complete truck turnaround is
less accurate. Mean turnaround is 33.81 minutes in simulation and 39.742
minutes in the hold-out, a deficit of 5.93 minutes or 14.92\,\%. Simulated P90
is 57.9 rather than 71 minutes. The narrow intervals across ten
Monte-Carlo seeds show that the discrepancy is stable for the fixed model and
test period rather than ordinary run-to-run simulation noise. They do not
identify its cause.

Two analyses delimit that error without claiming a causally decomposition. Real
mean turnaround increases by 2.45 minutes between the training and hold-out
periods, so the temporal evaluation combines model mismatch with operational
change. Route-specific fifth-percentile transition baselines range from 2.16
to 5.28 minutes. Their frequency-weighted boundary contribution is 7.62
minutes per visit, comparable in scale to the mean turnaround deficit. These values
support a non-zero movement and transfer component between recorded
activities, but they do not separate travel, traffic, dispatching, access
delay, and queueing. A separate transition component cannot be introduced
defensibly without first estimating movement and refitting the remaining
pre-service distribution. Otherwise, part of the observed gap would be
represented twice.

The same limitation is visible directly in the main RMG patterns. Relative to
the observed previous-completion-to-start gap, the \textit{visit-only}
historical-reference model underpredicts
mean composite pre-service delay by about 5.75 minutes for receive handling and
4.04 minutes for delivery handling. ProSiT's enabled timestamp is correctly
relative to the Petri-net marking, but no physical readiness timestamp exists
against which it could be validated.

The held-out bottleneck decomposition reinforces this interpretation. Of the
39.742-minute real mean turnaround, recorded handling contributes 14.755
minutes and composite pre-service delay contributes 24.987 minutes. The gap
before \texttt{Gate Out} is the largest single accumulated component. RMG
receive is the largest handling-related accumulation point and accounts for
27.99\,\% of the reconstructed held-out time, when service and pre-service delay are
combined. These are time-burdening findings, not proof of a physical queue or a
capacity shortage, because the event log does not observe the mechanisms
inside the gaps.

RQ3 shows that additional state does not automatically improve historical
simulation quality. The standardized $2\times2$ experiment separates static
demand/utilisation proxies from ProSiT's native workload and queue state.
No added-state configuration produces a resolved improvement on turnaround,
weighted yard-service, activity-rate, or NGD ($N=3$) error. The pre-specified rule
therefore selects \textit{visit-only} as the parsimonious historical reference.
This is a model-selection result for replay fidelity, it is not a claim that the
configuration is actually adequate for every intervention.

The intervention audit exposes the limitation of that historical selection.
Under the same demand ladder, \textit{visit-only} remains effectively invariant
even at the highest tested rate. The cap-three \textit{native-only} bundle is
the minimal tested configuration containing the endogenous state pathway
needed to test whether changed arrivals reach a fitted waiting-time rule. It is
therefore used as a demand-propagation mechanism probe, not because its
response proves that it is a better or rather validated simulator. Its historical
differences from
\textit{visit-only} are small and unresolved, but this does not establish
equivalence. The available state still cannot reconstruct truck position,
container location, equipment movement, spatial queues, or changing
waterside/landside allocation when those relations are absent from the event
log.

RQ4 is consequently intervention-specific. The T22 experiment tests executable
resource-pool removal and reassignment. Removing T22 produces no resolved
turnaround effect and only a small change in model-derived RMG pre-service, but
the model lacks persistent container location, relocation, and movement
semantics. The result therefore cannot be interpreted as evidence that closing
a physical block would have little effect. The demand experiment tests whether
increased arrivals can propagate through endogenous state. Increasing
working-time arrival intensity by 20\,\% raises elapsed arrival rate by
15.910\,\% but produces no resolved turnaround response. The selected change is
below the model's response region.

The extended demand ladder shows an apparent threshold-like response under
this fitted simulator and experimental design, rather than an general inability
to propagate demand. At a realised
ratio of 2.83, mean turnaround increases by 1.82 minutes and mean RMG
pre-service by 2.32 minutes. At ratio 3.504, the increases are 4.07 and
5.22 minutes.  Because the ladder keeps the case count fixed while
compressing inter-arrivals, it is a compressed-arrival-horizon
experiment that also changes calendar exposure and end-of-horizon effects.
The reported transition region reflects these combined factors and not just resource
capacity alone.
The T13 waiting-time tree contains one inspecable
\texttt{queue\_length} split at 6.5. Its high-queue leaf has a representative
value of 52.40 minutes compared with 7.17 minutes in the low-queue leaf.
This is a statistical state-conditioned rule, not a causal queueing law.
Neither scenario uses the complete white-box intervention space because
no learned threshold or subtree is manually edited.

The expert-validated ceiling of three RMGs per block illustrates the
difference between empirical parameter discovery and physical admissibility.
Overlap-derived capacities above three are retained as discovery evidence but
are capped for the effective execution bundles. The paired capped-versus-uncapped
diagnostic for the historical reference does not resolve a direct
mean-turnaround effect. The ceiling is therefore maintained, because the uncapped
state is physically impossible and not because it produces the headline findings.

The scientific contribution is the resulting layered audit of automatic
simulation-model generation in port logistics. The thesis provides a
reproducible CTB event-log engineering pipeline and a ProSiT workflow, a temporal and multi-seed
evaluation, an empirical comparison of alternative state sources, and a clear
separation between discovered parameters, expert-supplied constraints, and
intervention edits. The strongest defensible contribution is not a validated
truck-terminal simulator for reliable operational decision-making. It is a
methodology for diagnosing when a process-mining-based simulation reproduces
historical observations and when its state lacks the mechanism required by a
particular intervention.

The case shows that good historical fit is an important first check, but it is not enough to choose a model for what-if analysis. The workload-blind benchmark reproduces past process behavior, but it does not contain a mechanism through which higher demand can affect delays. Native state demonstrates such a pathway in the fitted
simulator, while the resulting extend and threshold remain unvalidated
against physical CTB congestion. T22 exposes a different boundary, as
resource-pool reallocation is executable but spatial relocation is absent. The
adaptive mechanism audit is therefore exploratory, and its stress response is
an outcome, rather than a criterion used to select a model. Control-flow
conformance, stochastic fidelity, temporal transfer, physical semantics, and
counterfactual validity do not have to be collapsed into one claim of simulation
accuracy. A model can have almost perfect held-out replay fitness, while
underestimating turnaround and a technically correct intervention can still
lack the mechanism required for a physical forecast.

The empirical scope is the retained one-RMG subprocess rather than all CTB
truck movements in order to include the specific target blocks attributes. The case-level representation excludes 1,693 multi-RMG visits
and 6,820 events because it cannot attach multiple changing block states to one
truck case. The study may therefore underrepresent movement, cross-block
interaction, and spatial complexity. Within that boundary, the model is a
transparent historical and diagnostic simulation. It is not yet a
decision-valid digital twin. Its present operational value is to make model
assumptions, scenario mechanics, and evidence gaps inspectable. A future model
with validated location, movement, equipment, and queue state could support the
decision loop, that motivates terminal simulation by comparing truckslot,
resource, and handling policies before another implementation.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future_work}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Future work should retain the reproducible discovery-and-validation protocol
while extending the mechanisms and evidence that currently limit physical and
counterfactual interpretation:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Investigate an object-centric process representation.}
    Future work could examine whether an object-centric event log is better
    suited to container-terminal operations than the current truck-visit case
    representation. In the present model, container characteristics are
    aggregated to case level, so information such as container type, loading
    status, reefer status, or hazardous-goods status is combined across all
    containers handled during one truck visit. An object-centric approach could
    keep each container as a separate object with its own attributes while
    linking it to the truck visit, handling activity, yard block, and resource.
    This would reduce information loss and preserve container-specific information throughout the process
    and could also support multi-container and multi-block visits without
    forcing one aggregated context value onto the complete case.

    \item \textbf{Add an explicit spatial and movement representation.}
    Future work could separate physical movement from the combined
    pre-service delay by using route, position, or equipment observations.
    Rather than representing every movement as an additional process activity,
    a richer simulation formalism could include location directly in the model
    state, for example through timed or coloured Petri-net tokens and
    location-dependent transition conditions. This would allow truck or
    container movement between yard areas to consume time and respect spatial
    constraints without treating travel as a recorded handling activity.
    Movement times would then have to be estimated from spatial observations
    and the remaining access and waiting-time models refitted, so that travel
    time is not counted twice.

   \item \textbf{Represent physical assignment and queueing explicitly.}
    Future models could bind trucks and containers to physically feasible
    yard locations and resources instead of treating resource labels as fully
    interchangeable. Equipment availability and assignment should be represented
    as dynamic state, and trucks waiting for handling should enter explicit
    simulated queues rather than having these effects absorbed into a fitted
    pre-service-delay distribution. An object-centric representation could
    preserve the relations between trucks, containers, yard blocks, and
    equipment, while a corresponding simulation model would still need explicit
    movement, resource-allocation, and queueing rules
    \cite{VanDerAalst2023OCPM,Knopp2023ObjectCentricSimulation}.

    
    
\end{itemize}
