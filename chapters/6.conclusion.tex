\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{chp:conclusion}

This thesis investigated the applicability, historical fidelity, and
limitations of automatically discovered data-aware process simulation for container-terminal truck operations. The work was motivated by the increasing availability of operational event data in container terminals and by recent advances in simulation discovery, which make it possible to derive executable simulation models directly from recorded process behavior. While these approaches have been studied primarily in conventional business-process settings, their applicability to physical logistics systems with spatial constraints, congestion effects, and resource dependencies has not yet been researches in depth.

\begin{sloppypar}
To investigate this problem, the thesis developed a simulation-ready event log from two months of continuous operational truck-handling data of the HHLA Container Terminal Burchardkai and applied the ProSiT simulation-discovery framework. The resulting models were evaluated on a temporally held-out test period resembling unseen future from the perspective of the simulation models development. The evaluation combined control-flow conformance, distributional quality measures, repeated Monte-Carlo simulation, and explicit structural checks. Different levels of contextual model expressiveness were compared, and the final data-aware model
was subsequently used in two transparent what-if experiments concerning an RMG resource-pool intervention and increased truck demand.
\end{sloppypar}

The implementation and empirical evaluation answer the four research questions
introduced in Chapter~1.

\textbf{RQ1} questions, to what extent a ProSiT simulation model discovered from historical CTB truck-operation data can reproduce the behaviour observed in an unseen temporal hold-out. The evaluation showed that the model reproduces some patterns of activities and process characteristics of the observed temporal behavior considerably better than others. The
final baseline achieves an unweighted yard-service Wasserstein distance of 2.99 minutes and a real-frequency-weighted distance of 2.34 minutes, while the inter-arrival distribution differs from the held-out log by only 0.072 minutes. Dispite these fairly acurate statistical results, the model systematically under-predicts complete truck turnaround time. Mean turnaround is 30.44 minutes in the simulation compared with 39.74 minutes in the held-out data, corresponding to an under-prediction
of 9.31 minutes.

\textbf{RQ2} investigated the suitability of automatically discovered
control flow for the CTB truck process. An Inductive-Miner model was
initially discovered in line with the ProSiT methodology, but its
generalised structure introduced concurrency and ordering behaviour that conflicted with known physical constraints of a single truck visit. Consequently, the discovered model could not be used unchanged as the simulation backbone. The final implementation therefore replaced it with a deliberately restrictive prefix-trie Petri net derived from the observed training variants. This representation covered 99.944\,\% of held-out traces, but its high coverage should not be interpreted as superior process discovery because the model intentionally sacrifices generalisation. The main finding of RQ2 is therefore that transferring process-discovery-based simulation to the CTB setting required explicit domain constraints on the control-flow model; automatic process discovery alone did not produce a sufficiently suitable simulation
structure.

\textbf{RQ3} asked how increasing the contextual expressiveness of the
simulation model affects held-out fidelity. The comparison between the
distribution-only \textit{no-rules} configuration and the full
\textit{rules$+$workload} configuration shows that additional data-aware
modelling does not automatically improve simulation fidelity. The more
expressive configuration produces a 56\,\% higher turnaround-distance measure
and greater variation across simulation seeds, while its improvement in
inter-arrival fidelity is operationally negligible. A subsequent
per-activity analysis shows that the available contextual attributes explain
little of the service-time variation of the dominant RMG activities. The
results therefore indicate that increasing model expressiveness is only useful
when the available contextual information captures variation that is
relevant to the simulated outcome. In the CTB event log examined here,
additional contextual conditioning introduces modelling complexity without
producing a corresponding improvement in overall held-out fidelity.

\textbf{RQ4} asked how the discovered simulation model responds to transparent
operational interventions and what these responses reveal about its suitability
for what-if decision support. Both interventions can be implemented,
inspected, and reproduced consistently. Removing T22 from the simulated RMG
resource pool eliminates all assignments to that resource, while increasing
the working-time arrival intensity by 20\,\% produces the intended change in
the underlying arrival parameter. Nevertheless, neither intervention produces
a statistically resolved change in mean truck turnaround time. More
importantly, the scenario analysis exposes limitations of the underlying
process abstraction. RMG resources represent statistical capacity assignments
rather than physical container locations, and the model does not represent
container relocation, equipment movement, waterside and landside crane
allocation, or explicit spatial queue states. Consequently, the scenarios are
valid experiments on the discovered simulation model, but their results cannot
be interpreted as experimentally established counterfactual effects of the
physical terminal.

Read jointly, the findings reveal a consistent boundary between patterns that
are present in the event log and mechanisms that are absent from the model.
The stable trace language and strong calendar structure are relationships that
the discovery pipeline can reproduce. By contrast, the weak duration signal
for dominant RMG activities, the systematic turnaround deficit, and the lack
of a congestion response under higher demand all point to missing event-level
and spatial state. This agreement across independent diagnostics strengthens
the interpretation, but it is not a causal proof: the analyses show which
explanation is consistent with the evidence and which stronger claims the
evidence cannot support.

In relation to recent data-aware Process Mining, the main advance demonstrated
here is therefore not an automatic increase in prediction accuracy. It is the
ability to represent, inspect, and intervene on conditional relationships while
retaining an event-log-based process model. The CTB case shows that this
transparency is useful precisely when additional context fails: it allows the
researcher to distinguish a weak or unstable association from an omitted
physical mechanism. Data awareness expands what can be modelled, but the
empirical content and information timing of the attributes determine whether
that additional expressiveness improves a held-out simulation.

Overall, the thesis shows that automatically discovered data-aware process
simulation can be transferred to container-terminal truck operations and can
produce a transparent, reproducible, and structurally consistent simulation
model from operational event data. At the same time, the case study
demonstrates that successful process discovery, historical fidelity, and
scenario executability are different requirements. High control-flow
conformance does not guarantee accurate temporal behaviour, additional
contextual features do not necessarily improve simulation fidelity, and an
executable what-if intervention does not establish that the corresponding
physical effect is represented correctly.

The contribution of this thesis is therefore not a demonstration that the
discovered model is already sufficient for unrestricted operational decision
support. Rather, it provides empirical evidence about where a recent
white-box simulation-discovery approach transfers successfully to a complex
logistics environment and where the abstraction reaches its limits. For the
CTB truck process, the results indicate that further progress towards
trustworthy operational decision support requires richer representations of
spatial process state, physical resource constraints, equipment movement, and
queueing mechanisms in addition to the event-log-based behavioural information
captured by the current simulation model.
