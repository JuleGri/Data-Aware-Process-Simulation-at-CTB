\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{chp:conclusion}

This thesis investigated the applicability, historical fidelity, and
limitations of automatically discovered data-aware process simulation for
container-terminal truck operations. The work was motivated by the increasing
availability of operational event data and by recent advances that derive
executable simulation models directly from recorded process behaviour. These
approaches have primarily been studied in conventional business-process
settings; their transfer to physical logistics systems with spatial
constraints, congestion, and resource dependencies remains less established.

\begin{sloppypar}
To investigate this problem, the thesis developed a simulation-ready event log
from two months of continuous truck-handling data from the HHLA Container
Terminal Burchardkai and applied the ProSiT simulation-discovery framework. The
resulting models were evaluated on a temporally held-out period that was unseen
during model development. The evaluation combined control-flow conformance,
distributional quality measures, repeated Monte-Carlo simulation, and explicit
structural checks. Different levels of contextual model expressiveness were
compared, and the maximally expressive \textit{rules$+$workload} reference model
was subsequently used in two transparent what-if experiments concerning an RMG
resource-pool intervention and increased truck demand. The workload-blind
\textit{rules} model remains the strongest configuration in historical
screening; the reference model was selected to examine ProSiT's full
state-aware scenario interface, not because it was the best forecaster.
\end{sloppypar}

The implementation and empirical evaluation answer the four research questions
introduced in Chapter~1.

\textbf{RQ1} asked to what extent a ProSiT simulation model discovered from
historical CTB truck-operation data can reproduce the behaviour observed in an
unseen temporal hold-out. The model reproduces some dimensions considerably
better than others. The data-aware reference model achieves an unweighted yard-service
Wasserstein distance of 3.025 minutes and a real-frequency-weighted distance
of 2.381 minutes, while the inter-arrival distribution differs by only 0.072
minutes. Despite these comparatively small distances, the model systematically
under-predicts complete truck turnaround. Mean turnaround is 31.63 minutes in
the simulation compared with 39.74 minutes in the hold-out, an under-prediction
of 8.12 minutes. The simulated 90th percentile is 53.70 minutes compared with
71.00 minutes in the hold-out.

The descriptive transition analysis supplies an important scale check for this
bias. Route-specific fifth-percentile baselines range from 2.16 to 5.28
minutes, while the route-frequency-weighted inbound and outbound components
sum to 7.62 minutes per visit. This is close to the 8.12-minute mean deficit and
supports omitted physical transition time as a plausible major contributor.
Because these lower-tail baselines do not separate movement, traffic, resource
waiting and other operational delay, they cannot be used as a case-level
correction or causal decomposition.

The temporal-transfer audit adds a second explanation that must not be merged
with movement. Real mean turnaround rises by 2.450 minutes from training to the
hold-out, and mean service time rises for all nine yard activities. For the
final cap-three baseline, the 8.115-minute held-out mean deficit decomposes into
a 5.666-minute discrepancy relative to the training-period mean and the
2.450-minute observed period shift. The simulator is therefore already too
fast relative to its calibration period, while temporal drift makes the later
comparison worse. The two mechanisms are empirically distinguishable at the
aggregate mean level but not identifiable case by case.

\textbf{RQ2} investigated the suitability of automatically discovered
control flow for the CTB truck process. When the explicit XES event order is
preserved, the Inductive Miner discovers a compact Petri net with no parallel
operator. Its loop permits varying sequential combinations and repetitions of
yard activities, which is consistent with the terminal-expert-validated domain
requirement: the internal order may vary, but one truck cannot perform two
activities simultaneously.
The model attains a held-out fitness of 0.999992, fits 99.994\,\% of held-out
traces completely, and retains a generalisation score of 0.9413. Its lower
precision of 0.7558 reflects a broader sequential language, not forbidden
concurrency. The principal methodological finding is that event-order semantics
must be protected during log conversion. The earlier apparent parallelism was
caused by timestamp-based reordering, not by the authoritative XES discovery.
After passing a hard structural eligibility check for parallel operators and
the simulation-output contract checks, the unchanged automatically discovered
net is suitable as the ProSiT backbone and does not require a manual
prefix-trie replacement. Its permissive silent bypass is disclosed as a
finding rather than removed through an additional physical constraint.
As a separate robustness experiment, a precision-oriented state split removes
only that bypass. Held-out fitness remains 0.999992 while precision rises from
0.7558 to 0.7939; generalisation falls from 0.9413 to 0.8859. This explicit
trade-off shows that structural repair can improve the unsupported language
without pretending that the repaired net remains a purely automatic discovery.

\textbf{RQ3} asked how increasing the contextual expressiveness of the
simulation model affects held-out fidelity. The three-level ablation separates
process- and case-state rules from workload information. Relative to
\textit{no-rules}, strict workload-blind \textit{rules} reduce activity-rate
L1 error by 0.0103 in paired ten-seed comparisons and suppress the rarely
sampled silent gate-only bypass. Their 0.115-minute turnaround-EMD improvement
is not statistically resolved. Adding workload information afterward yields
no resolved weighted service-time improvement, increases turnaround EMD by
0.375 minutes, lowers simulated mean turnaround by 0.576 minutes, lowers P90
by 1.000 minute, and increases activity-rate L1 error by 0.0598. The best state
abstraction is therefore process- and metric-specific: more data awareness is
not automatically more valid state. In CTB, the available workload proxies do
not transfer as a useful representation of the omitted physical queueing and
movement mechanisms.

\textbf{RQ4} asked how the discovered simulation model responds to transparent
operational interventions and what these responses reveal about its suitability
for what-if decision support. Both interventions can be implemented,
inspected, and reproduced consistently. Removing T22 from the simulated RMG
resource pool eliminates all assignments to that resource, while increasing
the working-time arrival intensity by 20\,\% produces the intended change in
the underlying arrival parameter. Nevertheless, neither intervention produces
a statistically resolved change in mean truck turnaround time. The experiments
do distinguish handling patterns: under higher demand, mean RMG service rises
by 0.091 minutes and mean receive service by 0.073 minutes, with both paired
intervals excluding zero. For the workload-blind rules model, by contrast,
RMG pre-service rises by +0.118 [0.038, 0.199] minutes and delivery
pre-service by +0.146 [0.016, 0.276] minutes, while mean turnaround and the
headline difference-in-differences remain unresolved. No T22-removal
mean-effect interval excludes zero. Repeating both scenarios with the
workload-blind rules bundle again produces no resolved mean-turnaround
response, and none of the headline difference-in-differences between state
configurations excludes zero. The small component effects are therefore
conditional on the current model and do not establish physical causality. A
separate capacity-pressure diagnostic further shows that the 20\,\%
intervention leaves the most-loaded held-out RMG block at only 0.506 nominal
mean utilisation under the three-task ceiling; the smallest multiplier to mean
saturation is 2.37. The six-level demand ladder then shows a non-linear model
response. A configured twofold working-time intensity produces a realised
elapsed-rate ratio of 2.367; at that level, mean turnaround rises by 4.644
minutes, P90 by 10.700 minutes and mean RMG pre-service by 5.878 minutes. At a
realised rate ratio of 3.504, the corresponding changes reach 6.160, 14.800 and
7.767 minutes. Mean RMG service remains unresolved at the high-load levels.
The simulator therefore propagates abstract capacity pressure through
pre-service delay once realised demand reaches the nominal saturation region.
This is stronger evidence than the original 20\,\% test, but it remains
model-conditional. RMG resources represent statistical capacity assignments
rather than physical container locations, and the model does not represent
container relocation, equipment movement, waterside and landside crane
allocation, or explicit spatial queue states. Consequently, the scenarios and
stress ladder are valid experiments on the discovered simulation model, but
their results cannot be interpreted as experimentally established
counterfactual effects of the physical terminal.

The matched capacity sensitivity removes a remaining methodological
ambiguity. Repeating the final Inductive-Miner baseline and scenarios with the
freely discovered RMG capacities produces no resolved direct cap effect on
mean turnaround and no resolved cap-by-scenario mean-turnaround interaction.
The expert-validated ceiling is therefore retained because the uncapped
capacities violate a physical bound, not because it creates the headline
result. A resolved cap-by-demand interaction in mean RMG service remains a
secondary model-sensitivity finding without a validated physical
interpretation.

Read jointly, the findings reveal a consistent boundary between patterns that
are present in the event log and mechanisms that are absent from the model.
The stable sequential control-flow structure and strong calendar pattern are
patterns that the discovery pipeline can reproduce. By contrast, the weak
duration signal
for dominant RMG activities, the observed train--test drift, the systematic
turnaround deficit, and a capacity response whose physical propagation chain
is not represented all point to missing event-level and spatial state. This
agreement across independent diagnostics strengthens
the interpretation, but it is not a causal proof: the analyses show which
explanation is consistent with the evidence and which stronger claims the
evidence cannot support.

This boundary is not unique to the CTB implementation. Recent work on
Process-Mining-supported simulation in production and logistics reports that
event logs with operation start and end times can supply processing-time
parameters, whereas transport, waiting, inventory, machine state, and
interacting material objects commonly require additional operational data and
semantic integration \cite{Grobis2025ProductionLogistics,Grobis2026SimulationDevelopment}.
The CTB results turn that general requirement into measured evidence: the
transition-time scale check, component bottleneck ranking, capacity-pressure
audit, and state ablation show which missing mechanisms materially constrain
the present model's interpretation.

In relation to recent data-aware Process Mining, the main advance demonstrated
here is therefore not an automatic increase in prediction accuracy. It is the
ability to represent, inspect, and intervene on conditional relationships while
retaining an event-log-based process model. The CTB case shows that this
transparency is useful precisely when additional context fails: it allows the
researcher to distinguish a weak or unstable association from an omitted
physical mechanism. Data awareness expands what can be modelled, but the
empirical content and information timing of the attributes determine whether
that additional expressiveness improves a held-out simulation.

Overall, the thesis shows that automatically discovered data-aware process
simulation can be transferred to container-terminal truck operations and can
produce a transparent, reproducible, and structurally consistent simulation
model from operational event data. At the same time, the case study
demonstrates that successful process discovery, historical fidelity, and
scenario executability are different requirements. High control-flow
conformance does not guarantee accurate temporal behaviour, additional
contextual features do not necessarily improve simulation fidelity, and an
executable what-if intervention does not establish that the corresponding
physical effect is represented correctly.

The layered validity audit is the main academic outcome. Event semantics and
sequential control flow are supported; historical stochastic fidelity is
mixed; temporal transfer is unstable; physical resource--space semantics and
scenario propagation are not yet validated. This ranking avoids both extremes:
the model is more than an unusable failed simulator because it provides strong
structural and diagnostic evidence, but it is less than a decision-valid
digital twin because its counterfactual mechanisms are incomplete. The
ablation and response-surface results identify which validity layer remains
unsupported and where a model extension must be evaluated.

This contribution is timely because a recent systematic review identifies no
shared validation procedure for Process-Mining-based simulation, reports that
36 of 105 reviewed studies provide no validation, and calls for assessments
that connect process-model quality, stakeholder expectations, historical
outcomes, and established simulation-validation principles
\cite{Sulis2026SimulationReview}. The thesis does not claim to establish a
universal framework from one terminal. It contributes a reproducible,
falsification-oriented implementation of such an assessment and demonstrates
why reporting only replay fitness or one aggregate KPI would have produced an
academically weaker and operationally misleading conclusion.

The contribution of this thesis is therefore not a demonstration that the
discovered model is already sufficient for unrestricted operational decision
support. Rather, it provides empirical evidence about where a recent
white-box simulation-discovery approach transfers successfully to a complex
logistics environment and where the abstraction reaches its limits. For the
CTB truck process, the results indicate that further progress towards
trustworthy operational decision support requires richer representations of
spatial process state, physical resource constraints, equipment movement, and
queueing mechanisms in addition to the event-log-based behavioural information
captured by the current simulation model.

This limitation does not diminish the operational rationale for the approach.
A validated terminal simulation should allow planners to translate forecast
conditions into prospective decisions: compare alternative hourly truck-slot
limits or resource-priority policies, observe their consequences for receive
and delivery handling as well as total turnaround, and select a policy before
committing it to live operations. Automatically discovered data-aware models can
make this loop easier to update and audit than a wholly manual model. The CTB
case implements the transparent intervention-and-measurement loop; its
threshold-shaped stress response shows that abstract capacity pressure is
represented and identifies the additional physical state required before the
loop can support prescriptive decisions.
\section{Future Work}

Future research should retain the reproducible discovery-and-validation
framework while extending and testing the process representation in seven
directions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Complete the structural-repair experiment:} Simulate the
    exported gate-bypass repair across the same ten seeds and rerun the scenario
    tests. A discriminator-based precision repair could then be investigated as
    an explicit extension of Vinci and de Leoni's addition-oriented framework,
    with language restriction accepted only if held-out fitness and scenario
    behaviour remain satisfactory.
    \item \textbf{Separate physical transit from residual delay:} Add
    timestamped route or position observations and model
    $D_{\mathrm{pre\text{-}service}}=T_{\mathrm{transit}}+D_{\mathrm{residual}}$.
    Transit should be generated once through visible auxiliary transitions or a
    simulator extension, while the existing delay distribution is fitted to
    the residual to avoid double counting. Sparse internal routes require a
    pooled or hierarchical fallback rather than unsupported pair estimates.
    \item \textbf{Represent sticky spatial resources and explicit queues:}
    Bind a case's target area to feasible blocks, distinguish individual
    availability and performance, and represent truck and equipment movement.
    The discovered process model can then be coupled with a discrete-event or
    agent-based physical layer for constraints that resource labels alone do
    not encode.
    \item \textbf{Evaluate an object-centric event and simulation design:}
    Reconstruct truck visits, trucks, containers, yard blocks, equipment, and
    appointments as related objects and associate each handling event with all
    participating objects. This could preserve container identity across
    operations, retain multi-block visits, and support location-specific
    assignment \cite{VanDerAalst2023OCPM,Knopp2023ObjectCentricSimulation}.
    It would not create missing physical observations: positions, movements,
    queues, and changing relations must still be logged and incorporated into
    executable multi-object semantics. Recent object-centric discovery work
    also shows that cross-object causality and model interpretability remain
    active research problems \cite{FigueiredoJalali2025OCCN}.
    \item \textbf{Adapt to temporal change:} Evaluate rolling-window or
    recency-weighted discovery prospectively and repeat the three-level state
    ablation in each period. This would show whether workload state becomes
    useful after drift-aware refitting or remains an unsuitable abstraction.
    \item \textbf{Validate bottleneck mechanisms:} Use the time-burden ranking
    to select candidate locations, then distinguish service, access, queue, and
    movement through additional timestamps and controlled interventions. Expert
    walkthroughs should verify the causal plausibility of each proposed
    propagation chain.
    \item \textbf{Validate operational policies:} Represent appointment-slot
    release, forecast utilisation, and receive/delivery priority policies
    explicitly. Candidate hourly admission rates should be evaluated against
    activity-specific and terminal-wide KPIs around the response region found
    by the demand ladder. The experiment must be repeated after spatial queues,
    feasible block assignment and movement have been represented, and its
    results compared with controlled historical or prospective outcomes before
    deployment.

\end{itemize}
