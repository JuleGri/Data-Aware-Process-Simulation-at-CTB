Container terminals use simulation to assess operational changes before they
are made. Data-aware process simulation promises to learn such models from event
logs, but its fitness for physical logistics processes with shared,
capacity-limited resources is largely untested. This thesis examines whether a
simulation learned with ProSiT from the operational data of HHLA Container
Terminal Burchardkai can support the assessment of a load-aware allocation of
truck deliveries to yard blocks. The terminal aims at a turnaround time between 25
and 35 minutes for every truck; in the test period, 47\,\% of the visits take
longer.

From an event log of 91,153 truck visits, nineteen nets of four discovery
families were compared with gates fixed on the training log: soundness,
acceptance of every sequence above a coverage threshold of 0.1\,\%, and the
domain rules of the truck visit. The Heuristics Miner net HM 0.7, used as
discovered, is the only net that passes all gates, and the most precise of those
that pass the coverage gate (precision 0.998); a look-ahead stop guard makes every
simulated truck perform its planned number of stops. As fitted, the simulation overestimates the turnaround time
strongly (median 57 against 33 minutes), because its learned block choice
concentrates the trucks. A component-wise audit locates the causes in the block
choice, the queue and the service. With replayed trucks and blocks, three lanes
per block, a waiting time measured from a free lane and load-dependent service,
the model reproduces the median within 5 minutes and the rise of the waiting with
the load of a block, but not the slowdown in the busiest hours: its validity is
partial. A net with
parallel branches fits the level better only through physically impossible
visits.

Within these limits, moving deliveries from full blocks to a neighbouring block
shortens the moved visits by about 9.5 minutes and reduces the visits above the
corridor by 3.7 percentage points in the model, with only a small change at the
receiving blocks; more visits fall below the lower bound, which the model cannot
evaluate. The simulation supports the direction and the mechanism of
this change, not its size at the terminal. The case shows that conformance
metrics and historical fit are not enough to judge a simulation for a what-if
question; the mechanism the intervention acts on has to be validated in the
simulator itself.
