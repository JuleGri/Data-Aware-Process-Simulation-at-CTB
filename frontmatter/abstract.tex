Container terminals use simulation to assess operational changes before they are
made. Data-aware process simulation promises to learn such models from event logs,
but it has rarely been applied in logistics, where queues and shared resources
dominate. This thesis examines whether a simulation learned with ProSiT from the
operational data of HHLA Container Terminal Burchardkai can support the assessment
of a load-aware allocation of truck deliveries to yard blocks. The study uses
91,153 truck visits recorded in March and April 2026, split in time into a training
log of six weeks and a test log of the following 18 days. The terminal aims at
an hourly mean turnaround time between 25 and 35 minutes. In the test period, the
mean exceeds 35 minutes in more than half of the hours.

Nineteen nets of four discovery families were compared with criteria fixed on the
training log. Of the three nets that are sound and accept every frequent sequence,
two generate visits that no truck can perform. Coverage and the domain rules
anticipated these failures, precision did not. The selected Heuristics Miner net,
used as discovered, reproduces the stop structure once a guard on the planned number
of stops, a data attribute of the case, is added.

As fitted, the simulation overestimates the turnaround time strongly, because its
learned block choice concentrates the trucks. An analysis along the path of a truck
separates defects of the software version, a mismatch between its resource semantics
and a block with three lanes, missing data on the cranes and a slower test period.
With the first two corrected and the observed trucks and blocks replayed, the model
reproduces normal hours and the rise of the waiting with the load of a block. It
produces, however, less than half of the hours above the corridor, and the magnitude
of its congestion depends strongly on the depth of the learned trees.

In this model, moving deliveries at the gate away from blocks that three trucks are
already heading to lowers how often the hourly mean exceeds 35 minutes in every
scenario, through less waiting at full blocks. Under higher demand, the change in the
severity of these hours becomes uncertain, a wider choice of blocks moves waiting to
other trucks, and a rule restricted to neighbouring blocks is a promising candidate
rather than an established best rule. The terminal confirmed the rules and the
decision point and found the result plausible, but not the missing peak hours. The
result is a set of hypotheses for a pilot at the terminal, not a validated
improvement.
